# Anaemia in children

## Define and recognise

- **In children, anaemia is most often due to iron deficiency.** Anaemia = Hb under the lower limit of the age-appropriate reference range. [S1]
- **Lower limit of normal Hb (g/L):** 90 at 2 months · 95 at 2–6 months · 105 at 6–24 months · 115 at 2–11 years · over 12 years: 120 female, 130 male. [S1]
- **History:** ethnic background (guides testing for haemoglobinopathies and G6PD deficiency); medications, especially those that can cause haemolysis in G6PD deficiency; diet — iron-rich foods, breastfeeding, cow's milk, B12 and recent fava bean ingestion; family history of anaemia, jaundice, gallstones or splenomegaly. [S1]
- **Examine for** poor growth, pallor, tachycardia, murmur, **signs of cardiac failure**, and **signs of haemolysis** (jaundice, scleral icterus, splenomegaly, dark urine). [S1]

## Investigate

- **First-line: FBC and film, reticulocyte count, ferritin.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Iron studies or a serum iron should not be used to diagnose iron deficiency** — serum iron shows recent intake rather than stores. [S1]
- **Classify first by MCV** (microcytic, normocytic, macrocytic). [S1]

## Red flags (consider admission)

- ⚠️ **Consider admission for:** Hb under 60 g/L (including iron deficiency) · tachycardia, murmur or cardiac failure · haemolysis (dark urine, jaundice, scleral icterus) · reticulocytopenia · nucleated red cells on the film · thrombocytopenia or neutropenia (possible malignancy or infiltration) · severe B12 or folate deficiency · need for transfusion. [S1]
- ⚠️ **If you can, hold off transfusing until the diagnosis is definite.** [S1]
- **Consult the local paediatric team for red-flag features.** Discharge when the cause is identified and follow-up is arranged. [S1]

## Microcytic anaemia

- **Beta thalassaemia trait:** usually asymptomatic, microcytic hypochromic cells, Hb normal or borderline-low; the diagnosis is made on HPLC or Hb electrophoresis (HbA2 over 3.5%, often raised HbF). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Concurrent iron deficiency can mask a raised HbA2** — if ferritin is low, **treat the iron first, then test.** [S1]
- **Alpha thalassaemia trait cannot be diagnosed on HPLC or electrophoresis** — DNA testing is needed, but not first-line; exclude other causes first. **Pre-pregnancy partner carrier testing matters** for both traits. [S1]

## Normocytic anaemia

- ⚠️ **Acute haemolysis can be life-threatening: discuss every case with a haematologist and admit for observation**, with frequent heart-rate monitoring for a further Hb fall. [S1]
- **To pick up ongoing haemolysis, repeat the FBC within 6–12 hours**; monitor reticulocytes and bilirubin. Further tests (DAT, BGAB, G6PD assay, EMA staining) follow the film. [S1]
- **Hypoplastic/aplastic:** reticulocytes usually low. **Neutrophils or platelets also low → consider marrow infiltration.** Isolated anaemia with low reticulocytes and normal counts → consider TEC or congenital forms (e.g. Diamond-Blackfan). Bone marrow aspirate is usually required. [S1]

## Macrocytic anaemia

- **B12 and folate deficiency** can present with failure to thrive or with neurodevelopmental problems such as seizures, regression, poor feeding or irritability. **B12 risk:** exclusively breastfed infants of B12-deficient mothers, vegan or vegetarian diets, pernicious anaemia, metabolic disorders. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Investigate urgently** (red cell folate, active B12; if active B12 low, add homocysteine and urine methylmalonic acid) and **start treatment urgently, especially with neurological symptoms or regression.** [S1]
