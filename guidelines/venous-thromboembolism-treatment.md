# Venous thromboembolism (DVT and PE): diagnosis and treatment

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE): diagnosis, choice and duration of anticoagulation, extended therapy, bleeding and recurrence risk, thrombophilia testing and complications. VTE prevention in hospital is in `venous-thromboembolism-prevention`; anticoagulation in children is in `anticoagulation-children`; anticoagulation for AF is in `atrial-fibrillation`. PBS access is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ✅ **COMPILED FROM 2 SOURCES.** 34 claims; **62 fragments or anchors re-checkable by machine; 2 doses.** **S1** Thrombosis and Haemostasis Society of Australia and New Zealand (THANZ) (18 March 2019; AU): 22 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored · **S2** Ortel TL (published 2 October 2020; international): 12 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored

> ⚠️ **Older than 5 years (2019).** Cancer-associated VTE, reduced-dose DOAC use and PE thrombolysis/catheter therapy have moved on since; the PBS lists apixaban and rivaroxaban for both DVT and PE. **S2 (ASH 2020)** adds GRADE-rated advice on home treatment, thrombolysis and catheter therapy, treatment length and reduced-dose DOACs; it is also more than 5 years old, and cancer-associated VTE is still not covered.

> ⚠️ International guideline (ASH, 2020; S2). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ. Where S1 (THANZ) and S2 differ, both are shown.

## Recognise and diagnose

- **VTE symptoms are non-specific: only about 20% of suspected cases are confirmed,** so actively seek the diagnosis and confirm it with imaging; misdiagnosis in either direction does harm. [S1]
- **Wells or Geneva scores must be paired with D-dimer; neither alone can safely rule out VTE.** These rules are for outpatients and emergency departments, not inpatients. (GRADE strong, high) [S1]
- **PERC can rule out PE without more tests in patients under 50 when the expected PE rate is below 15%,** which applies in most Australian and New Zealand EDs; a positive PERC needs further work-up. [S1]
- **A negative D-dimer with a non-high clinical probability avoids imaging; a positive D-dimer alone proves nothing,** since it rises with cancer, inflammation, infection, trauma and pregnancy. [S1]
- **DVT: a single negative ankle-to-groin duplex ultrasound generally excludes DVT.** A single repeat scan 3–6 months later, before stopping anticoagulation, gives a baseline for future symptoms; repeated scans without symptoms do not help. [S1]
- **PE: CTPA is the preferred test** (GRADE strong, high); VQ scanning suits renal impairment and is preferred in pregnancy. A normal VQ excludes PE, but 27–55% of scans are non-diagnostic and need leg ultrasound or CTPA. [S1]
- **Think of hidden cancer after an unprovoked VTE:** up to 10% are diagnosed with cancer within a year. Examine carefully and do age-appropriate screening; abdominopelvic CT adds nothing. [S1]

## Treat

- **Classify the event as provoked (surgical or non-surgical; transient or persistent) or unprovoked:** this sets recurrence risk and treatment length. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Anticoagulate most VTE:** it cuts extension or recurrence by at least 80%. DOACs and warfarin work equally well (GRADE strong, high); DOACs are usually favoured as they need no routine monitoring. [S1]
- ⚠️ **For proximal DVT and PE, choose an oral factor Xa inhibitor (apixaban or rivaroxaban) over dabigatran or warfarin,** because no injectable lead-in is needed. (GRADE strong, high) [S1]
- ⚠️ **No DOACs in pregnancy or breastfeeding: use low molecular weight heparin.** In cancer-associated VTE, edoxaban or rivaroxaban match dalteparin for efficacy but bleed more, so they can be considered case by case. [S1]
- **Treat at home when you can (ASH, conditional):** for uncomplicated DVT, and for PE at low risk of complications (PESI or simplified PESI can help pick these patients but do not replace judgement). Admit instead for another reason needing hospital, no support at home, inability to pay for medicines or poor adherence; limb-threatening DVT, high bleeding risk, IV analgesia and submassive or massive PE may also need hospital. [S2]
- **ASH favours a DOAC over a vitamin K antagonist (conditional, moderate certainty)** but may not apply with creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min, liver disease of moderate or greater severity, or antiphospholipid syndrome. **ASH names no preferred DOAC:** choose by need for parenteral lead-in, once- or twice-daily dosing, cost, kidney function, CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein interactions and cancer. [S2]
- **Proximal DVT: anticoagulation alone for most rather than adding thrombolysis (ASH, conditional).** Consider thrombolysis for limb-threatening DVT (phlegmasia cerulea dolens) or for selected younger patients at low bleeding risk with iliac and common femoral vein clot; it should be rare below the common femoral vein. If thrombolysing extensive DVT, prefer catheter-directed to systemic. [S2]
- ⚠️ **PE with haemodynamic compromise: thrombolyse, then anticoagulate (ASH, strong despite low certainty).** Submassive PE (RV dysfunction on echo or biomarkers without compromise): anticoagulate alone, watch closely for deterioration, and reserve thrombolysis for younger low-bleeding-risk patients or those with cardiopulmonary disease likely to decompensate. For PE, systemic thrombolysis is preferred to catheter-directed, except in experienced centres for higher bleeding risk. [S2]
- **No IVC filter on top of anticoagulation (ASH, conditional)** for proximal DVT with significant cardiopulmonary disease or PE with haemodynamic compromise. If anticoagulation is contraindicated, a retrievable filter may be placed and removed once anticoagulation can start (consistent with S1). [S2]

## How long to treat

- ⚠️ **Proximal DVT or PE: at least 3 months for everyone.** If the cause was major surgery or trauma that has resolved, stop at 3 months. (GRADE strong) [S1]
- **Unprovoked, or provoked by a transient non-surgical factor: treat 3–6 months, then decide whether to extend.** Recurrent events (two or more), active cancer or antiphospholipid syndrome warrant extended anticoagulation. [S1]
- **Distal DVT:** anticoagulation benefit is uncertain; two duplex scans over 2 weeks is a reasonable alternative, especially with high bleeding risk. Most distal DVT is treated for 6–12 weeks, and 6 weeks if a major provoking factor has resolved. [S1]
- **Extending beyond 3 months** lowers recurrence by at least 80% during treatment, for a major bleeding risk under 1% a year; the benefit ends when treatment stops. Weigh bleeding against recurrence, with the patient's preference. [S1]
- ⚠️ **For extended therapy, consider a lower DOAC dose:** apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily works as well as 5.0 mg twice daily beyond 6 months, and rivaroxaban 10 mg once daily as well as 20 mg once daily. Either DOAC dose is preferred over warfarin. Avoid aspirin (100 mg daily) unless anticoagulation is impossible: it prevents far less recurrence yet bleeds like rivaroxaban 10 mg. (GRADE strong, high) [S1]
- **Recurrence predictors that drive most decisions:** unprovoked or persistent-factor events (vs surgical), PE or proximal DVT (vs distal), prior VTE, and male sex. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Bleeding risk:** active or recent (under 30 days) bleeding usually rules out anticoagulation. Other predictors: prior bleeding (especially on anticoagulants), a bleeding lesion such as peptic ulcer, surgery in the last 14 days, active cancer and severe kidney disease. [S1]
- **ASH primary treatment: 3–6 months rather than 6–12 months** whatever the trigger (transient, chronic or none; conditional, moderate certainty). A longer first phase can be justified where a chronic risk factor is expected to improve, such as mobility with rehabilitation. [S2]
- ⚠️ **ASH then advises indefinite antithrombotic therapy after unprovoked VTE, or VTE with a chronic risk factor,** rather than stopping (conditional, moderate), unless bleeding risk is high. VTE from a transient factor usually stops after primary treatment. (S1 instead treats unprovoked VTE for a fixed first period and then decides whether to extend.) [S2]
- **For unprovoked VTE, do not routinely use prognostic scores, D-dimer or ultrasound for residual clot to decide duration (ASH, conditional, very low certainty);** they may help when the patient is undecided or the balance is unclear. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Long-term prevention after primary treatment (ASH):** an anticoagulant rather than aspirin (conditional); on warfarin, target INR 2.0–3.0 rather than 1.5–1.9 (strong); on a DOAC, standard or reduced dose, such as apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily or rivaroxaban 10 mg once a day (conditional). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Recurrence on warfarin despite a therapeutic INR: switch to low molecular weight heparin rather than a DOAC and look for a cause (ASH, conditional);** a DOAC is reasonable if the INR was poorly controlled. [S2]
- **A second VTE (ASH):** recurrent unprovoked VTE warrants indefinite therapy (strong). A new transient-factor event after an earlier unprovoked or chronic-factor VTE: continue indefinitely; after an earlier transient-factor VTE: stop after primary treatment (both conditional). [S2]
- **Aspirin taken for stable cardiovascular disease: usually pause it while anticoagulated (ASH, conditional),** after reviewing why it was started; except soon after an acute coronary event or a coronary intervention. [S2]

## Testing and complications

- **Do not test routinely for inherited thrombophilia:** factor V Leiden and prothrombin gene heterozygosity change neither duration nor family advice. Testing for antiphospholipid syndrome after unprovoked VTE is reasonable. (GRADE strong, moderate) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Suspect CTEPH if breathlessness persists over the 2 years after PE** (incidence 3.4%): arrange a VQ scan and echocardiogram. Untreated, 5-year survival is 30%. [S1]
- **Post-thrombotic syndrome affects one in three after DVT;** anticoagulation does not prevent it, and compression stockings are for symptom relief of swelling and pain only. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Thrombolysis (or an alternative) is for massive, haemodynamically unstable PE;** invasive clot removal otherwise has a limited role. An IVC filter is only for acute VTE with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation, not for anticoagulated patients. (GRADE strong) [S1]
