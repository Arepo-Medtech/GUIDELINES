# Nappy rash

## Recognise it

- **Nappy rash is an irritant contact dermatitis; by twelve months over half of all infants have had it.** [S1]
- **Typical:** widespread erythema on convex skin that touches the nappy directly, **the groin folds usually spared**, with scaling, swelling, scattered papules and ulcers from broken skin. [S1]
- **Contributors:** urine and faecal enzymes overhydrating and macerating the skin (worse with infrequent changes, cloth nappies and plastic pants), friction, *Candida*, irritants in wipes, powders, creams and moisturisers, eczema, and recent antibiotics. [S1]
- **History to take:** nappy-change frequency and type, growth, feeding and stool output, prior treatments, atopy, prematurity or bowel surgery/short gut, and **maternal vegan/vegetarian diet in the third trimester (zinc deficiency).** Examine the whole body including scalp, the mouth for thrush, and the growth trend. [S1]

## Think of something else

- **Consider other causes when the rash is severe, unusual or not responding** — more than one condition can coexist. [S1]
- ***Candida*: erythema in the skin folds with satellite spots or pustules.** [S1]
- **Perianal streptococcal cellulitis:** well-demarcated perianal erythema with fissuring and maceration; may cause itch, painful defecation or constipation. [S1]
- **Psoriasis:** sharp, non-scaly, bright red plaques. **Seborrhoeic dermatitis:** salmon-pink patches that don't itch, ± greasy scale, also on scalp, face and folds. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Malabsorption** (cystic fibrosis, IBD, cow milk protein intolerance, lactose overload) may show as poor growth, diarrhoea and erosive dermatitis, causing intractable nappy rash. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Zinc deficiency (acrodermatitis enteropathica):** sharply defined anogenital rash, often extensive — check for dermatitis around the mouth and nose and on hands/feet, alopecia, diarrhoea and failure to thrive. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Langerhans cell histiocytosis:** chronic anogenital or inguinal rash, often erosive, with petechiae and brownish-red scale, that fails to respond to treatment; there may be fever, purpura, hepatosplenomegaly or diarrhoea. [S1]
- **Generally no investigations are required.** [S1]

## Treat

- **Cleanse with warm water ± bath oil and a soft cloth at every change**; pat or air dry, no rubbing; maximise nappy-free time; daily warm bath with fragrance-free cleanser or bath oil; **no soap, no bubble bath.** [S1]
- **Barrier cream, thickly, with each change** — zinc, white soft paraffin or petrolatum. **Not responding to low-zinc cream → switch to a higher concentration, eg 40%.** [S1]
- **Don't strip the barrier at each change** — remove visible soiling and layer more on top. [S1]
- **Not responding to simple measures: over the barrier cream add 1% hydrocortisone ointment, 1–2 times a day for 1–2 weeks.** [S1]
- ***Candida*:** single agent, twice daily — clotrimazole 1% cream **or** miconazole 2% cream; **or** combination — a cream of hydrocortisone 1% + miconazole 2%, twice daily, **or** an ointment of zinc oxide 150 mg/g + miconazole 2.5 mg, thickly at every change. A topical antifungal can be combined with 1% hydrocortisone. [S1]
- **Bacterial infection:** see Cellulitis and skin infections. Formulary restrictions may apply — check local guidelines. [S1]

## Prevent and follow up

- **Prevention:** change wet or soiled nappies as soon as possible, wash and rinse cloth nappies thoroughly, **no plastic pants**, maximise nappy-free time, clean with good baby wipes or warm water, **barrier cream at every change**, and **avoid powders (eg talc) and soaps.** [S1]
- **Consult the local paediatric team** if the rash isn't improving or a cause other than irritant dermatitis is suspected. **Discharge** once the family knows the plan and follow-up is in place. [S1]
