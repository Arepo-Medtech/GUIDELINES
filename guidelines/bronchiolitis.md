# Bronchiolitis

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** viral lower respiratory infection in infants, mostly under 12 months: recognition, risk factors, oxygen and high-flow thresholds, feeding and hydration, what not to give, referral and discharge. Related pages in this set: `croup` (a viral illness of the same age group treated almost the opposite way) and `acute-asthma-children`.

> ✅ **COMPILED FROM 2 SOURCES.** 52 claims; **93 fragments or anchors re-checkable by machine; 4 doses.** **S1** The Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne (last updated November 2023, PIC endorsed; AU): 40 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored · **S2** The Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne (management approach diagram on the same page, transcribed in Wave 1; AU): 12 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored

> ⚠️ **Supportive care only.** No bronchodilator, steroid, adrenaline (outside arrest), hypertonic saline, antibiotic or antiviral. In `croup`, by contrast, steroids and nebulised adrenaline are the treatment.

> ⚠️ **Oxygen threshold is 90%.** Brief dips below it are expected and are not a reason to start oxygen; high flow is only for failure of standard nasal prong oxygen.

> 🖼️ S2 claims come from the RCH management-approach diagram (respiratory and hydration branches, including the 2 L/kg per min high-flow rate).

## Key points

- **Diagnose it clinically;** tests are not needed. [S1]
- **Aim of care:** keep the infant oxygenated and hydrated. Apart from simple pain relief, no medicine is indicated. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ex-premature infants and those with heart or lung disease** deteriorate more often and are more likely to be admitted. [S1]
- **Nasal prong oxygen when SpO2 stays below 90%;** keep high-flow nasal prong (HFNP) therapy for when standard nasal prong oxygen has failed. [S1]

## Recognise it

- **What it is:** a viral infection of the lower respiratory tract, usually in infants below 12 months, diagnosed from the typical history and examination. [S1]
- **Course:** worst at about day 2-3 after the work of breathing increases, settling over 7-10 days (the course varies). It resolves on its own and usually needs no treatment; the cough can last weeks. [S1]
- **Onset:** a runny-nose prodrome, then any of cough · fever · feeding difficulty · apnoeas · irritability or unsettledness. [S1]
- **Risk factors for more serious illness:** congenital heart disease · chronic lung disease · chronic neurological conditions · born before 37 weeks, or under 10 weeks old at presentation · growth restriction · Indigenous ethnicity · immunodeficiency · cigarette smoke exposure · trisomy 21. [S1]
- **Consider admitting infants with these risk factors** even when they present early with mild symptoms. [S1]
- **On examination:** fast breathing · accessory muscle use · wheeze and/or fine crackles on both sides · hypoxia. [S1]

## Investigations

- **Most infants need no tests.** Investigate only if the child deteriorates or the diagnosis is in doubt (for example a murmur with signs of heart failure). [S1]
- **No chest X-ray:** it cannot tell viral from bacterial infection, is poorly reproducible at this age, and can prompt needless antibiotics. [S1]
- **No blood tests** (not a gas, full blood examination or blood culture). [S1]
- **No viral swab or aspirate** for managing the individual child; the result should not steer treatment. [S1]

## Supportive care and oxygen

- **Treatment is supportive:** adequate oxygen, adequate fluids, and handling the infant as little as possible. [S1]
- **Observation without treatment:** some young infants with risk factors, or with apnoeas (no breath for more than 15 seconds, often with a slow heart rate and/or desaturation), benefit from a stay in hospital even if they need neither fluids nor oxygen. [S1]
- **Target SpO2: 90%,** unless an underlying heart or lung condition sets its own target. [S1]
- **Short, mild or moderate dips below 90% are expected** and are not a reason to start oxygen. [S1]
- **Start nasal prong oxygen only for SpO2 that stays below 90%.** If it stays below 90% and 2 hours of low-flow oxygen have not improved breathing, move to HFNP. [S1]
- **No HFNP** for an infant whose SpO2 is consistently 90% or more. [S1]
- **Wean oxygen down to the least that keeps SpO2 at 90% or more;** there is no need to push saturations into the mid or high 90s. [S1]
- **Every 6 hours, try the infant off oxygen, whether HFNP or nasal prong;** restart nasal prong oxygen if SpO2 is still under 90%. [S1]
- **Off oxygen for 2 hours → stop saturation monitoring.** Keep observations 2-4 hourly, and bring back intermittent oximetry if the infant worsens. [S1]

## Feeding and fluids

- **Keep offering comfort oral feeds;** infants tend to settle better. If other hydration is needed, use a nasogastric (NG) tube first; it is safe during respiratory support. [S1]
- **NG bolus feeds are safe;** start at two-thirds of maintenance because ADH secretion may be raised. [S1]
- **IV fluids only when NG feeding is not possible;** use an isotonic fluid with glucose added. [S1]

## Medicines

- **Simple analgesia only;** other medicines are not routinely indicated. [S1]
- **Chloral hydrate** can settle agitation in an infant whose respiratory distress is worsening, whether or not they are on respiratory support; it works best under 2 years and under 15 kg. [S1]
- **Chloral hydrate dose, 0-2 years:** start at 10 mg/kg every 4-6 hours; if needed go to 15 mg/kg, then up to 20 mg/kg every 4-6 hours. ⚠️ Outside critical care, closer monitoring and senior involvement are required. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Chloral hydrate adverse effects:** oversedation · respiratory depression · hypotension · paradoxical agitation. Take care in renal or hepatic impairment. [S1]
- **Caffeine:** not enough evidence to use it to prevent apnoea in bronchiolitis. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not give:** beta-2 agonists (even with personal or family atopy) · corticosteroids by any route (nebulised, oral, IM or IV) · adrenaline by any route (nebulised, IM or IV) unless the infant is peri-arrest or in arrest · nebulised hypertonic saline · antibiotics, azithromycin included · antivirals · caffeine. [S1]
- **Nasal suction:** not deep (beyond the nasopharynx). Superficial suction may help a moderately affected infant feed; saline nose drops at feed times may be considered. [S1]
- **No chest physiotherapy.** [S1]

## What the diagram adds

- **Two assessments side by side:** breathing (respiratory status) and hydration. [S2]
- **Not persistently hypoxaemic (SpO2 90% or more in air):** discharge, unless moderate to severe work of breathing, risk factors for severe disease or apnoeas justify a period of observation. [S2]
- **Persistently hypoxaemic:** low-flow nasal prong oxygen at 2 L/min; hourly observations in moderate disease, continuous if severely unwell. ⚠️ Severe or life-threatening illness: get an ICU review and start HFNP or CPAP. [S2]
- **Review the response within 2-3 hours,** sooner if the infant is unwell. [S2]
- **Still hypoxaemic (below 90%), tachycardic, tachypnoeic or working hard after 2-3 hours?** If the hypoxaemia is only mild and the infant is otherwise better, a longer trial of nasal prong oxygen is an option. [S2]
- **Improving:** titrate and wean oxygen to keep SpO2 at 90% or more; stop saturation monitoring once off oxygen for 2 hours or more. [S2]
- **Not improving:** consider HFNP at 2 L/kg per min, adjusting FiO2 to keep SpO2 at 90% or more. [S2]
- **Response to HFNP means:** SpO2 90% or more · better heart rate, breathing rate and/or work of breathing · no significant or repeated apnoeas with desaturation or bradycardia. [S2]
- **No response to HFNP:** get paediatric intensive care advice (on site or through retrieval); consider CPAP in severe disease. [S2]
- **Responding to HFNP:** wean FiO2 towards room air keeping SpO2 at 90% or more; try off oxygen every 6 hours, restarting nasal prong oxygen if SpO2 is under 90%; stop HFNP once stable in air. [S2]
- **Hydration branch:** if intake is below 50% over 12 hours or the infant is clinically dehydrated, start NG feeds at 2/3 of maintenance, and stop them once 2/3 of feeds are taken by mouth. [S2]
- **Observation regardless of treatment:** with apnoeas, moderate to severe work of breathing or risk factors for severe disease, consider keeping the infant in hospital even if no oxygen or fluids are needed. [S2]

## Referral, transfer and discharge

- **Talk to the local paediatric team** if the infant has a listed risk factor · needs oxygen · is taking under half the usual feeds and is clinically dehydrated · has moderate or severe respiratory status. [S1]
- **Think about transfer** for severe bronchiolitis · risk factors for severe illness · care beyond what the local hospital is comfortable with · HFNP needing FiO2 above 50%. [S1]
- **Discharge** once SpO2 is 90% or more and feeding is adequate. ⚠️ Infants under 8 weeks are more likely to re-present. [S1]
- **Hospital in the Home on home oxygen** may suit selected infants after observation, per local policy. Explain the illness to parents, how it is expected to progress, and when and where to get help. [S1]

## Currency and evidence

- **Currency:** PIC endorsed; page last updated November 2023. [S1]
- **Key evidence cited:** Dalziel et al., Lancet 2022, and the 2018 Australasian Bronchiolitis Guideline from the PREDICT network. [S1]
