# Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (mycosis fungoides and Sézary syndrome)

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, staging principles and stage-appropriate treatment of primary cutaneous T-cell lymphoma in adults, chiefly mycosis fungoides (MF) and Sézary syndrome (SS): skin-directed therapy, radiotherapy, systemic agents (bexarotene, interferon, brentuximab vedotin, mogamulizumab, HDAC inhibitors), extracorporeal photopheresis, maintenance and follow-up. Systemic (non-cutaneous) T-cell lymphoma: see `peripheral-t-cell-lymphoma`. Cutaneous B-cell lymphoma is only noted.

## Recognise and stage

- **Cutaneous lymphomas arise in the skin with no other organ involved at staging**; about 73% are T-cell types. They must be told apart from skin spread of nodal lymphoma or leukaemia, which is managed very differently. [S1]
- **Manage jointly between a specialist centre and the primary doctor.** [S1]
- **Clinical findings often suggest the diagnosis, but confirm it with histology, immunohistology and molecular tests**; examine the whole skin and every lymph node region. [S1]
- **Stage MF and SS with the revised TNMB system.** Early MF (IA-IIA) usually carries a very good outlook, with mean survival of about 10 to 20 years. [S1]
- **Work-up at first staging (S1 Table 3):** for MF and its variants, a chest X-ray with abdominal and lymph node ultrasound; from stage IIB onwards, and in Sézary syndrome, a contrast CT of neck, chest, abdomen and pelvis with lymph node ultrasound, adding PET-CT where indicated. Use flow cytometry if blood involvement is suspected, and include clonality testing in the diagnosis. Re-staging after treatment, at progression and in aggressive types is tailored to the patient. [S1]

## Early-stage disease: skin-directed treatment

- **Treat by entity and stage, and keep treatment restrained** for the common CTCL types. [S1]
- **In early stages, prefer skin-directed options:** topical steroids, phototherapy, topical chlormethine (or carmustine), bexarotene gel or imiquimod. [S1]
- **Phototherapy for stages IA-IIA:** narrow-band UVB (311 nm) or PUVA. Reserve PUVA mainly for thick plaques or folliculotropic MF; either suits erythrodermic MF. [S1]
- **Radiotherapy:** for localised CTCL, at least 30 Gy in 2 Gy fractions controls 90-100% of lesions long term. Low-dose palliative radiotherapy (8 Gy, given as one or two fractions) gave complete remission in at least 92%. [S1]
- **Total skin electron beam:** conventional doses (30-36 Gy) work but are harsher on the skin; low-dose 10-12 Gy over 2-3 weeks is a safer short-term option, followed by maintenance treatment. [S1]

## Advanced disease: systemic treatment

- **Advanced stages call for systemic treatment**, typically PUVA combined with a retinoid or interferon-alpha; bexarotene is also recommended. [S1]
- **Conventional interferon-alpha (Roferon) is no longer available;** pegylated interferon is used off-label; in a small series peginterferon alfa-2a at 180 μg weekly worked as well as higher doses. Start at a moderate dose and watch for liver and marrow toxicity. [S1]
- **Brentuximab vedotin is an option for advanced CD30-positive MF** (tumour stage). [S1]
- **Mogamulizumab (anti-CCR4, approved 2019) is a second-line systemic option** for refractory or advanced MF and for SS after at least one prior systemic therapy; it works especially well on blood involvement. [S1]
- **HDAC inhibitors (vorinostat, romidepsin) and pralatrexate are alternatives** approved in the USA rather than Europe. [S1]
- 🇦🇺 **Mogamulizumab (eviQ ID 4556), for relapsed or refractory MF or SS after at least one systemic therapy:** 1 mg/kg IV on days 1, 8, 15 and 22 of the first 28-day cycle, then on days 1 and 15 of later cycles, until progression or unacceptable toxicity. Give each dose within 2 days of its date; if more than 2 days late, give it as soon as possible and restart the schedule from then. PBS Authority; premedication is required. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Mogamulizumab rash can mimic progression:** it takes many forms (papular, lichenoid, granulomatous, psoriasiform and others), so consider a skin biopsy to tell drug eruption from disease; SJS and TEN, some fatal, have occurred. Watch for immune-mediated harm (myositis, myocarditis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, Guillain-Barré, hypothyroidism; consider thyroid tests) and high blood sugar. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Mogamulizumab before an allogeneic transplant raises the risk of severe and steroid-refractory GVHD and transplant death,** more so when given within about 50 days of the transplant; watch closely for early GVHD. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **Vorinostat (eviQ ID 1391), for relapsed or chemotherapy-refractory CTCL:** 400 mg by mouth once a day until progression or unacceptable toxicity (100 mg capsules; PBS Authority). If it is not tolerated, drop to 300 mg once a day with or just after food, then if needed to 300 mg on 5 consecutive days each week. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Vorinostat safety (eviQ):** it can prolong the QT interval (correct electrolytes, avoid other QT drugs, consider ECG and electrolyte checks), causes venous thromboembolism, and can dehydrate through diarrhoea and vomiting; patients should drink at least 2 L a day. Check blood count, kidney and liver tests, calcium, magnesium, phosphate and glucose at baseline, fortnightly over the first 2 months, and 4-weekly after that. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **Brentuximab vedotin (eviQ ID 1665) for relapsed or refractory CD30-positive CTCL** (MF, SS or primary cutaneous ALCL): 1.8 mg/kg IV (capped at 180 mg) every 21 days for up to 16 cycles; PBS Authority. Assess neuropathy before every dose (grade 2 or worse may need a lower dose, delay or omission) and watch for PML, pancreatitis and severe skin reactions. [S2]
- **Before any of these protocols (eviQ):** screen for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti-HBc and anti-HBs), and avoid live vaccines in moderately or severely immunocompromised patients. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Prefer single-agent chemotherapy** (for example low-dose gemcitabine or doxorubicin) to combination regimens, which cause heavy immunosuppression without improving survival. [S1]

## Sézary syndrome and late stages

- **Extracorporeal photopheresis works in SS with few side effects**, alone or with interferon-alpha, PUVA, topical steroids or bexarotene. [S1]
- **Second-choice options in SS:** bexarotene or low-dose methotrexate (ideally with PUVA and photopheresis), or total skin electron beam. Doxorubicin or gemcitabine can debulk advanced SS; use alemtuzumab at low doses to limit infection. [S1]
- **S1's Sézary syndrome table (order within each column is not a ranking):** first choice is extracorporeal photopheresis, combined as needed with PUVA, interferon-alpha and/or bexarotene, or else PUVA with interferon-alpha and/or bexarotene. Second choice: mogamulizumab; chlorambucil with a steroid (the Winkelmann regimen); low-dose methotrexate; bexarotene; total skin electron beam; alemtuzumab (IV, or low-dose subcutaneous); doxorubicin, fludarabine, cladribine or gemcitabine; allogeneic stem cell transplant for selected patients; or brentuximab vedotin (off-label). [S1]
- **Allogeneic stem cell transplant may suit selected patients.** Palliative chemotherapy in late stages has no proven survival benefit and adds infection risk. [S1]

## Maintenance, supportive care and follow-up

- **After remission or down-staging of advanced disease, maintain with the guideline's non-cytotoxic treatments.** [S1]
- **For itch (off-label):** in CTCL, gabapentin 300 to 2400 mg/day, or mirtazapine 7.5-30 mg taken at bedtime; in SS, aprepitant 80 mg/day. [S1]
- **Follow-up aims to catch relapse, second lymphomas and treatment harms** (such as PUVA-related skin cancers). Teach every patient to check their own skin and feel their lymph nodes regularly. [S1]
