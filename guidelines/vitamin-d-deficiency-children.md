# Vitamin D deficiency in children

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** risk factors, screening, colecalciferol treatment and prevention of low vitamin D and nutritional rickets in infants, children and adolescents. Related: `hypocalcaemia-children` (vitamin D deficiency is its commonest cause). The adult bone-health counterpart is `osteoporosis`, which does not address calcium and vitamin D.

## Background and definitions

- **At every age, sunlight is the main source of vitamin D.** Australian cases of nutritional rickets are generally confined to dark-skinned infants and children. [S1]
- **Breastmilk has almost no vitamin D**, whereas infant formula is fortified. [S1]
- **Recommended intake without sun exposure:** 0–12 months 400 units daily; 1–18 years 400–600 units daily. [S1]
- **Status by 25-OH-D:** severe deficiency under 12.5 nmol/L · moderate 12.5–29 · mild 30–49 · **sufficient 50 or more (the target at all ages)** · elevated 250 or more. **Toxicity = over 250 nmol/L with hypercalcaemia and PTH suppression.** [S1]

## Recognise it

- **Risk factors:** little sun exposure (time indoors, covering clothes), dark skin, conditions that alter vitamin D metabolism (medications, severe malabsorption, liver/renal failure, obesity); **infants: exclusive breastfeeding plus** any of these, maternal deficiency or prematurity. [S1]
- **Low-calcium symptoms:** tetany, muscle cramps, stridor, seizures (seizures are rare after 6–12 months of age). [S1]
- **Signs of rickets:** long-bone deformity, widened wrists/ankles, late closure of the anterior fontanelle (normally shut by 2 years), frontal bossing, rachitic rosary, increased fracture risk; also late dentition (still toothless at 9 months, or molar-free at 14 months). [S1]

## Investigate

- ⚠️ **Consider admission for severe rickets or symptomatic hypocalcaemia (tetany, stridor, seizures).** Refer for specialist review: clinical rickets · an infant with symptoms/signs · abnormal serum calcium · deficiency not responding to high-dose supplements. [S1]
- **A breastfed infant under 12 months who has other risk factors but no symptoms or signs can usually start 400 units daily untested.** [S1]
- **Any child/adolescent with at least one risk factor: screen with 25-OH-D, calcium, phosphate, ALP**; add PTH when there are symptoms, several risk factors or a low calcium intake. **Rickets:** add Mg, PTH, UEC, urine studies and a left wrist X-ray. [S1]

## Treat (oral D3 / colecalciferol; 1 microgram = 40 units)

- **Treat low vitamin D to restore levels to 50 nmol/L or more**, ensure adequate calcium intake, and educate on sun exposure. Evidence for high-dose treatment under 3 months is limited. [S1]
- **Preterm:** 200 units/kg/day when mild (30–49 nmol/L), max 400 units/day (same for maintenance); moderate–severe (under 30) 800 units/day, review after 1 month. [S1]
- **Term, under 3 months:** mild 400 units/day for 3 months (then 400 units daily); moderate–severe 1,000 units/day for 3 months. [S1]
- **3–12 months:** mild 400 units/day for 3 months (then 400 units daily); moderate–severe, 3 months of 1,000 units/day **or** a stat 50,000 units, review after 1 month and consider repeating. [S1]
- **1–18 years, mild (30–49):** 3 months of 1,000–2,000 units/day, **or** a stat 150,000 units. **Maintenance:** 400–600 units a day, **or** 3000–4000 units a week, **or** 150,000 units as autumn begins. [S1]
- **1–18 years, moderate–severe (under 30):** 6 months of 1,000–2,000 units daily, **or** 3 months of 3,000–4,000 units daily, **or** a stat 150,000 units, repeated at 6 weeks. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Nutritional rickets: specialist-guided; minimum vitamin D 2000 units daily for at least 3 months plus oral calcium 500 mg daily** (diet or supplement); phosphate may also be needed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Check which product the family uses** — **overdose can happen**, as some preparations contain 1000 units per drop; most adult tablets are 1000 units. [S1]

## Prevention and follow-up

- **At-risk breastfed infants: vitamin D 400 units a day, for the first 12 months at least.** Fully formula-fed infants usually need no supplement. [S1]
- **Repeat bloods:** not usually needed in mild deficiency; otherwise at 1 month in infants, 3 months if older. [S1]
- **Self-management plan:** over the cooler months (May–August), consider 400–600 units a day **or** 3000–4000 units a week to avoid blood tests and high-dose therapy. [S1]
- **Sun:** normal sunscreen use does not cause low vitamin D; children with dark skin can tolerate intermittent sun without sunscreen (hat and sunglasses still recommended). In winter in southern Australia, sun alone may not maintain levels. [S1]
