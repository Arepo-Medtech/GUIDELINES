# Kidney transplant recipients — immunosuppression and rejection

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** induction, initial and long-term maintenance immunosuppression, drug-level monitoring, treatment of acute rejection and chronic allograft injury, and graft-function monitoring and biopsy in kidney transplant recipients in Australia and New Zealand. Not covered: pre-transplant assessment, donors, infection prophylaxis (see `cytomegalovirus-infection`), and heart or other organ transplants. Edition 2.0 adds S2 (The Transplantation Society consensus, 2020) on antibody-mediated rejection.

## Induction

- **Start combined immunosuppression before or at the time of transplant** (1A), and include a biologic induction agent (1B). [S1]
- **First-line induction is an IL-2 receptor antagonist** (1B). With high risk of acute cellular rejection, a T-cell-depleting agent may be used instead (2B). A mycophenolate loading dose can be considered (2B). [S1]
- **Donor-specific anti-HLA antibody:** consider peri-transplant plasmapheresis and/or high-dose IV immunoglobulin (2C). **ABO-incompatible transplant:** plasmapheresis or immunoadsorption to the titre the unit accepts, with post-transplant antibody removal depending on baseline titre (2A). [S1]

## Initial maintenance

- **Maintain with a calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) plus an antiproliferative, with or without steroids** (1B); **mycophenolate is the first-line antiproliferative** (1B). [S1]
- **Tacrolimus is the preferred CNI in higher-risk patients** (2A). Start tacrolimus or ciclosporin before or at transplant, not after graft function begins (2D tacrolimus; 2B ciclosporin). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not start an mTOR inhibitor until the graft is working, wounds have healed and there is no rejection** (1B). [S1]
- **Low immunological risk with induction:** steroids may be minimised or withdrawn early (2B). [S1]

## Long-term maintenance

- ⚠️ **By 4 months, move rejection-free patients to low-level exposure** as in the Symphony trial: a tacrolimus trough of 3–7 ng/mL, 1–2 g of mycophenolate a day and 5 mg of prednisone a day (1B). [S1]
- **Keep the CNI rather than withdraw it** (2B); if prednisolone is still used after week one, continue it (2C). [S1]
- **In Australia and New Zealand, most recipients get anti-CD25 induction** (about 95% of transplants in Australia, around 50% in New Zealand) and triple maintenance: a CNI (usually tacrolimus), a mycophenolate and steroids. mTOR inhibitors are used in under 10%. [S1]

## Drug-level monitoring

- **Individualise target ranges** to immunological and toxicity risk and co-therapy (2C). Note the assay: immunoassays cross-react with metabolites and usually read higher than HPLC (1B). [S1]
- **Measure ciclosporin and tacrolimus levels often early on** (for example every second day until stable), after dose changes, after relevant changes in clinical state or interacting drugs, and when over- or under-immunosuppression is suspected (1C). [S1]
- **Tacrolimus: use trough (C0) levels** (2D). **Ciclosporin:** 12 h trough (C0), 2 h post-dose (C2), or a validated limited sampling strategy (2C). [S1]
- **Mycophenolate levels are not routine** but may help in selected cases (high immunological risk, interacting changes, suspected over- or under-immunosuppression). Early target mycophenolic acid AUC0–12: 30 to 60 mg·h/L; no target is known beyond 12 months (2C). [S1]
- **Monitor mTOR inhibitor blood levels** (1C) after starting or changing dose, with suspected interactions, or when immunosuppression seems excessive or inadequate; trough levels can be used, but targets vary by drug, rejection risk and time since transplant (2C). [S1]

## Acute rejection

- ⚠️ **Biopsy before treating acute rejection unless it would substantially delay treatment** (1C). Treat subclinical and borderline cellular rejection too (2D). [S1]
- **Acute cellular rejection: first give a short course of high-dose steroids** (1D), and add back maintenance prednisone in patients not on it (2D). Use lymphocyte-depleting antibody for resistant rejection or vascular rejection (Banff grade II or more) (2C). [S1]
- **Antibody-mediated rejection:** consider plasma exchange and/or IV immunoglobulin (2C). [S1]
- **After any rejection, step up baseline immunosuppression:** add mycophenolate or switch azathioprine to it; add a CNI; switch ciclosporin to tacrolimus or an mTOR inhibitor to a CNI; or raise doses (2D). [S1]

## Antibody-mediated rejection (TTS consensus)

- **Antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) is a leading cause of graft loss:** up to 25% of recipients develop de novo donor-specific anti-HLA antibody (dnDSA) by 10 years, and AMR was the commonest cause of failure in one cohort biopsied before graft loss. [S2]
- **Before transplant, type donor and recipient HLA, screen for anti-HLA antibody (single-antigen bead assays first line) and take a history of sensitising events** such as an earlier transplant, transfusion or pregnancy (1A). Recipients with no such history and negative bead testing are at low AMR risk. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Test for dnDSA whenever the clinician reduces immunosuppression, when non-adherence is known, and at any rejection episode** (2B). dnDSA signals under-immunosuppression: review maintenance therapy, watch graft function closely (1B) and biopsy to look for cellular or antibody-mediated rejection. [S2]
- **Reading DSA results:** single-antigen bead results vary between and within laboratories (a positive cut-off is usually an MFI of 1000–1500), and complement-binding (C1q or C3d) assays are not advised routinely. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Early active AMR (within 30 days): plasma exchange plus IV immunoglobulin with corticosteroids is the consensus standard of care;** some centres keep steroids for concurrent T-cell-mediated rejection. Where graft-loss risk is high, add a complement inhibitor, rituximab or splenectomy as available, and treat any concurrent T-cell-mediated rejection. [S2]
- **Later AMR (from 30 days) with pre-existing DSA but no chronic features:** plasma exchange, IV immunoglobulin and corticosteroids. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Chronic active AMR: plasma exchange and IV immunoglobulin, with or without rituximab, have not been shown to help** and add infection risk and cost. Optimise immunosuppression and supportive care instead: restart steroids if the regimen was steroid-free, keep the tacrolimus trough above 5 ng/mL, and control blood pressure, glucose and lipids. [S2]
- **AMR with dnDSA usually follows under-immunosuppression** (non-adherence or a deliberate reduction) and often comes with T-cell-mediated rejection: optimise baseline immunosuppression, tackle adherence and treat the cellular rejection. Some centres add plasma exchange, IV immunoglobulin and rituximab, but the evidence is weak (3C). [S2]
- **Adjuncts without trial support:** rituximab showed no clear benefit in three small randomised trials; bortezomib alone did not help in the only randomised trial (late AMR); T-cell-depleting antibody has shown no benefit in pure AMR. [S2]

## Chronic allograft injury and graft monitoring

- **Biopsy any graft with falling function of unclear cause** to find reversible causes (1C). With CNI toxicity on histology, reduce, stop or replace the CNI (2C); swap to an mTOR inhibitor only if eGFR is over 40 mL/min per 1.73 m2 with urine protein under 50 mg/mmol creatinine, and not when eGFR is below 40 (2D). [S1]
- **Check serum creatinine often** (1B): at least daily for 7 days or until discharge, two to three times weekly in weeks 2–4, weekly in months 2 and 3, every 2 weeks in months 4–6, monthly in months 7–12, then every 2–3 months. Check urine PCR or ACR once in month one, every 3 months in year one, then annually (2C). [S1]
- **Biopsy when creatinine rises persistently without explanation** (1C), when it fails to return to baseline after rejection treatment (2D), with new or unexplained proteinuria (2C), every 5–10 days during delayed graft function (2C), or if expected function is not reached by 1–2 months (2D). [S1]
