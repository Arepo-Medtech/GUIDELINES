# Progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** children and adults with PFIC types 1–6 and benign recurrent intrahepatic cholestasis (BRIC): recognition by subtype, nutrition, pruritus treatment including ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT) inhibitors, cancer surveillance, biliary diversion and liver transplant. Alagille syndrome is in the source but not summarised here. Adult cholestatic disease: see `primary-biliary-cholangitis`.

> ⚠️ International sources (S1: Hepatology Communications, 2023; S2: Latin American consensus, 2026; S3: European expert opinion, 2024). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **S1 is a narrative review; S2 is a formal expert consensus (PICO questions, systematic review, Delphi voting); S3 is an expert opinion paper.** Subtypes, nutrition, the older pruritus drugs, surgery and cancer risk rest mainly on S1; S2 adds when to suspect and refer, genetic confirmation and surgical limits; S3 adds an IBAT-first treatment algorithm, response criteria and a monitoring schedule. ## Recognise

- PFIC is a group of single-gene disorders of canalicular bile acid transport or secretion; the intermittent form is BRIC and the chronic form is PFIC. [S1]
- PFIC disorders are autosomal recessive. Pruritus is the most significant clinical feature and can affect school, sleep and quality of life. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Serum GGT separates the types:** PFIC1 and PFIC2 have low or normal GGT; PFIC3 (ABCB4/MDR3) is distinguished by raised GGT. [S1]
- **PFIC1 (ATP8B1)** starts in infancy and typically progresses to cirrhosis by the second decade, with extrahepatic features (pancreatitis, diarrhoea, sensorineural hearing loss) that can persist or worsen after liver transplant. [S1]
- **PFIC2 (ABCB11/BSEP)** is the most common form, usually presenting in the neonatal period; without treatment it progresses rapidly to cirrhosis. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption can cause catastrophic bleeding (vitamin K), rickets (vitamin D) and neuromuscular dysfunction (vitamin E).** [S1]

## Suspect, refer and confirm (Latin American consensus)

- **Suspect PFIC in jaundice with itch, raised transaminases, usually low GGT (raised in types 3, 6, 8 and 9) and high serum bile acids, once other causes of cholestasis are excluded.** More than 12 subtypes are now described; about 10-15% of neonatal cholestasis is PFIC, and it affects roughly one in 50,000 to one in 100,000 births. [S2]
- **Do not work up every cholestatic newborn for PFIC.** Widen the search when cholestasis persists without a cause after common ones are excluded, when GGT is low or normal with intense itch, when standard treatment fails, or with a family history of early liver disease or an unexplained sibling death. [S2]
- **Refer early:** intractable or hard-to-control itch (with or without jaundice) should go to a specialist, and suspected PFIC to a paediatric hepatologist. [S2]
- **Confirm with molecular testing.** A negative panel does not exclude PFIC when suspicion is high: intronic variants, microdeletions, unreported variants, undiscovered genes or a panel with too few genes can all be missed. [S2]

## Nutrition and supportive care

- Failure to thrive and malnutrition are common and need close monitoring and supplementation, especially with **formula containing medium-chain triglycerides**, plus fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K). Some children need nasogastric or gastrostomy feeds. [S1]
- Skin emollients, trimmed fingernails and avoiding hot baths help minimise itching and excoriation. [S1]

## Pruritus: first-line medicines

- Antihistamine and ursodeoxycholic acid can be trialled but are typically not very effective alone in PFIC. **Ursodeoxycholic acid 10–20 mg/kg daily** may improve liver biochemistry but helps little in severe pruritus with PFIC1 or PFIC2; in PFIC3 it has normalised liver tests in half of patients. [S1]
- Other medicines to consider include rifampicin, cholestyramine or antihistamines. Cholestyramine must be given at least 2 hours apart from other medicines and increases the risk of fat-soluble vitamin deficiency. [S1]
- **Start supportive treatment straight away** (S2): fat-soluble vitamins and MCT dosed for age, and for itch a choice individualised to local rules among IBAT inhibitors, UDCA, rifampicin, cholestyramine, antihistamines such as hydroxyzine, sertraline, ondansetron and naltrexone. [S2]

## Pruritus: IBAT inhibitors (odevixibat)

- Ileal bile acid transporter inhibitors have shown efficacy for refractory pruritus in PFIC; two are available, maralixibat and odevixibat. [S1]
- Odevixibat is approved (in the source's jurisdiction) for pruritus in all types of PFIC from 3 months of age. [S1]
- In the phase 3 PEDFIC trial (PFIC1 or PFIC2), odevixibat improved pruritus by week 4, with sustained improvement, lower aminotransferases and better growth and sleep; no serious adverse events were reported. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Odevixibat dosing: start at 40 µg/kg once daily; if pruritus has not improved after 3 months, increase in 40 µg/kg steps up to 120 µg/kg once daily.** [S1]
- Maralixibat in PFIC1 and PFIC2 (INDIGO) gave a response that varied by subtype; BSEP deficiency with residual function did best. [S1]
- **IBAT inhibitors should be started by a specialist, guided by the clinical picture and the molecular result; measure serum bile acids before starting** (S2). [S2]
- **European expert algorithm (S3): if raised bile acids and low GGT strongly suggest PFIC, start an IBAT inhibitor empirically while the gene panel is pending;** consider it too for suspected PFIC3 with raised GGT once conditions needing urgent treatment are excluded. If the panel is negative and suspicion stays high, request whole-exome sequencing and continue treatment. [S3]
- ⚠️ **S3 dosing sequence: odevixibat 40 µg/kg/day for 12 weeks; if itch improves only partly or not at all, 120 µg/kg/day for another 12 weeks; if still no better, stop.** Then try the older drugs one at a time (such as rifampicin, UDCA, ondansetron, sertraline, naltrexone, cholestyramine, hydroxyzine, diphenhydramine or phenobarbitone), and consider surgical biliary diversion if they fail. [S3]
- **Define response (S3) as serum bile acids falling by more than 70%, or the itch score dropping by at least 1 point.** Odevixibat is approved from 6 months in the EU and from 3 months in the US and UK. [S3]
- ⚠️ **A single protein-truncating BSEP (ABCB11) variant predicts severe disease** with poor response to biliary diversion and to IBAT inhibitors; no patient with such a variant had a bile acid response to maralixibat. [S3]

## Monitor

- ⚠️ **PFIC2 carries a risk of hepatocellular carcinoma and cholangiocarcinoma, as early as 10 months of age**: screen with tumour markers (including alpha-fetoprotein) and imaging. [S1]
- **Monitoring schedule (S3):** liver tests, fat-soluble vitamins, serum bile acids and growth every 3–6 months (an arbitrary interval), with itch and quality-of-life questionnaires; liver ultrasound and liver stiffness every 6–12 months; with cirrhosis, ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein every 6 months for HCC surveillance. [S3]
- **Bile acids track outcome:** children with PFIC1 whose serum bile acids stayed below 194 μmol/L were three times as likely to keep their own liver to 15 years as those above that level. [S3]

## Surgery and transplant

- For pruritus refractory to medicines, surgical biliary diversion can be considered. After partial external biliary diversion one study reported improved pruritus in 81% with PFIC1 and 76% with PFIC2; no non-transplant operation has been shown superior to another. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Hepatoportoenterostomy (Kasai) is not indicated in PFIC** and can increase liver injury. [S1]
- Liver transplant is considered for end-stage liver disease, HCC or refractory pruritus. With severe FIC1 or BSEP deficiency only 50% survive with their native liver at 10 years. [S1]
- ⚠️ **In PFIC1 without cirrhosis, strongly consider non-transplant surgery first**, because transplant can bring pancreatitis, allograft steatosis and diarrhoea. In PFIC2, anti-BSEP antibodies can cause recurrent disease after transplant. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Biliary diversion (S2) is reported in PFIC types 1 and 2 but should not be done with cirrhosis, advanced fibrosis, HCC, or PFIC2 with no residual protein activity.** [S2]
- **Transplant (S2)** is for intractable itch, HCC or end-stage disease. Graft recurrence can occur in PFIC2, diarrhoea can worsen in PFIC1, and severe steatosis can follow in types 1 and 5; PFIC1 with diarrhoea may itself contraindicate transplant. [S2]
