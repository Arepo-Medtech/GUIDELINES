## Recognise

- **Australia has the world's highest incidence** (Queensland age-adjusted rate 1.6 per 100000). Here about 80% of cases are UV-driven and 20% polyomavirus-driven, the reverse of Europe and North America. [S1]
- **Risk factors:** older age (median 76), male sex, pale skin, UV exposure and immunosuppression, which accounts for about 10% of patients (transplant recipients, haematological cancer, HIV). [S1]
- **Typical lesion:** a fast-growing, painless, firm pink to violet dome or plaque of 1–2 cm on sun-exposed skin, most often head and neck. It is easily mistaken for BCC, SCC, amelanotic melanoma or benign lesions; dermatoscopy helps. [S1]
- **Examine the regional lymph nodes and assess comorbidity and life expectancy;** confirm the diagnosis histologically with H&E and the dedicated immunostain panel. [S1]

## Stage

- **Imaging is mandatory:** CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis (plus head and neck for primaries there), with whole-body FDG-PET/CT preferred where available. [S1]
- **Do sentinel node biopsy with the primary excision** to detect occult nodal disease; it is less reliable in the immunosuppressed and in head, neck or midline-trunk tumours. [S1]
- **Every decision belongs to a multidisciplinary team with real Merkel cell experience,** ideally with trial access. [S1]

## Local and locoregional disease (stage I–III)

- **Wide local excision with 1–2 cm margins;** if that is not achievable, 0.5–1.0 cm margins plus adjuvant radiotherapy may do. Definitive radiotherapy is the alternative when excision is not feasible. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Adjuvant radiotherapy of 50–60 Gy to the tumour bed** for tumours 1 cm or larger or with adverse features (stage IB or higher). Observation alone in stage IA is a referral-centre decision. [S1]
- **Positive sentinel node:** adjuvant nodal radiotherapy, with or without completion dissection, after MDT review. Clinically involved nodes or extranodal spread: node dissection plus postoperative radiotherapy (or definitive radiotherapy if inoperable). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not give adjuvant chemotherapy;** offer trials of (neo)adjuvant immunotherapy instead. [S1]

## Advanced and metastatic disease

- ⚠️ **First line for inoperable or disseminated disease is a PD-1 or PD-L1 antibody** rather than chemotherapy, being more effective and safer. Avelumab, pembrolizumab, retifanlimab and nivolumab are all recommended in the first or second line unless contraindicated. [S1]
- **Avelumab (JAVELIN Merkel trial):** in untreated patients the response rate was 39.7% with median survival 20.3 months; outcomes are best when it is used first line, and immunosuppressed patients benefit too. [S1]
- **No biomarker predicts benefit** (PD-L1, viral status, tumour mutational burden and CD8 infiltration all failed), and the best duration of immunotherapy is unknown. [S1]
- **Oligometastatic progression:** consider surgery or stereotactic radiotherapy in fit patients, but start with systemic immunotherapy. If immunotherapy is contraindicated or fails, palliative radiotherapy or chemotherapy are options of uncertain survival benefit. [S1]

## Older patients

- **Use geriatric and comorbidity assessment to guide treatment;** radiotherapy alone suits those unfit for surgery, and checkpoint inhibitors are the preferred systemic therapy in older patients with inoperable disease. [S1]

## Follow-up

- **Relapse risk peaks in the first 2–3 years.** Review every 3–6 months for 3 years, 6-monthly to year 5, then yearly for life with a full skin check; cross-sectional imaging may be offered to higher-risk patients. [S1]
- **Teach whole-skin self-examination:** these patients are at higher risk of further skin cancers. MCPyV oncoprotein antibody tracking is of uncertain value but may be used if seropositive at baseline. [S1]
