# Juvenile idiopathic arthritis

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** children and young people with JIA, specialist-led care: methotrexate, JIA-associated uveitis after methotrexate, glucocorticoids (systemic and intra-articular), tapering DMARDs, foot orthoses and exercise. The living guideline answers selected questions only; it does **not** cover first biologic choice for polyarticular JIA, IL-1/IL-6 treatment of systemic JIA, or enthesitis-related arthritis. Systemic JIA (Still's disease) diagnosis, treatment and macrophage activation syndrome come from S2 (EULAR/PReS 2024). PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ⚠️ International guideline (EULAR/PReS, 2024) for S2. Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ: **tocilizumab is the only PBS-listed drug for systemic JIA**; anakinra and canakinumab, which S2 names, are not in that listing.

## Methotrexate

- **Methotrexate is the first DMARD to try** when a systemic DMARD is needed: 15 mg/m² once weekly, capped at 25 mg a week. Prefer it to leflunomide unless methotrexate is contraindicated. [S1]
- **Treat to target, with decisions shared with the family,** including the decision to step up to methotrexate. [S1]
- **Blood monitoring:** full blood count, kidney and liver tests before starting, again one to two months in, then every three to four months. [S1]
- **Give folic acid with methotrexate** for tolerability. Common schedules: 1 mg daily except on methotrexate day, or 5 mg on the day before and/or after it. [S1]
- **Oral methotrexate is usually fine to start.** Switch to (or start) the subcutaneous route for extensive disease, or when oral intolerance has occurred or is likely; if parenteral, inject under the skin rather than into muscle. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Review and adjust early (3 monthly)** if methotrexate fails or is not tolerated, especially with a polyarticular course or disease in the wrist, ankle or cervical spine. [S1]

## JIA-associated uveitis

- ⚠️ **Uveitis not controlled by methotrexate: add a b/tsDMARD, preferably adalimumab.** Do not use etanercept. [S1]
- **Other TNF antibodies such as infliximab, non-TNF biologics or JAK-type drugs remain options** without trial support. Treatment belongs with paediatric rheumatology and ophthalmology teams experienced in this uveitis. [S1]

## Glucocorticoids

- **When starting a DMARD in non-systemic JIA, a short steroid course can help** those most likely to benefit, such as children with marked pain or widespread inflammation. Agree how it will be tapered and stopped at the outset. [S1]
- **Prefer joint injection to systemic steroid** where possible. In non-systemic JIA give systemic steroid only alongside a new DMARD, at the lowest dose for the shortest time, with a written taper. [S1]
- **Severe disease:** an IV methylprednisolone pulse, up to 30 mg/kg (maximum 1,000 mg) daily for up to three days (sometimes five), can precede oral prednisolone. [S1]
- **Watch for steroid harms:** weight gain, mood and sleep disturbance, and infection. [S1]
- **For joint injections use triamcinolone hexacetonide** in preference to other steroids, and agree with the child and carer on topical and/or injected local anaesthetic. [S1]

## Reducing or stopping DMARDs

- **Consider reducing or stopping a conventional DMARD** after a sustained spell of low or no disease activity, when its harms outweigh the risk of a flare. [S1]
- **Consider reducing or stopping a b/tsDMARD** only after sustained inactive disease, on the same harm-versus-flare judgement; take extra care after uveitis. [S1]
- **In systemic JIA, stopping a biologic** may mean fewer children stay inactive and more have joint flares; the effect on systemic flares is uncertain. [S1]

## Timing and follow-up after tapering

- **Wait at least 12 months of stable low activity** before tapering; 18 to 24 months may be better. Stopping immunosuppression after uveitis may be considered once the eye has been quiet for 24 months. [S1]
- **After tapering, review at least every 3 months with a validated composite score,** give the family a flare plan and a way to reach the team, and restart the previous regimen if control is lost. [S1]

## Non-drug care

- **Foot orthoses are not routinely recommended.** Offer an individually tailored exercise programme when symptoms or functional limits persist despite optimal medical treatment. [S1]

## Systemic JIA (Still's disease): recognise (S2, EULAR/PReS 2024)

- **Systemic JIA and adult-onset Still's disease are one disease, now called Still's disease.** Clues: spiking fever of 39°C or more lasting at least a week; a fleeting salmon-pink rash, mostly on the trunk, that comes with the fever; joint or muscle pain; and high neutrophils, ESR, CRP and ferritin. **Arthritis is not needed for the diagnosis** and may come later. [S2]
- **A marked rise in serum IL-18 and/or S100 proteins (such as calprotectin) strongly supports the diagnosis;** test if available. Carefully exclude cancer, infection, other immune-mediated disease and monogenic autoinflammatory disorders. [S2]

## Systemic JIA (Still's disease): treat (S2, EULAR/PReS 2024)

- ⚠️ **Start an IL-1 or IL-6 inhibitor as soon as the diagnosis is made** (observational data favour within 3 months of onset), whatever the severity; the aim is drug-free remission. If bacterial infection is still possible, prefer an IL-1 blocker; anakinra is often first because of its short half-life and safety. [S2]
- **Steroids: high dose (2 mg/kg/day prednisone equivalent or more in children, 1 mg/kg/day in adults) for severe disease** — high spiking fever, widespread polyarthritis, pain above 6–7/10, pericarditis or impending MAS. Milder disease may need low or intermediate doses or none. Start tapering once fever has gone and active joints have halved, and never use steroids to hold the target: if steroid-dependent, add other treatment. [S2]
- **Targets on the way to drug-free remission:** day 7, no fever and CRP down by more than 50%; week 4, no fever, active joints down by more than 50%, normal CRP and global assessments under 20 out of 100; month 3, inactive disease on under 0.2 mg/kg/day steroid in children (0.1 in adults); month 6, inactive disease off steroids. Remission means at least 6 months of inactive disease. [S2]
- **Missing the 3-month or 6-month target: rotate to the other IL-1 or IL-6 inhibitor,** continuing a slow steroid taper. Still not off steroids after rotation means difficult-to-treat disease: discuss with an expert centre (options include JAK inhibitors, stem cell transplant and immunosuppressants). [S2]
- **Serious adverse events and infections are more frequent with IL-6 than IL-1 inhibition; anakinra looks safest.** Methotrexate was no better than placebo in the one sJIA trial, so conventional DMARDs are for places without IL-1/IL-6 inhibitors, and NSAIDs only for fever and joint pain during the work-up. [S2]
- **Tapering the biologic:** only after steroids are off and inactive disease has held at least 3 months (6 for severe or difficult disease; the algorithm says at least 6), in steps every 3–6 months, preferably by spacing injections rather than stopping abruptly. [S2]

## Macrophage activation syndrome and lung disease (S2, EULAR/PReS 2024)

- ⚠️ **Suspect macrophage activation syndrome (MAS)** with persistent fever, splenomegaly, high or rising ferritin, inappropriately low blood counts, abnormal liver tests, activated coagulation or high or rising triglycerides; watch the trend, not single values. It can occur at onset, in remission, and on IL-1 or IL-6 inhibitors, where canakinumab or tocilizumab can blunt or delay the fever and ferritin rise. [S2]
- **Check ferritin routinely in everyone with Still's disease,** and daily or even twice daily when MAS is suspected or the child is at risk. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Treat MAS with high-dose steroids: IV methylprednisolone 15–30 mg/kg/day (maximum 1 g per infusion),** or dexamethasone if the brain is involved. Add anakinra (above the standard 1–2 mg/kg/day; over 4 mg/kg/day in children), ciclosporin and/or an IFN-γ inhibitor (emapalumab) early if the response is poor or MAS is severe. Look for and treat an infective trigger, without delaying MAS treatment. [S2]
- **Screen for Still's lung disease:** ask about clubbing, persistent cough and breathlessness, check pulse oximetry and DLCO, and do a high-resolution CT if symptomatic. Lung disease or its risk factors do not rule out IL-1 or IL-6 inhibitors; manage it, severe MAS and difficult-to-treat disease with an expert centre. [S2]
