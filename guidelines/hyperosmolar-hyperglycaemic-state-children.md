# Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state in children

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosis, fluid resuscitation, electrolytes, delayed insulin and complications of HHS in children and adolescents. Distinguish from `diabetic-ketoacidosis-children`, whose management differs. No adult HHS guideline is in this corpus; adult type 2 disease is in `type-2-diabetes`.

> ✅ **PARAPHRASED, HASH-ANCHORED.** 19 of 19 claims paraphrased from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state*** (Last updated July 2024); **27 anchors re-checkable by machine; 5 doses.** The RCH text is not reproduced: its terms licence personal use only.

> ⚠️ **HHS is not DKA.** Fluids first; **insulin only after fluid resuscitation**, when glucose is falling under 2–3 mmol/L/hour on fluids alone. **No subcutaneous insulin and no insulin bolus.** Discuss every case with a senior clinician, ICU or retrieval.

---

## Recognise it

- **HHS = serum hyperosmolality, hyperglycaemia and dehydration without ketoacidosis** — a medical emergency with **higher morbidity and mortality than DKA**. [S1]
- **Biochemical criteria:** glucose over 33.3 mmol/L · venous pH over 7.25 (arterial over 7.30) or bicarbonate over 15 mmol/L · ketonuria negative or small, ketonaemia absent to mild · serum osmolality over 320 mOsm/kg. [S1]
- **Serum osmolality = 2 × sodium + glucose + urea (mmol/L).** [S1]
- **Versus DKA:** onset over days to weeks (DKA hours to days); ketones under 3 mmol/L (DKA over 3); dehydration severe and often underestimated. **Kussmaul breathing points to DKA.** [S1]
- **Risk factors:** obesity, undiagnosed or uncontrolled (especially type 2) diabetes, acute illness or substance use, very high carbohydrate intake, and drugs such as corticosteroids, atypical antipsychotics, phenytoin and beta-blockers. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Dehydration tends to be severe yet under-recognised** — because hyperosmolality maintains intravascular volume, the signs are masked. **Assume at least a 12–15% deficit.** [S1]

## Where and what to test

- ⚠️ **Urgent senior, ICU or retrieval consultation for every child with HHS; manage in paediatric HDU or ICU where possible.** [S1]
- **Bloods:** glucose, venous gas, UEC with calcium, magnesium and phosphate, FBC, bedside ketones, blood cultures if febrile; urinary ketones. **Consider an ECG if potassium will be delayed — hypokalaemia is common.** [S1]

## Fluids

- ⚠️ **Shocked: 0.9% sodium chloride 10–20 mL/kg IV bolus**, repeated if needed to restore perfusion, with senior/ICU/retrieval discussion. [S1]
- **Next, replace the estimated deficit (typically 12–15%) plus full maintenance across 24–48 hours.** Hypotonic maintenance (e.g. 0.45% sodium chloride) is likely needed to reduce osmolality — **only under ICU/retrieval guidance**; use 0.9% if perfusion is poor, the child is unstable, or hypotonic fluid is unavailable. [S1]
- **Replace urine output, with 0.45% sodium chloride where possible** (urine will be hypotonic). [S1]

## Electrolytes

- **Total-body potassium is depleted: aim for serum potassium of 4–5 mmol/L.** ⚠️ **Withhold potassium replacement if the child is anuric or potassium exceeds 5.5 mmol/L.** Once insulin starts, check potassium within 1 hour and 2–4-hourly; ECG and cardiac monitoring. [S1]
- **Corrected sodium = measured sodium + 0.3 × (glucose − 5.5) mmol/L.** Sodium may rise at first; **aim for a fall of 0.5 mmol/L/hour.** ⚠️ Sodium failing to rise as glucose falls, or new hyponatraemia, suggests over-correction and **raises cerebral oedema risk**. [S1]
- **Phosphate:** severe hypophosphataemia raises rhabdomyolysis risk — consider potassium-containing phosphate preparations, monitoring potassium hourly. **Magnesium:** replace only in severe cases. [S1]

## Insulin

- ⚠️ **Only give insulin after adequate fluid resuscitation.** Glucose should fall on fluids alone (initially 4–5 mmol/hour); a faster fall in glucose and osmolality can cause circulatory collapse. [S1]
- **Start an IV insulin infusion when glucose falls less than 2–3 mmol/L/hour on fluids alone.** The rate is **lower than in DKA.** ⚠️ **No subcutaneous insulin and no insulin bolus.** [S1]
- **If glucose fails to fall as expected, reassess renal function.** After recovery, many children with type 2 diabetes need insulin only temporarily. [S1]

## Complications and escalation

- ⚠️ **Watch for** venous thrombosis (especially with central lines), rhabdomyolysis, malignant hyperthermia, compartment syndrome, **cerebral oedema from rapid correction**, and ventricular arrhythmias. [S1]
- **Manage in a unit with frequent, timely laboratory access and paediatric HDU/ICU.** [S1]
