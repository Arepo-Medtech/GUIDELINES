# Altered conscious state in children

## Recognise it

- **First priority: limit further neurological damage**, while investigations and treatment get under way. [S1]
- **Parents judge an altered conscious state best**, especially when the child has a disability. Take parental and nursing concerns seriously. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider child abuse** when a young child has signs of injury along with an altered conscious state. [S1]
- **Altered conscious state is defined by a GCS below 15.** It is uncommon but carries significant mortality and morbidity; **in children, coma is most often due to head injury or infection.** [S1]
- **Screen with AVPU** (Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive), particularly in younger children. **Any impairment on AVPU → formal GCS.** [S1]

## Causes to consider

- **Trauma and infection:** head injury, falls, concussion; sepsis, meningitis, encephalitis, malaria, cerebral and extracerebral abscesses. [S1]
- **Neurological:** post-ictal phase or status epilepticus; raised intracranial pressure (mass, cerebral oedema, **blocked VP shunt**); cerebral hypoxia or ischaemia from respiratory failure, shock, asphyxiation or stroke/intracranial haemorrhage. [S1]
- **Poisoning and metabolic:** alcohol, hallucinogens, opiates, lead; **hypoglycaemia**, DKA, renal or hepatic failure, hypo/hypernatraemia, hypothermia, inborn errors of metabolism. Also acute confusional migraine, psychiatric causes and arrhythmia. [S1]

## Initial assessment

- ⚠️ **Attend to ABCDE first, regardless of cause.** If trauma is possible, **immobilise the cervical spine and consult neurosurgery.** [S1]
- **Then identify a cause as quickly as possible.** History: headache, confusion, seizures, focal neurology, abrupt vs gradual course or recurrence, injury, fever, past history including immunisation and development, family history including consanguinity, drug/toxin exposure (quantity, timing) and travel. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Raised intracranial pressure — signs:** pupillary dilatation (one or both sides) · **GCS drop of more than 2 points** · focal neurology · decorticate or decerebrate posturing · Cushing reflex of bradycardia, hypertension and irregular respirations (**a late sign**). [S1]
- **Examination clues:** rash, petechiae, bruising, needle marks; the smell of alcohol, glue, ketones, petrol or phenol; scalp haematoma, bloody or clear ear/nasal discharge, otitis media, mastoiditis, neck bruising; hepatomegaly or abdominal guarding. [S1]

## Investigations

- **Guided by the differential:** point-of-care glucose; FBE, renal function, glucose, liver function; blood gas, ammonia, cortisol; coagulation; urine toxicology; blood and urine culture. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not perform a lumbar puncture in a child with a reduced GCS.** If needed, do it once the child is clinically stable. [S1]
- **CT or MRI only after initial resuscitation and treatment**, where feasible, for suspected trauma, stroke or raised intracranial pressure. [S1]
- **ECG:** a broad QRS suggests critical tricyclic antidepressant toxicity. [S1]

## Treat

- **Definitive management follows the working diagnosis** (head injury, trauma, meningitis/encephalitis, sepsis, child abuse, poisoning). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Suspected infection: give antibiotic and antiviral therapy urgently.** [S1]
- **Treat signs of raised intracranial pressure.** For poisoning, 24-hour advice: **Poisons Information Centre 13 11 26.** [S1]

## Consult, transfer, discharge

- **Consult the local paediatric team** for any persistent or unexplained altered conscious state, or any concern for child abuse. [S1]
- **Consider transfer** when the child is at risk of deteriorating and needs management beyond local capability. [S1]
- **Consider discharge** only when conscious state has returned to normal and there is a clear follow-up plan if required. [S1]
