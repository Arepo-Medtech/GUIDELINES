## Who is at risk

- **CMV retinitis is the commonest eye manifestation of CMV in immunocompromised people,** affecting about 20–40% of people with HIV. HTLV-1, common among Aboriginal adults in Australia, also predisposes to it. [S1]
- **In HIV, a CD4 count below 50 cells/µL should prompt early assessment;** CD4 can also guide screening. Non-HIV patients (transplant, autoimmune disease, lymphoma or chemotherapy) are a growing at-risk group. [S1]

## Recognise

- **Early retinitis may be symptom-free; symptoms are non-specific** (floaters, flashes, blurred vision, blind spots). Lesions start peripherally and advance toward the posterior pole; untreated, retinitis causes retinal detachment and irreversible vision loss. [S1]
- **Typical fundus appearances:** wedge-shaped, haemorrhagic or granular retinitis with little or no vitritis; central necrosis with haemorrhage gives the 'pizza pie' look. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Zone 1 (within 1500 µm of the optic disc or 3000 µm of the fovea) threatens central vision:** involvement of the macula or optic nerve causes major visual loss. [S1]

## Diagnose

- **The diagnosis is made on fundus examination,** supported by aqueous humour PCR for active CMV replication. Blood or urine CMV tests can help, but a negative result does not exclude retinitis. [S1]
- **Exclude the mimics:** progressive outer retinal necrosis, acute retinal necrosis, syphilitic retinitis, tuberculosis and toxoplasmosis. [S1]
- **Telling the mimics apart (S1 Table 1):** CMV gives superficial, granular yellow-to-white lesions, usually with haemorrhage; PORN gives multiple deep, sharply demarcated white lesions with optic nerve involvement and little vitreous infiltration; ARN gives massive whitish oedema with marked vitreous and anterior chamber infiltration; syphilis gives yellow-to-orange lesions with creamy superficial precipitates or placoid lesions and an Argyll Robertson pupil; tuberculosis gives grey-white lesions with focal vasculitis, vein occlusion and periphlebitis; toxoplasmosis gives retinochoroiditis with a pigmented scar and adjacent vitritis. [S1]
- **Blood CMV antigenaemia and PCR can predict CMV disease months before it shows;** an antigen level below 45 strongly suggests there is no retinitis (negative predictive value 98.2%). CMV in the urine on its own does not justify antiviral prophylaxis. [S2]

## Treat

- **First-line antivirals are ganciclovir, valganciclovir, cidofovir and foscarnet,** given systemically (IV or oral) and/or locally (intravitreal injection or implant). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Intravenous ganciclovir 5 mg/kg over 1 hour every 12 hours for 7 to 14 days, then 5 mg/kg once daily 7 days a week (or 6 mg/kg once daily 5 days a week).** Watch for myelosuppression and liver toxicity. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Oral valganciclovir 900 mg twice daily for induction is as effective and safe as IV ganciclovir** and easier to take; it can cause neutropenia, anaemia and thrombocytopenia. [S1]
- **Zone 1 disease: consider intravitreal ganciclovir injections or a slow-release ganciclovir implant**; intravitreal therapy carries risks of vitreous haemorrhage and retinal detachment. [S1]
- **Ganciclovir-resistant or intolerant disease:** foscarnet 60 mg/kg IV every 12 hours for 14 days (nephrotoxic; monitor renal function and electrolytes), or cidofovir 5 mg/kg IV weekly for 2 weeks then every 2 weeks (proteinuria, renal failure, neutropenia). [S1]
- **Induction usually runs 14 to 21 days, its final length set by clinical response, and is followed by continuous maintenance** until CD4 rises on effective ART and/or the retinitis stops progressing. S1 gives a shorter IV ganciclovir induction (see Unresolved). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Where the lesions are decides the route:** immediately sight-threatening lesions get intravitreal injections together with systemic therapy; for lesions that do not threaten sight straight away, systemic therapy alone with close observation is reasonable. [S2]
- **No systemic agent has been shown superior to another;** the choice rests on whether oral medicines are tolerated, comorbidities and other medicines, and expected adherence. Aciclovir is not used, because CMV cannot activate it. [S2]
- **Intravitreal therapy on its own is not usually recommended unless systemic therapy is contraindicated:** in HIV, intravitreal-only regimens had more progression than systemic treatment. In non-HIV immunocompromised patients (for example after transplant, where systemic drugs worsen myelosuppression), weekly intravitreal ganciclovir for about 6 weeks has cleared retinitis. [S2]
- **Cidofovir is not first line:** it is nephrotoxic, needing IV saline pre-hydration and probenecid with every infusion, and it can cause anterior uveitis and hypotony. [S2]
- **Recalcitrant or relapsed retinitis: combination therapy is recommended;** ganciclovir plus foscarnet gave longer survival than either drug alone, with no extra toxicity. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Resistance builds with prolonged ganciclovir or valganciclovir:** in one AIDS study up to 20% were resistant to every anti-CMV drug by 9 to 12 months. UL97 mutations cause ganciclovir resistance first; UL54 mutations then add cidofovir and foscarnet resistance. Genotype testing by PCR is possible when CMV is detectable in blood or eye fluid. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Maribavir penetrates the retina poorly** (vitreous-to-plasma ratio up to 0.28), so it is a poor choice for retinitis despite its activity against resistant CMV. [S1]
- **Drug trade-offs (S1 Table 2):** foscarnet (IV or intravitreal) causes less bone marrow suppression and can be combined with ganciclovir, but brings kidney impairment, anaemia and electrolyte disturbance, and intravitreally vitreous haemorrhage and retinal detachment; cidofovir is the least expensive but causes proteinuria, renal failure, neutropenia, uveitis and ocular hypotony. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Letermovir is approved only for prophylaxis** and is less effective at high viral loads (S1 Table 2), though it is well tolerated and can be combined with ganciclovir. [S1]
- **CMV-specific adoptive T-cell therapy (an option in drug resistance) and CMV immunoglobulin, both IV, aim to restore immunity** and are well tolerated, but donor selection is complex and both are costly; immunoglobulin can cause infusion reactions (S1 Table 2). [S1]
- **Antiretroviral therapy has greatly reduced CMV retinitis in HIV,** but people with pre-existing retinitis who start it can develop immune recovery uveitis, a common cause of new vision loss. [S1]
- **Immune recovery uveitis can take vision through macular oedema, new retinal vessels and cataract without active CMV;** its risk factors are immune reconstitution on ART, more extensive CMV lesions and cidofovir. [S2]
- **Retinal detachment from CMV retinitis is repaired by vitrectomy** (silicone oil repair succeeds in 56–94% of HIV patients). [S1]

## Monitor

- **During treatment monitor renal function, full blood count, liver function, CD4 count and viral load,** and arrange regular ophthalmology reviews to track the retinitis. A final CD4 above 50 cells/mm³ is associated with better survival in AIDS. [S1]

## Stopping maintenance

- ⚠️ **In HIV, maintenance can stop once CD4 stays above 100 cells/µL after a rise of at least 50 cells/µL, with 3–6 relapse-free months of inactive, scarred retinitis** (US guideline criteria as summarised in S2). Keep up dilated eye examinations, because ART failure can let the retinitis reactivate. [S2]
- **Non-HIV patients relapse too:** among 20 non-HIV immunosuppressed patients followed for 17 months, 33.3% recurred after stopping anti-CMV therapy, so routine eye monitoring is advised. [S2]
