# Wilson disease: diagnosis, chelation, zinc and monitoring

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** when to suspect Wilson disease, the diagnostic work-up (ceruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper, Kayser–Fleischer rings, ATP7B genetics), acute liver failure, initial chelation (D-penicillamine or trientine), zinc, diet, pregnancy, treatment monitoring, overtreatment and maintenance. Liver transplantation is only mentioned. Other chelation: `iron-overload-and-chelation`. Acute liver failure and cirrhosis care generally: `hepatic-encephalopathy`.

The full AASLD guidance and the 2025 EASL-ERN guideline are closed access and were not read. Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **S2 is a Chinese multidisciplinary consensus (Current Neuropharmacology, 2025).** It supplies the doses and urine-copper targets below; it also covers drugs not used in Australia (DMPS, DMSA, Gandou tablet), which are not carried here. Where S1 and S2 differ, both are shown.

> ⚠️ **Australian funding differs.** The PBS row `Chelation of elevated copper levels` lists **trientine** only. This guidance also relies on D-penicillamine and zinc, which are not on that row.

## Suspect

- **Consider Wilson disease** with liver abnormalities of unclear cause at any age, liver disease with a neurological or psychiatric disorder, acute liver failure with non-immune haemolysis, or recurrent self-limiting non-immune haemolysis. [S1]
- ⚠️ **About 3%–5% present in acute liver failure,** with jaundice, Coombs-negative haemolysis, coagulopathy, encephalopathy, an ALP:bilirubin ratio below 1:4 and AST:ALT above 2.2; without a transplant, death is near-certain. [S1]
- **Kayser–Fleischer rings are seen in 90% of patients with neurological disease** but missing in about 60% of those with liver disease; neurological features typically appear around age 20. [S1]
- **Exclude Wilson disease in children with apparent autoimmune hepatitis** and in anyone whose autoimmune hepatitis responds poorly to steroids. [S1]

## Diagnose

- **Work-up:** history (including family) and examination for liver, neurological and psychiatric disease; liver tests, FBC and INR, serum ceruloplasmin, basal 24-hour urine copper, slit-lamp or optical tomography for KF rings, neurological assessment and ATP7B testing. [S1]
- ⚠️ **No single test is diagnostic.** Ceruloplasmin is usually <14 mg/dL (normal >20) but a normal value does not rule Wilson disease out, and a low one does not confirm it. [S1]
- **Basal 24-hour urine copper is >100 μg (>1.6 μmol) in symptomatic disease;** >40 μg/ 24 hours (>0.6 μmol) may point to it in children or people without symptoms and needs further work-up. [S1]
- **ATP7B genetic testing** confirms the diagnosis when biochemistry is equivocal and is an efficient way to screen a proband's first-degree family; the Leipzig score helps in atypical cases. [S1]
- **A Leipzig score of 4 or more confirms Wilson disease** and treatment can begin. The score is built stepwise from clinical features, biochemistry, genetics and liver biopsy. [S2]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **Every newly diagnosed patient starts lifelong treatment;** stopping it leads to relapse, liver failure, transplant or neuropsychiatric deterioration. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Symptomatic patients start on a chelator;** trientine may be tolerated better than D-penicillamine, which about a third of patients stop because of side effects, including neurological worsening. [S1]
- **Trientine suits patients intolerant of D-penicillamine.** Penicillamine side effects usually settle after switching, but neurological worsening from penicillamine may persist. No head-to-head trial has compared the two for initial therapy. [S1]
- **Asymptomatic patients all need treatment:** a lower-dose chelator or zinc, which AASLD now recommends first line; zinc is also an option for predominantly neurological disease and is useful in renal impairment. [S1]
- **Hepatic treatment failure was commoner on zinc than on chelators** in one retrospective series (14/88 vs 4/313 treatments), and responded when a chelator was restarted. [S1]
- **Diet:** a dietitian-designed plan keeping copper intake below 0.9 mg/day, alongside, not instead of, drug treatment. [S1]
- **Pregnancy:** anti-copper treatment is generally safe and should continue; treated women miscarry less than undiagnosed women. Discourage conception after decompensated cirrhosis. [S1]
- **D-penicillamine dosing (S2):** adults start at 125–250 mg/day and rise by 250 mg/day every 4 to 7 days to a maximum of 1,000–1,500 mg/day; maintenance is 750–1,000 mg/day (10–15 mg/kg/day) in 2–4 divided doses. Children build up to 20 mg/kg/day (maximum 750–1,000 mg/day), maintenance 10–20 mg/kg/day. Give it 1 hour before or two hours after food, with vitamin B6 10–30 mg/day. S2 requires a negative penicillin skin test first. [S2]
- ⚠️ **D-penicillamine reactions:** about 30% of patients stop it. Fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, proteinuria, neutropenia or thrombocytopenia usually appear 1–3 weeks in and mean stopping it at once; neurological worsening occurs in 10–50% after starting. Nephrotoxicity (proteinuria, haematuria, acute kidney failure) also means immediate discontinuation. [S2]
- **Trientine** suits every form of Wilson disease, especially neuropsychiatric disease and penicillamine allergy or intolerance. Avoid iron supplements on trientine (copper–iron chelates are toxic). Its adverse effects, once thought rare, now look comparable to penicillamine's: about 26% have them during initial treatment. [S2]
- **Zinc dosing (S2):** adults and older children 150–220 mg/day of elemental zinc in 3 divided doses; children aged 5–15 years under 50 kg 75 mg/day in 3 doses; under 5 years 50 mg/day in 2 doses. Take on an empty stomach, at least 2 hours apart from a chelator. Do not use zinc alone for a severe acute presentation. [S2]
- **Pregnancy (S2):** continue anti-copper therapy but **reduce the chelator dose**; some patients can be managed on zinc alone. [S2]
- **Liver transplant:** evaluate patients with acute liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis who fail or cannot tolerate anti-copper therapy. S2 bridges acute liver failure with plasma exchange plus a chelator before transplant. [S2]

## Monitor and maintain

- **On treatment, check 24-hour urine copper, and twice a year** liver tests, INR, FBC, urinalysis (especially on a chelator), serum copper and ceruloplasmin, signs of overtreatment and of non-adherence. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Overtreatment** shows as cytopenias, rising ferritin or a very low 24-hour urine copper (<100 μg/24 h on a chelator; <20 μg/24 h on zinc); manage by lowering the dose or briefly pausing. [S1]
- **Move to maintenance after, generally, more than 1 y of good response:** a lower chelator dose or full-dose zinc, with closer checks in the first 2–6 months and lifelong follow-up for adherence and late failure. [S1]
- **Expected response (S2):** liver disease usually improves markedly within 2-6 months and can fully remit by 1 year; neurological disease improves slowly, often over 1 to 3 years. Review once or twice a month at first, every 1 to 3 months once liver tests improve, and 2 to 3 times a year on maintenance. [S2]
- **Urine copper on a chelator (S2):** peaks about 1 month in (up to 1,500-8,000 μg/24 h), then falls; most reach the maintenance range of 200-500 μg/24 h after 6 months to 1 year. More than 100 μg/24 h after 48 h off penicillamine suggests poor adherence. [S2]
- **Urine copper on zinc (S2):** maintenance target below 75 μg/24 h. Urinary zinc is usually 2,000-8,000 μg/24 h on treatment; urine copper above 100 μg/24 h with urine zinc below 1,000 μg/24 h suggests poor adherence. If transaminases rise on zinc, add a chelator promptly. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Lower chelator doses for maintenance** to avoid overtreatment: iatrogenic copper deficiency can cause sideroblastic anaemia and myelopathy, and penicillamine skin effects are dose-dependent. [S2]
