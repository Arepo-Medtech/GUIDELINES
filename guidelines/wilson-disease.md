# Wilson disease: diagnosis, chelation, zinc and monitoring

## Suspect

- **Consider Wilson disease** with liver abnormalities of unclear cause at any age, liver disease with a neurological or psychiatric disorder, acute liver failure with non-immune haemolysis, or recurrent self-limiting non-immune haemolysis. [S1]
- ⚠️ **About 3%–5% present in acute liver failure,** with jaundice, Coombs-negative haemolysis, coagulopathy, encephalopathy, an ALP:bilirubin ratio below 1:4 and AST:ALT above 2.2; without a transplant, death is near-certain. [S1]
- **Kayser–Fleischer rings are seen in 90% of patients with neurological disease** but missing in about 60% of those with liver disease; neurological features typically appear around age 20. [S1]
- **Exclude Wilson disease in children with apparent autoimmune hepatitis** and in anyone whose autoimmune hepatitis responds poorly to steroids. [S1]

## Diagnose

- **Work-up:** history (including family) and examination for liver, neurological and psychiatric disease; liver tests, FBC and INR, serum ceruloplasmin, basal 24-hour urine copper, slit-lamp or optical tomography for KF rings, neurological assessment and ATP7B testing. [S1]
- ⚠️ **No single test is diagnostic.** Ceruloplasmin is usually <14 mg/dL (normal >20) but a normal value does not rule Wilson disease out, and a low one does not confirm it. [S1]
- **Basal 24-hour urine copper is >100 μg (>1.6 μmol) in symptomatic disease;** >40 μg/ 24 hours (>0.6 μmol) may point to it in children or people without symptoms and needs further work-up. [S1]
- **ATP7B genetic testing** confirms the diagnosis when biochemistry is equivocal and is an efficient way to screen a proband's first-degree family; the Leipzig score helps in atypical cases. [S1]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **Every newly diagnosed patient starts lifelong treatment;** stopping it leads to relapse, liver failure, transplant or neuropsychiatric deterioration. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Symptomatic patients start on a chelator;** trientine may be tolerated better than D-penicillamine, which about a third of patients stop because of side effects, including neurological worsening. [S1]
- **Trientine suits patients intolerant of D-penicillamine.** Penicillamine side effects usually settle after switching, but neurological worsening from penicillamine may persist. No head-to-head trial has compared the two for initial therapy. [S1]
- **Asymptomatic patients all need treatment:** a lower-dose chelator or zinc, which AASLD now recommends first line; zinc is also an option for predominantly neurological disease and is useful in renal impairment. [S1]
- **Hepatic treatment failure was commoner on zinc than on chelators** in one retrospective series (14/88 vs 4/313 treatments), and responded when a chelator was restarted. [S1]
- **Diet:** a dietitian-designed plan keeping copper intake below 0.9 mg/day, alongside, not instead of, drug treatment. [S1]
- **Pregnancy:** anti-copper treatment is generally safe and should continue; treated women miscarry less than undiagnosed women. Discourage conception after decompensated cirrhosis. [S1]

## Monitor and maintain

- **On treatment, check 24-hour urine copper, and twice a year** liver tests, INR, FBC, urinalysis (especially on a chelator), serum copper and ceruloplasmin, signs of overtreatment and of non-adherence. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Overtreatment** shows as cytopenias, rising ferritin or a very low 24-hour urine copper (<100 μg/24 h on a chelator; <20 μg/24 h on zinc); manage by lowering the dose or briefly pausing. [S1]
- **Move to maintenance after, generally, more than 1 y of good response:** a lower chelator dose or full-dose zinc, with closer checks in the first 2–6 months and lifelong follow-up for adherence and late failure. [S1]
