# Urinary tract infection in children

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosis, urine collection, empiric antibiotics and imaging for UTI in infants and children. The adult counterpart is `uti-acute-cystitis-women`.

> ⚠️ **Infants under 3 months and any seriously unwell child: admit for IV antibiotics.** Everyone else with an uncomplicated UTI: usually oral.

---

## Recognise it

- **In infants and young children the symptoms are frequently non-specific**: fever · poor feeding · vomiting · lethargy · irritability · jaundice · faltering growth. Older children present more typically: dysuria, frequency, new daytime accidents, haematuria, abdominal or loin pain. [S1]
- **Consider UTI in any child whose fever without a focus persists beyond 48 hours.** [S1]
- **Cystitis** suggests frequency, urgency, dysuria and lower abdominal discomfort; **pyelonephritis** suggests fever, rigors, malaise, vomiting and loin tenderness. The distinction is hard in younger children. [S1]
- **Atypical UTI** = seriously ill · septicaemia · no response to antibiotics within 48 hours · a non-*E. coli* organism · poor urine flow · raised creatinine · abdominal or bladder mass. [S1]
- **Urine is not needed** when the fever has another clear focus and the child is not systemically unwell. [S1]

## Collect urine properly

- **Collect the sample before antibiotics** — except when a seriously unwell child needs IV therapy straight away. [S1]
- **Suprapubic aspirate is the gold standard up to 6 months.** Midstream urine for toilet-trained children; clean catch for pre-continent children who are not seriously unwell. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Bag urine is not recommended for culture** — high false-positive rates. [S1]
- **Clean the perineum with saline-soaked gauze for 10 seconds; do not use antiseptic.** [S1]

## Interpret

- **Dipstick screens; culture with pyuria confirms.** Dipstick is less reliable in neonates and young infants, mainly through false negatives. [S1]
- **Send for culture if:** under 3 months · leukocyte esterase or nitrite positive · suspected upper UTI · recurrent UTI · not responding in 24–48 hours · clinical UTI despite an unremarkable dipstick. [S1]
- **Absent nitrites do not exclude UTI**; blood and protein are not reliable markers. [S1]
- **Febrile, unwell infants under 4 weeks:** FBE, blood culture and LP, and admit for IV antibiotics. [S1]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **Admit any seriously unwell child and every infant under 3 months for initial IV antibiotics.** For other children with uncomplicated UTI, oral treatment is usually appropriate. [S1]
- **Duration: 3–5 days for cystitis; 7–10 days for pyelonephritis.** [S1]
- **Cystitis:** oral cefalexin three times daily, 20 mg/kg (up to 750 mg). [S1]
- **Pyelonephritis, oral:** cefalexin — under 12 months, 25 mg/kg (up to 1 g) four times daily; from 12 months, 45 mg/kg (up to 1.5 g) three times daily. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not delay antibiotics in a seriously unwell child** to obtain urine. **Empiric IV: gentamicin**; check levels and renal function if continuing beyond 48 hours, and consider a 3rd-generation cephalosporin if IV therapy will be prolonged. [S1]
- **Switch to oral when clinically improved or tolerating oral.** Local susceptibility patterns and prior microbiology should guide the empiric choice. [S1]

## Imaging and follow-up

- **Renal tract ultrasound:** all children with atypical UTI, and infants under 6 months with recurrent UTI or without antenatal monitoring. **Most children do not need an ultrasound for a first UTI.** [S1]
- **Routine antibiotic prophylaxis is not generally recommended.** Arrange specialist follow-up for recurrent UTI or known renal anomalies. [S1]
- **Consider an STI screen in sexually active young people.** [S1]
