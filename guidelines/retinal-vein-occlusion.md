# Retinal vein occlusion (branch and central) with macular oedema

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation

## Recognise and assess

- **Outlook depends on where the vein is blocked and how ischaemic the retina is:** distal, less ischaemic occlusions do better. Central and hemi-central occlusions behave alike, are linked with glaucoma and carry more risk of anterior-segment new vessels and neovascular glaucoma than branch occlusions, so keep eye pressure controlled. [S1]
- **The first examination and imaging should decide:** branch, hemi or central occlusion; whether there is macular oedema; how much ischaemia; and whether new vessels are present on the retina or iris. [S1]
- **Examine for:** a relative afferent pupillary defect (reflects ischaemia and predicts new vessels); fine new iris vessels on slit lamp; pressure; and gonioscopy before dilating, above all in ischaemic central occlusion, raised pressure or high risk of iris new vessels. [S1]
- **OCT is the key test for finding and measuring macular oedema, and it drives decisions to re-inject or change treatment**, though retinal thickness does not always match visual acuity. [S1]
- **Fluorescein angiography needs informed consent (death occurs in about 1 in 200,000).** Ask women of childbearing age about pregnancy and breastfeeding and use it only when truly needed. [S1]

## Systemic risk

- **Work with the GP:** RVO raises the risk of an occlusion in the other eye and of cardiovascular disease and stroke. **Under 50, consider haematology or general-medicine referral for a clotting work-up.** [S1]
- **Hypertension accounts for about 48% of RVO, high lipids 20% and diabetes 5%.** Control them, although it is unproven that doing so improves vision or complications. [S1]
- **Eye-clinic tests (RCOphth, S2):** history, blood pressure, blood glucose, full blood count and ESR; further tests and risk-factor care, such as help to stop smoking, sit best with the patient's own doctor. Diabetes is no commoner in RVO, but testing at diagnosis finds undiagnosed cases. [S2]
- **No antiphospholipid testing after an isolated RVO** without other features of antiphospholipid syndrome (S2). A thrombophilia result changes neither treatment nor outlook, and good evidence for anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs in RVO is lacking. [S2]
- **RVO under 50 is uncommon** (about 0.26% at ages 30–39 and 0.44% at 40–49). Young people with central occlusion need anti-VEGF less often, yet at least 20% end with poor vision from severe neovascular complications. [S2]

## Treat macular oedema

- ⚠️ **Intravitreal anti-VEGF is first-line for macular oedema in both branch and central occlusion.** Intravitreal steroids work but are second-line because they cause glaucoma and cataract; laser has a limited role in branch occlusion. [S1]
- **Four anti-VEGF agents are in routine use:** ranibizumab, aflibercept 2 mg and faricimab (FDA-approved) and bevacizumab (off-label, with supporting evidence). In trials, 8 mg of aflibercept given 8-weekly was noninferior to 4-weekly 2 mg, after 3–5 monthly loading doses. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not delay treatment.** In BRAVO, eyes first given sham and treated later never caught up with eyes treated from the start. [S1]
- **Faricimab:** in BALATON (branch) and COMINO (central), 6 monthly doses of faricimab 6 mg were noninferior to aflibercept 2 mg for vision at week 24; treatment then moved to treat-and-extend at intervals of up to 16 weeks. [S1]
- **Expect long treatment:** about half of branch and 56% to 75% of central occlusions still need anti-VEGF for oedema more than 5 years after starting. [S1]
- **Before every intravitreal injection, use a topical antiseptic such as povidone-iodine;** routine antibiotic drops are not advised. Serious injection harms are uncommon: endophthalmitis, cataract, retinal detachment, raised pressure. [S1]
- **Systemic safety:** anti-VEGF drugs carry a theoretical risk of arterial clots, but meta-analyses have not shown more major cardiovascular events or deaths. [S1]
- **Branch occlusion: start with anti-VEGF.** Consider steroids and/or grid laser when the response fails or falls short. [S1]
- **Steroid options:** in SCORE (central occlusion), triamcinolone 1 mg was preferred to 4 mg, which caused the most cataract and pressure rise. The dexamethasone implant's benefit rose from day 30, peaked at 90 days and was gone by 6 months; COBALT re-treated as often as every 4 months in branch occlusion. [S1]
- **Overall, pressure rise and cataract with steroids make anti-VEGF the better first treatment.** [S1]
- **Grid laser can be considered in branch occlusion with a perfused macula, oedema and vision of 20/40 or worse,** but anti-VEGF gives more gain and is preferred unless contraindicated. Laser suits patients who cannot attend monthly (review 3 months later). Adding laser to anti-VEGF gives no extra benefit. [S1]
- **Central occlusion: focal laser does not help the macular oedema** (CVOS). [S1]
- **RCOphth (S2), central occlusion: licensed anti-VEGF or the dexamethasone implant are both recommended for the oedema,** chosen with the patient by injection frequency and the risks of pressure rise and cataract. Adding grid laser or PRP to injections gives no benefit. [S2]
- **Inject monthly until vision is at its best and stable:** a little over a third reach it after 3 injections and another third need 6. On an as-needed regimen, review every 4–8 weeks. Delaying the start by up to 6 months cost vision, so begin once the diagnosis is made unless a deferral is agreed. [S2]
- ⚠️ **When to stop in poor-vision eyes (S2):** with presenting vision below 6/96, reconsider if neither acuity nor OCT thickness improves after 3 monthly loading injections, and do not go on if there is no response after 6. In ischaemic central occlusion, stop after 3 injections if the oedema clears with no visual gain; but do not exclude eyes with more than 10 disc areas of non-perfusion, or vision of 6/96 or worse with significant oedema, from a trial. [S2]
- **Branch occlusion (S2): mild, recent loss can be watched for the first 3 months,** but with marked loss only 18–41% of eyes improve unaided, on average not to 6/12, so treat early. Licensed anti-VEGF or the dexamethasone implant are both recommended. Reserve grid laser for oedema of at least 3 months with vision of 6/12 or worse, no significant macular haemorrhage and a perfused fovea on angiography. [S2]

## Treat new vessels

- ⚠️ **Neovascularisation:** in branch occlusion with retinal new vessels, laser the non-perfused area to cut the risk of vitreous haemorrhage. In central occlusion with retinal or iris new vessels, give dense peripheral panretinal laser. An anti-VEGF injection first gives short-term control and can make full laser easier. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ischaemic central occlusion (S2): check monthly for iris or angle new vessels,** and every 1–2 months in the first year after anti-VEGF stops. At the first sign of them give anti-VEGF, then enough PRP on the same day (before the injection) or within 1–2 weeks. With a dexamethasone implant, finding iris vessels early is vital. [S2]
- **Established neovascular glaucoma:** if the eye has any visual potential, control the pressure with drops, surgery or cyclo-ablation; regression of the iris and angle vessels helps long-term comfort. [S2]
- **Branch occlusion new vessels (S2): sector laser to the ischaemic retina once disc or retinal new vessels appear;** evidence suggests waiting for a vitreous haemorrhage does not harm the outlook, and angiography is not usually needed first. Review every 3–4 months if a quadrant or more is ischaemic. A quadrant usually takes at least 500 burns of 500 µm, 1 burn-width apart, to a mild grey-white. [S2]

## Follow-up and referral

- ⚠️ **Central occlusion: check the iris and angle (undilated slit lamp and gonioscopy) as often as monthly for 6 months,** and again in ischaemic central occlusion after anti-VEGF stops, to catch new vessels early. [S1]
- **Refer every patient to their GP for the systemic conditions, share the results, and tell both about the risk to the other eye.** Offer counselling, vision rehabilitation and social support when vision is lost despite treatment. [S1]
- **Follow-up with significant ischaemia (S2): monthly for 6 months, then 3-monthly to 1 year.** Uncomplicated cases rarely need review beyond 3 years, and disc collaterals with spontaneous resolution of the oedema allow discharge after 6 months. [S2]
- **Service standards (UK, S2):** see and treat within 2–4 weeks of presentation, starting treatment within 1–2 weeks of the specialist assessment. Offer low-vision support early, without waiting until every treatment has been tried. [S2]
