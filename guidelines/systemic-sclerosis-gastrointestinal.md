# Systemic sclerosis — gastrointestinal involvement (including scleroderma oesophagus)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and children with systemic sclerosis (SSc) and gastrointestinal involvement: reflux and dysphagia from scleroderma oesophagus, refractory reflux, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, diarrhoea and constipation, malnutrition, faecal incontinence, surgery and Barrett's surveillance. Skin, lung, heart, kidney and vascular SSc are out of scope. General reflux care: see `gord-and-dyspepsia`. Edition 2.0 adds S2 (World Scleroderma Foundation, 2026) on refractory reflux.

> ⚠️ International guideline (BSR, 2024). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **Evidence is weak.** Almost every GI recommendation is expert consensus with low-quality evidence (level C); grades are shown as strength (1 strong, 2 conditional), evidence level, and panel agreement.

> ⚠️ **S2 is an international expert consensus** (World Scleroderma Foundation, 2026) on refractory reflux in SSc, built from expert discussion and focused searches rather than a systematic review. It adds the definition of refractory reflux, PPI doses, testing and named add-on drugs. No Australian SSc gastrointestinal guideline was found.

## Recognise

- **The gut is the organ most often involved in SSc**, and GI symptoms are among the most burdensome for patients, adults and children alike. [S1]
- **Reflux oesophagitis often responds to acid suppression, but dysmotility and anorectal disease with incontinence are much harder to treat.** Gastroenterology and, when needed, GI surgical input can transform outcomes. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Nutrition matters**: many people struggle to eat enough, and dietary changes can help both gut and general disease. [S1]

## Oesophagus: reflux and dysphagia

- **Optimise and check adherence to general and lifestyle measures** for oesophageal symptoms such as reflux. (1C, 99%) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Use a proton pump inhibitor and/or an H2-receptor antagonist for symptomatic reflux and dysphagia.** (1C, 99%) [S1]
- **Prokinetics (for example dopamine-antagonist promotility drugs) may be added** for dysphagia and reflux. (1C, 98%) [S1]
- **If reflux is refractory**, investigate upper GI structure and motility and confirm acid reflux, for example with pH testing. (2C, 96%) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Barrett's metaplasia is common in SSc** and, with other risk factors, should prompt follow-up gastroscopy; a history of Barrett's oesophagus marks a high-risk case for follow-up cancer screening. (2C, 94%) [S1]
- **In SSc lung disease, manage reflux as part of supportive care**; oesophageal involvement may limit eligibility for lung transplant. [S1]

## Refractory reflux (WSF)

- **Refractory reflux in SSc (WSF):** symptoms, or new or continuing mucosal disease (Barrett's, oesophagitis, strictures, ulcers, even without symptoms), despite optimal acid suppression. Optimal means a high-dose PPI (omeprazole 40 mg or more a day or equivalent, for example twice daily) or a combination such as PPI plus H2 blocker or standard-dose PPI plus a promotility drug; standard dose is omeprazole 20 mg a day. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Look for red flags** (iron-deficiency anaemia, unintended weight loss, night sweats, fever) that need urgent tests for cancer, infection or bleeding. In early diffuse or anti-RNA polymerase III-positive disease, remember gastric antral vascular ectasia ('watermelon stomach') as a bleeding source. Finish the work-up before blaming SSc for GI symptoms. [S2]
- **Review other drugs:** calcium channel blockers and other vasodilators for Raynaud's can worsen reflux by lowering lower oesophageal sphincter tone. Check that PPIs are taken about 30 minutes before meals. [S2]
- **Test objectively:** examine the mucosa in everyone with refractory symptoms, and strongly consider high-resolution manometry (particularly with dysphagia) and pH-impedance monitoring on a PPI to link symptoms to acid exposure. If manometry is not tolerated, scintigraphy or a timed barium swallow can screen for dysmotility. [S2]
- **Test for Helicobacter pylori and eradicate it if present.** It does not cause reflux, but long-term acid suppression in people carrying it raises the risk of gastric atrophy. [S2]
- **Night-time reflux:** raise the head of the bed, leave a longer gap between dinner and bed, and lie on the left side. Keep up weight control, stopping smoking and avoiding large, fatty or spicy meals throughout the disease. [S2]
- ⚠️ **High-dose PPI is the first-line drug strategy for refractory SSc reflux,** a move away from H2 blockers, which often failed. Weigh the risks when osteoporosis, electrolyte disturbance, bacterial overgrowth or infection risk is high; heavy PPI exposure is linked with more severe calcinosis, and potassium-competitive acid blockers have similar side effects. [S2]
- **Add-ons when a PPI alone is not enough or cannot be increased:** an H2 blocker, a promotility drug or an alginate. With refractory reflux and dysphagia, strongly consider a trial of a promotility drug (prucalopride, pyridostigmine, domperidone or metoclopramide); vomiting, early satiety, post-meal pain or bloating also favour one. [S2]
- **Further options:** buspirone for a hypotensive lower oesophageal sphincter (stop it if a 2–4 week trial does not help); baclofen when pH-impedance shows mostly weakly acidic or non-acid reflux with a positive symptom link; an anti-emetic (ondansetron, promethazine, mirtazapine) for nausea that promotility drugs do not settle. Immunosuppression has no firm evidence for SSc gut disease. [S2]
- **Endoscopic anti-reflux procedures have little evidence** and are unsuitable with a large (over 3 cm) hiatal hernia. Surgery such as Nissen fundoplication is a last resort for highly selected patients, but may matter when upper-GI disease threatens eligibility for a life-saving lung transplant. [S2]
- **With lung disease (SSc-ILD),** screen for aspiration (a modified barium swallow if there is proximal dysphagia, or coughing with meals or on lying flat), optimise reflux treatment, and test high-risk patients for silent reflux. **With overlapping myositis,** screen for pharyngeal involvement; IV immunoglobulin 2 g/kg a month, split over several days, may greatly help pharyngeal myositis. [S2]

## Small bowel and colon

- **For symptomatic small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, give intermittent broad-spectrum oral antibiotics** (for example ciprofloxacin), possibly rotating agents; rifaximin is an option when refractory. (2B, 95%) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Stop a fluoroquinolone at the first sign of a serious adverse reaction**, such as tendon pain or inflammation. [S1]
- **Diarrhoea and constipation often alternate**: treat symptomatically with antidiarrhoeals (for example loperamide) or laxatives, after excluding non-SSc causes. (1C, 96%) [S1]
- **Pelvic floor physiotherapy with anorectal biofeedback** may help selected patients with incontinence. (2C, 91%) [S1]

## Nutrition and surgery

- ⚠️ **Consider parenteral nutrition for severe weight loss or malnutrition (including high risk) that does not respond to enteral supplements.** (1B, 97%) [S1]
- **Children with juvenile SSc:** actively assess and monitor nutrition, growth and puberty, and manage faltering growth with tertiary paediatric gastroenterology and dietetic input. (1C, 99%) [S1]
- **Operate on GI complications only when essential and there is no alternative.** (1C, 97%) [S1]
