# Lower abdominal pain in adolescent girls (gynaecological causes)

## Key points

- **This page covers gynaecological causes only** — other causes of acute abdominal pain must also be considered. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Obstetric emergencies** (ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, placental abruption, uterine rupture) **and gynaecological emergencies** (ovarian or adnexal torsion) **need urgent gynaecological or surgical referral.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Torsion is a clinical diagnosis; do not delay gynaecological referral for investigations such as ultrasound.** [S1]

## Assess

- **Menstrual history:** menarche, LMP, cycle frequency, duration, flow, pain, flooding, **clots larger than 2 cm**, pad/tampon change frequency. [S1]
- **Also ask** about vaginal discharge, pain characteristics (sudden onset, quality, severity, triggers, bowel/bladder symptoms, fever, vomiting), sexual history and contraception, systemic features, and a HEADSS screen. [S1]
- **Non-specific episodic pain:** consider constipation, abdominal migraine and functional abdominal pain. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Examine for pallor, hypotension, tachycardia and an abdominal (uterine or ovarian) mass.** **Vaginal examination is rarely indicated** — discuss with a senior clinician and perform it only once. [S1]

## Critical causes

- ⚠️ **Ovarian or adnexal torsion:** sharp pelvic pain of acute, sudden onset — generalised or one-sided (usually right lower quadrant), possibly intermittent; adnexal mass; nausea and vomiting. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ectopic pregnancy:** abdominal/pelvic pain, missed period then vaginal bleeding, possible shoulder-tip pain. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Acute placental abruption:** back and/or abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, contractions and DIC in severe cases; any gestation, peak 24–26 weeks. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Uterine rupture:** peritonism, uterine tenderness, abnormal fetal heart rate, vaginal bleeding. **Molar pregnancy:** severe vomiting, an enlarged uterus, pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding. [S1]
- **PID:** risk factors (e.g. unprotected sex), mucopurulent discharge, post-coital bleeding, fever, pelvic or lower abdominal pain. [S1]

## Other causes

- **Mittelschmerz:** mild unilateral recurrent midcycle pain. **Ruptured ovarian cyst:** one-sided pelvic pain, severe and sudden, sometimes after strenuous activity. [S1]
- **Endometriosis:** crampy pelvic pain with menses, possibly with pain on defecation, urination or intercourse. [S1]
- **Imperforate hymen:** amenorrhoea, cyclical pain, vaginal distension causing back pain or pain on defecation or urination. **Retained foreign body:** discharge, intermittent bleeding or spotting. [S1]

## Investigate

- ⚠️ **Do a βhCG (with consent)** — urine, and/or blood if a quantitative result is needed. [S1]
- **Critical cause suspected: pelvic ultrasound**, transabdominal (full bladder) or transvaginal (sexually active, with consent) — but never delaying torsion referral. [S1]
- **Infection suspected:** urine MCS; first-pass urine NAAT for *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, or a vaginal/endocervical swab (MCS, chlamydia, gonorrhoea); FBE, CRP ± blood culture if PID or sepsis is a real concern. [S1]

## Refer and discharge

- **Consult the local paediatric team** for haemodynamic instability, marked pain or unclear cause. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Urgent gynaecology (or surgical) consultation** for suspected torsion, ectopic pregnancy, threatened miscarriage or non-viable pregnancy; also for causes needing surgery, and PID. [S1]
- **Discharge when stable with a GP review scheduled.** [S1]
