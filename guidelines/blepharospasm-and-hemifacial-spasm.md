# Blepharospasm and hemifacial spasm — botulinum toxin therapy

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** botulinum toxin type A (BT) treatment of facial dystonia, which includes blepharospasm: the general treatment algorithm, choosing target muscles and doses, dilution and injection volume, product equivalence, injection intervals and drug handling. S1 does **not** cover hemifacial spasm; S2 (a 2025 review) covers its causes, investigation and treatment, including microvascular decompression. Other botulinum toxin uses (cervical dystonia, spasticity): see `spasticity`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Principles

- **Botulinum toxin therapy is individual: hit the right muscle with the right dose.** Both are recorded in an injection scheme, which may take several injection series to optimise. [S1]
- **Choose target muscles by documenting the abnormal posture and movements**, and tell compensatory or protective muscle activity apart from the primary abnormal activity. [S1]
- **Use the lowest dose that gives a robust, lasting effect.** Lower doses limit weakness, spread to neighbouring muscles, total dose and cost. The dose depends on muscle mass, the muscle's therapeutic window and the risk of weakening adjacent muscles. [S1]
- **Adjust within the dose range:** go higher when abnormal activity is strong and helper muscles are available, lower when the abnormal activity is functionally useful (as in dystonic tremor). [S1]

## Facial dystonia (including blepharospasm)

- **Facial dystonia is the second most common dystonia.** In the reference-centre series of 100 patients (mean age 65.2 years, 69% women), 45% also had cervical and 25% oromandibular dystonia. [S1]
- **Typical facial dose:** a total of 78.8 ± 31.6 MU (range 4 to 220 MU) across 3.7 ± 1.8 muscles. Orbicularis oculi (pars orbitalis) was injected in 91%, procerus in 32% and orbicularis oculi (pars palpebralis) in 26%. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Injection cautions:** perioral injections readily cause weakness; inject frontalis carefully because it helps open the eyelid; add pars palpebralis of orbicularis oculi when there is an eyelid-opening apraxia component. [S1]

## Hemifacial spasm

- **Hemifacial spasm (HFS) is involuntary, paroxysmal twitching of the facial-expression muscles on one side,** all supplied by that side's facial nerve. Women are affected about twice as often as men, onset is usually from the fourth to the sixth decade, and 2.6% of cases are bilateral. [S2]
- **The usual cause is a neurovascular conflict:** an ectatic or looping artery (typically the anterior- or posterior-inferior cerebellar artery or the vertebral artery) presses on the facial nerve in the posterior fossa. [S2]
- **Image every patient with MRI** to exclude a structural cause (vestibular schwannoma, meningioma, arteriovenous anomaly); high-resolution T2 sequences with MRA can show the conflict. EEG and EMG help exclude mimics such as seizures and myokymia when the clinical signs are subtle. [S2]
- ⚠️ **A vessel touching the nerve is common without symptoms** (found on 38.8% of asymptomatic sides versus 97.9% of symptomatic ones), so the MRI needs a reader who knows facial-nerve root anatomy; first reports can miss the true compression or blame an incidental contact. [S2]
- **HFS is not life-limiting, and many people choose no procedure;** treatment is for symptoms that distress or reduce quality of life. In one cohort followed for an average of 12 years without treatment, 41.3% were in remission. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Botulinum toxin A is the mainstay for HFS:** it helps most patients (reported efficacy 73 to 98.4%), is used in up to 90%, and keeps working for up to 10 years. Ptosis and facial weakness are the commonest side effects, usually mild and short-lived. [S2]
- **Toxin resistance is rare at the low doses used in HFS,** but the effect wears off, so injections are repeated every 3 to 6 months by a clinician skilled in choosing doses and target muscles. [S2]
- **Oral drugs help only a small minority,** mostly on case-report evidence. Carbamazepine, baclofen, pizotifen and clonazepam often cause drowsiness, dizziness, ataxia or weakness in older people; gabapentin and levetiracetam may be better tolerated. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Microvascular decompression (MVD) is the only potentially curative treatment,** helping up to 90% of those operated on, yet in some groups only about 10% are referred, after delays of up to 5 years. Tell suitable patients about it and refer in good time. [S2]
- **After MVD the benefit may be delayed** (in up to 30% of cases, usually about 30 days, occasionally up to a year). Previous botulinum toxin does not worsen surgical results. Up to 9% need a second operation, which works less often and has more complications (nervous system lesions 10.6% after a first operation, 20.7% after revision). [S2]
- **Other options rest on case reports:** pulsed radiofrequency ablation gives only short-lived relief and often a disfiguring facial palsy; electroacupuncture helped one patient temporarily. [S2]

## Preparation and products

- **Standard dilution:** 2.5 ml of 0.9% saline per 100 MU of onabotulinumtoxinA (ONA) or incobotulinumtoxinA (INCO). Inject 0.5 ml (20 MU) per site, or 0.2 ml (4 MU) per site in the face. [S1]
- **Keep the dilution constant** for patient safety, except in special indications such as hyperhidrosis. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Products are not interchangeable unit for unit, except ONA and INCO,** which compare at 1:1. Conversion to abobotulinumtoxinA (ABO) is uncertain (1:2 to 1:5), so ABO was left out of the dosing tables; other conversions remain disputed. [S1]
- **Drugs of the same toxin type behave similarly;** INCO, which lacks complexing proteins, has lower antigenicity. [S1]
- **Storage:** unreconstituted toxin keeps a long time, and only INCO can be stored and shipped at room temperature. Use reconstituted toxin within 24 h, although INCO data suggest it stays stable for at least 1 year. [S1]

## Intervals and total dose

- **Injection intervals:** traditionally not less than 12 weeks. INCO has been given at shorter intervals, down to 6 weeks, without toxic or immune complications ('short interval therapy'). [S1]
- **Total dose per session:** product registrations usually cap it at about 300–400 MU; high-dose studies found INCO totals up to 1250 MU safe, reached by treating more muscles rather than giving more per muscle. [S1]
- **These doses reflect a centre with few cost or legal limits** that routinely varied registered doses, intervals and contraindications with permission for off-label use. [S1]
