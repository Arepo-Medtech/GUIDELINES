# Transfusional iron overload and iron chelation (transfusion-dependent thalassaemia)

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** why iron overload develops, how to measure it (serum ferritin, MRI liver iron and cardiac T2*), the aims of chelation, choosing and adjusting chelation with the three licensed chelators (desferrioxamine, called deferoxamine in the source; deferiprone; deferasirox), cardiac iron, and chelation during fever and pregnancy, for people with transfusion-dependent thalassaemia (TDT). The PBS conditions below also cover other transfusion-dependent anaemias, which this source does not address directly. ## Why iron builds up

- **In regularly transfused TDT, iron overload always happens**, because the body has no way to get rid of excess iron. In non-transfusion-dependent thalassaemia, increased gut absorption matters more. Excess iron damages the heart, liver (cirrhosis and cancer), growth and endocrine glands. [S1]
- **Transfusion context:** transfuse every 2–4 weeks, keeping pre-transfusion haemoglobin above 90–105 g/L (up to 110–120 g/L with cardiac complications) (A), and post-transfusion haemoglobin below 140–150 g/L (A). [S1]

## Measure and monitor

- **Serum ferritin at least every 3 months** (every 1–3 months), aiming for 500 to 1000 μg/L. Base changes to therapy on the trend, not on one result. [S1]
- **Liver iron concentration (LIC) by validated, standardised MRI**, ideally the same method each time. Normal is up to 1.8 mg/g dry weight; LIC persistently over 15 mg/g dry weight is linked to a worse prognosis and liver fibrosis. Total body iron (mg/kg) is 10.6 × LIC. [S1]
- **How often to repeat liver MRI:** every 1 or 2 years if stable at 3–7 mg/g; yearly above 7 mg/g; every 6–12 months if below 3 mg/g or falling fast. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Cardiac T2* MRI:** every 2 years if above 20 ms, yearly at 10–19 ms, and every 6 months below 10 ms. Below 10 ms, check cardiac function: heart failure risk is high and chelation must be intensified urgently. [S1]
- **Iron reaches the heart after the liver, and seldom before age 8** (B). Liver iron also clears faster than heart iron with chelation (B). [S1]
- **Screen regularly for iron damage to organs:** heart, liver and endocrine function, including diabetes, hypothyroidism, hypoparathyroidism and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. [S1]
- **Keep records for each patient:** the age at which regular transfusion and chelation began, and each year's blood use (mL/kg packed cells) and daily iron load (mg/kg/day). [S1]

## Chelation: aims and choice

- **Chelation has five aims:** prevention (keep body iron safe by matching excretion to transfusion intake); rescue (remove stored excess); emergency (urgent intensification for iron-induced heart failure); dose adjustment (avoid both under- and over-chelation); and adherence. Intermittent high-dose chelation does not protect continuously against labile iron and adds toxicity. [S1]
- **Three chelators are licensed:** deferoxamine (desferrioxamine), deferiprone and deferasirox. All three lower total body iron and liver iron (A). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Chelation improves survival and lowers heart failure risk and iron-related illness** (A). At the right dose and frequency it can balance the iron gained from transfusion (A). [S1]
- **Prevent build-up rather than rescue later** (B): iron damage is often permanent, and stored iron comes out slowly, especially once it has spread beyond the liver. [S1]
- **Response depends on dose and duration of exposure** (A) and on how fast the patient is being transfused (B). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Lower the dose as ferritin or liver iron falls**, because over-chelation increases side effects (B; shown most clearly with deferoxamine). [S1]
- **Tailor the regimen to the person and their current state, and work on adherence:** chelation fails if it is not taken regularly (B). Patients report better outcomes with oral than with parenteral chelation (B), and psychological wellbeing affects adherence and survival (C). [S1]

## Cardiac iron

- ⚠️ **Cardiac iron overload needs intensified chelation, preferably two drugs together**, with or without heart dysfunction or failure (B). Chelation guided by cardiac T2* is the best way to prevent iron cardiomyopathy (B). [S1]
- **Heart function can recover within weeks** if chelation covers all 24 hours (A), but stored heart iron takes months or years to clear (A). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Without cardiac MRI:** a fall in left ventricular function on serial echocardiograms is a warning sign of iron toxicity and should prompt strong, sustained escalation of chelation (B). [S1]

## Special situations

- ⚠️ **Fever:** stop deferoxamine temporarily and start antibiotics promptly. Patients with iron overload can keep taking oral deferiprone or deferasirox while febrile. [S1]
- **Pregnancy:** if heart function worsens, deferoxamine can be used with caution after the first trimester. [S1]
- **Endocrine complications can reverse** when total body iron is normalised with intensive combined deferoxamine plus deferiprone (B). [S1]
- **Vitamin C (2–3 mg/kg/day)** is recommended with deferoxamine infusions, or when deficiency is proven. [S1]
- **Offer stem cell transplant early**, before iron complications develop, when an HLA-identical sibling donor is available. [S1]
