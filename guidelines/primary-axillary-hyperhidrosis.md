# Primary axillary hyperhidrosis (and other focal hyperhidrosis)

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with primary focal hyperhidrosis, above all axillary: separating primary from secondary sweating, the treatment ladder (topical, oral, iontophoresis, botulinum toxin, ablation, surgery) and Australian access. Generalised and craniofacial sweating are touched on only where the source compares them.

## Recognise

- **Hyperhidrosis is sweating beyond what thermoregulation needs.** About 5% of US adults are affected (up to 16% in some groups); fewer than half seek care and fewer still are treated well. [S1]
- **Primary hyperhidrosis has no underlying cause.** It is usually focal (axillae first, then palms), starts in adolescence or early adulthood, and runs in families: up to 60% have an affected first-degree relative in palmar cohorts. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Think secondary hyperhidrosis** when sweating is generalised, begins after age 25, or occurs at night; look for an underlying cause. [S1]
- **The burden is real:** around 50% report reduced quality of life, and generalised anxiety, social anxiety and depression occur far more often in people with hyperhidrosis. [S1]

## Treatment ladder

- **Work up the ladder:** topical, oral and device treatments come before injections, ablation or surgery. The four-point Hyperhidrosis Disease Severity Scale can record severity and response. [S1]
- **Topical treatment is first line for focal hyperhidrosis**: easy to use, well tolerated and effective. [S1]
- **Use clinical-strength aluminium chloride (15%–25%), not ordinary 1%–2% antiperspirant.** Response rates reach 98% in the axillae, a little lower on palms and soles. [S1]
- **Apply aluminium salts at bedtime,** when sweating is lowest, daily or every other day; expect benefit in 1–2 weeks. [S1]
- **Irritation is the main problem:** up to 70% get at least mild itch, which eases with continued use. There is no clear evidence tying these antiperspirants to Alzheimer disease or breast cancer. [S1]
- **Topical glycopyrrolate is an alternative** (63% improved in an open-label wipe study). Warn patients to wash their hands afterwards: touching an eye can dilate the pupil. [S1]
- **Oral anticholinergics (glycopyrrolate, oxybutynin) are usually second line for axillary sweating** and first line for generalised sweating. Oxybutynin response was 76% in a systematic review; side effects rise with dose and in older people. [S1]
- **Propranolol suits sweating that flares with stress,** taken regularly or when needed. Take care with bradycardia, low blood pressure, asthma and hypoglycaemia risk. [S1]

## Botulinum toxin

- **Botulinum toxin is second or third line,** when topical or device treatment has not worked. It is injected at the dermal-subcutaneous junction. [S1]
- **Axillary injections satisfy up to 96% of patients,** and the effect lasts on average 6–9 months. Mapping the sweating area with a starch-iodine test guides injection. [S1]
- **Compensatory sweating after toxin is uncommon.** Weakness from toxin spreading into muscle is more of a problem in the palms and face than the axillae. [S1]

## Ablation and surgery

- **Microwave thermolysis (miraDry) is licensed for axillary hyperhidrosis,** with response up to 90% at 12 months. It needs anaesthesia, reduces underarm hair, and is not for palms or soles. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Endoscopic thoracic sympathectomy is mainly for refractory palmar sweating.** Compensatory sweating elsewhere follows in 80% and can be worse than the original problem, so counsel carefully first. [S1]

## Australian access

- **Refer patients who fail an initial trial of topicals for specialist care** (usually a neurologist or dermatologist); advanced therapies control sweating in nearly everyone. [S1]
- ⚠️ **In Australia, botulinum toxin is subsidised by Medicare and the PBS for axillary hyperhidrosis only.** Palmar, plantar and facial toxin, iontophoresis devices, ablation and surgery mostly mean out-of-pocket costs. [S1]
