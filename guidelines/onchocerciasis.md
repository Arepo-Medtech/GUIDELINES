# Onchocerciasis (river blindness) — imported infection

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, diagnosis and ivermectin/doxycycline treatment of onchocerciasis acquired abroad, in migrants (mostly from sub-Saharan Africa) and travellers, as seen outside endemic areas. ## Who gets it

- **Most onchocerciasis seen outside endemic areas is in migrants, refugees and visiting-friends-and-relatives travellers, almost all from sub-Saharan Africa.** Expatriate travellers usually have lived there for at least 3 months, though short intense exposure can be enough. [S1]
- **It is transmitted by blackflies near fast-flowing rivers;** adult worms live 9–15 years, and disease comes from inflammation against dying microfilariae and *Wolbachia*. [S1]

## Recognise

- **Itch is the commonest symptom (60–90%).** Travellers mostly have acute papular dermatitis; migrants more often have chronic papular or lichenified dermatitis (sowda), depigmentation and skin atrophy. [S1]
- **Sowda (lichenified onchodermatitis) is a rare hyperreactive response with a low parasite burden:** intensely itchy, hyperpigmented plaques. The generalised form, by contrast, gives mild or intermittent skin symptoms despite heavy infection. [S2]
- **Eye disease is almost confined to migrants;** about two-thirds report eye symptoms and 20–80% have slit-lamp findings, mostly old corneal scarring, with about 10% having some functional vision loss. [S1]
- **Other clues:** subcutaneous nodules (onchocercomata) in migrants, acute fixed unilateral limb swelling in travellers, and up to 20% asymptomatic. Symptoms can start 1–1.5 years after leaving, and diagnosis in migrants is often delayed by 1–2 years. [S1]

## Diagnose

- **A normal eosinophil count does not exclude it:** eosinophilia is present in only about two-thirds of cases. IgE is raised in 50–90%. [S1]
- **Proof needs the parasite: microfilariae in a skin snip, an adult worm in a removed nodule, or microfilariae seen in the eye on slit lamp.** Pan-filarial serology is widely used but non-specific; recombinant-antigen assays and the OV-16 rapid test are more specific but mostly confined to reference laboratories. [S1]
- **Outside a reference centre, a presumptive diagnosis rests on exposure, typical features and antifilarial serology;** serology is negative in up to 20% of travellers and can cross-react with *Strongyloides*. A trial of empiric ivermectin is reasonable in suspected cases. [S1]
- **Look for other parasites:** 37% of migrants with onchocerciasis had at least one more parasitic infection, often another filaria. [S1]
- **Skin-snip microscopy is very specific but insensitive in early or light infection and after ivermectin,** and it is invasive and painful. Feeling for nodules is non-invasive but neither sensitive nor specific. [S2]
- **OV16 IgG4 serology (ELISA or rapid test) cannot separate past from current infection** and cross-reacts where several filarial infections coexist. [S2]
- **PCR and LAMP on skin snips are more sensitive and specific than microscopy and tell filarial species apart,** so they avoid serological cross-reaction; cost limits routine use. [S2]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **Standard care is ivermectin 150 microgram/kg as a single dose repeated every 3–6 months while symptoms persist,** with doxycycline added where there is no contraindication. Ivermectin clears microfilariae from skin and eye but does not kill adult worms, so it must be repeated. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Exclude Loa loa before ivermectin in anyone exposed to a Loa-endemic area:** a heavy Loa microfilarial load risks encephalopathy. If the load is high, use doxycycline instead. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Doxycycline 200 mg daily for 6 weeks (off-label)** depletes *Wolbachia*, sterilising and slowly killing adult worms; it is safe with heavy Loa loa, which lacks *Wolbachia*. The trial evidence carries a high risk of bias. [S1]
- **Doxycycline works through Wolbachia: a six-week course blocks embryogenesis, sterilising female worms, and suppresses microfilariae for over a year without serious side effects.** It is contraindicated in pregnancy and in children under 8. [S2]
- **Adding ivermectin to doxycycline gave only a marginal gain in one randomised trial:** skin free of microfilariae at 21 months in 89% with the combination against 67% with doxycycline alone, not statistically significant. [S2]
- **Ivermectin is primarily microfilaricidal:** long repeated courses have only modest effects on adult worms and their fertility, repeated or high doses raise toxicity, and defects in the P-glycoprotein transporter can cause neurotoxicity. In endemic programmes it must be taken for about 10–15 years to clear infection. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Diethylcarbamazine (DEC) is contraindicated as treatment:** it provokes severe Mazzotti reactions, especially with heavy microfilarial loads. The DEC-based Mazzotti diagnostic test has largely fallen out of use for the same reason. [S2]
- **Moxidectin (FDA-approved in 2018) has stronger, longer microfilaricidal action than ivermectin with similar safety,** but Mazzotti reactions are more common and approval for children under 12 is still pending. [S2]
- **Reduced response to ivermectin is emerging in endemic areas:** microfilariae return to the skin faster than expected, suppression weakens after repeated rounds, and phenotypic and genetic resistance have been described. [S2]
- **How long to treat is unknown.** Travellers may need ivermectin only while symptomatic; in one cohort, stopping after a median of 3.9 doses over 1.75 years left no long-term sequelae after more than 10 years. [S1]
