# Hypercalcaemia in neonates and infants (non-malignant causes)

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising, investigating and treating non-malignant hypercalcaemia in neonates and young infants: high-PTH causes (neonatal severe hyperparathyroidism, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia), low-PTH causes (nutritional, vitamin D excess, idiopathic infantile hypercalcaemia, Williams syndrome, subcutaneous fat necrosis) and acute and longer-term treatment. **Adults and older children are not covered.** Hypercalcaemia with cancer: see `hypercalcaemia-of-malignancy`. Low calcium in children: see `hypocalcaemia-children`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **Infant hypercalcaemia is uncommon but can cause lasting harm:** nephrocalcinosis with permanent kidney damage, osteoporosis and neurodevelopmental impairment. [S1]
- **Hypercalcaemia means a serum calcium more than two standard deviations above the normal mean; use age-appropriate ranges,** because calcium runs higher in newborns and preterm infants. [S1]
- **Look for failure to thrive, poor feeding, constipation, polyuria, irritability, lethargy, seizures and hypotonia.** [S1]

## Assess

- **Work-up:** family and diet history; examination for dysmorphic features, granulomatous disease or subcutaneous fat deposits; total and ionised calcium, albumin, phosphate, creatinine, intact PTH, vitamin D metabolites, and urine calcium, phosphate and creatinine. [S1]
- **Sort the cause by PTH:** neonatal causes are maternal or neonatal and split into high-PTH and low-PTH disorders. [S1]
- **Transient neonatal hypercalcaemia from maternal hypocalcaemia** is usually diagnosed by checking the mother's calcium and the family history, and usually settles within a few weeks. [S1]
- **Other causes to consider:** thyroid disease (hyper- or hypothyroidism), adrenal insufficiency, and medicines such as thiazides and lithium. [S1]

## High-PTH causes

- ⚠️ **Neonatal severe hyperparathyroidism usually presents in the first 2 weeks with calcium above 4.5 mmol/L and can be fatal if untreated.** [S1]
- **Early parathyroidectomy is the preferred treatment,** with bisphosphonates to bring calcium down beforehand; cinacalcet has lowered PTH and calcium in some infants, but its effect depends on genotype. [S1]
- **Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia is usually benign and persists after parathyroidectomy,** so distinguish it from primary hyperparathyroidism (genetics, urine calcium/creatinine ratio below 0.02) to avoid needless surgery. [S1]
- **Cinacalcet for symptomatic FHH has been reported in children, but its safety and efficacy there are unproven** and it is not FDA-approved for this age group. [S1]

## Low-PTH causes

- **Nutritional causes:** excess calcium in enriched formulas, or too little dietary phosphate, can raise calcium. [S1]
- **In formula- or parenterally-fed preterm infants, equimolar calcium:phosphate ratios (0.75:1 to 1:1) and close electrolyte and phosphorus monitoring are advised;** changing formula and/or giving phosphate supplements corrected hypophosphataemia. [S1]
- **Vitamin D excess (in the infant or the breastfeeding mother):** rehydration with furosemide usually fixes it; bisphosphonates have been used when 25(OH)D stayed high. [S1]
- **Idiopathic infantile hypercalcaemia (IIH) presents in the first year** with suppressed PTH, hypercalciuria and often nephrocalcinosis; calcium usually normalises before age three, but nephrocalcinosis and hypercalciuria may persist, and early diagnosis limits kidney damage. [S1]
- **IIH with SLC34A1 variants (2016) adds hypophosphataemia from kidney phosphate wasting** to the classic picture. [S1]
- **In Williams syndrome, hypercalcaemia is reported in 5% to 50%;** it usually resolves in the first few years but may return at puberty. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Subcutaneous fat necrosis of the newborn** (weeks after a traumatic birth or therapeutic hypothermia) can cause fatal hypercalcaemia; it responds to furosemide and glucocorticoids, and bisphosphonates normalised calcium with less recurrence. [S1]

## Treat (IIH and Williams syndrome)

- ⚠️ **In every case, stop vitamin D supplements at once and rehydrate;** rehydration with furosemide often works in Williams syndrome, though some children need more. [S1]
- **Bisphosphonates have been used in Williams syndrome** with normal calcium afterwards and no adverse effects reported. [S1]
- ⚠️ **A low-calcium diet works in IIH and Williams syndrome but needs close monitoring,** because it can cause calcium deficiency and rickets, and it may not fully normalise calcium and PTH. [S1]
- **Corticosteroids are often used for symptomatic hypercalcaemia but should be brief,** and in CYP24A1 disease they sometimes fail. [S1]
- **Calcitonin has worked (alongside thiazides for hypercalciuria), but tachyphylaxis within 48 h limits it to short-term use.** [S1]
- **Cellulose phosphate, which binds dietary calcium, has resolved IIH hypercalcaemia** but is unavailable in some countries. [S1]
