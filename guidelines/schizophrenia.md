# Schizophrenia — assessment, antipsychotic treatment and physical health

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with schizophrenia in Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand: physical-health workup, choice and sequence of antipsychotics, treatment resistance and clozapine, side-effect management, stopping medication, psychosocial and lifestyle care. Acute behavioural disturbance, mental health law and substance-induced psychosis are outside the guideline. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`. No schizophrenia counterpart exists in the corpus.

> ✅ **COMPILED FROM 2 SOURCES.** 41 claims; **75 fragments or anchors re-checkable by machine; 3 doses.** **S1** Suetani S (published 15 January 2026 (Aust N Z J Psychiatry 2026;60:367-404); AU): 30 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored · **S2** Wagner E (published online 2 June 2025; issue April 2026 (addendum 10.1192/bjp.2025.10372 adds only the expert-group list); international): 11 claims, quoted

> ⚠️ **S2 is an international expert consensus** (Delphi, 93 experts from six continents, 2025) on clozapine-associated myocarditis only. Australian practice follows local clozapine protocols, the product information and the PBS listings below, which may differ.

> ℹ️ **All the recommended first-line agents are PBS-listed for schizophrenia** (aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, lurasidone), as are clozapine and the paliperidone and aripiprazole long-acting injections (see the table below). The guideline notes that VMAT-2 inhibitors for tardive dyskinesia are **not** subsidised in Australia or New Zealand.

## Assessment and physical workup

- **The diagnosis is clinical.** No blood test, scan or cognitive test confirms it; it rests on a full psychiatric assessment, collateral history and ruling out conditions that mimic it. [S1]
- **Life expectancy is cut by about 15 to 20 years**, mostly from cardiometabolic disease tied to modifiable risks such as inactivity, diet and smoking. [S1]
- **About 5% of first presentations have an organic cause.** Reasonable initial bloods are electrolytes, kidney, liver and blood counts; metabolic measures are needed at baseline and ongoing, and fasting samples are usually not required. [S1]
- **Order thyroid tests only when the history or examination raises concern.** **Check prolactin at baseline, 3 months after starting or changing a drug or dose**, and whenever menstrual, sexual or neurological findings suggest it. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Get an MRI brain if intracranial disease is possible:** headache, nausea or vomiting, seizure-like episodes, onset before 12 or after 40, abrupt or fluctuating psychosis, marked cognitive deficits, catatonia or abnormal neurological signs. Let the clinical picture decide on anti-neuronal antibodies and lumbar puncture. [S1]
- **First-episode workup checklist (S1 Table 12):** history of past and current illness, family history, development, neurological symptoms and recent systemic illness; examination with vital signs including postural BP and pulse, neurological, cardiovascular and eye examination, weight and BMI. Baseline tests: FBC, electrolytes, renal and liver function, prolactin, HbA1c and lipids, thyroid tests if indicated, urine drug screen and ECG; consider a pregnancy test and brain MRI. Add by context: with developmental delay or intellectual impairment, neuropsychology and MRI, considering karyotype, genetic and metabolic tests; in older adults or with cognitive decline, cognitive assessment and MRI, considering neurology or geriatric referral and CSF; in atypical psychosis (catatonia, rapid or fluctuating course, cognitive or neurological signs, early or excessive EPSE), serum neuronal antibodies (NMDAR, VGKC, GAD), EEG and MRI, considering CSF; with sexual or injecting risk or liver disease, serology for HIV, syphilis and hepatitis B and C; with occupational exposure, consider heavy-metal testing. [S1]

## First-episode psychosis

- ⚠️ **First choice: a dopamine D2 partial agonist (oral aripiprazole, brexpiprazole or cariprazine) or lurasidone**, chosen because they are generally better tolerated, with less metabolic harm. [S1]
- **Start low and go slow:** raise the dose every 1–2 weeks only if response is inadequate and the drug is tolerated. Review mental state closely (for example weekly) at the start, and ask about adherence without judgement; taking under 80% of doses usually counts as non-adherence. [S1]

## Switching and second-line choice

- ⚠️ **Switch to another antipsychotic if:** there is no response at all after 2 weeks at a target-range dose with confirmed adherence; the response is still suboptimal after 2–4 weeks at the maximum recommended dose; or side effects are intolerable at any dose. [S1]
- **Pick the second drug from the other first-line options, or amisulpride, risperidone, or a long-acting injection of aripiprazole or paliperidone.** Prefer a different mechanism: after a partial agonist, try an antagonist such as lurasidone, amisulpride, risperidone or paliperidone depot. [S1]
- **For negative symptoms, first look for secondary causes** (untreated positive symptoms, depression, substance use, medical illness, extrapyramidal effects). Cariprazine, aripiprazole or low-dose amisulpride may work better, and adding an antidepressant helps some people. [S1]

## Treatment-resistant schizophrenia

- ⚠️ **Treatment resistance means inadequate response, with moderate to severe impairment, after two different antipsychotics each given at adequate dose and duration with confirmed adherence** (about 30% of people). Clozapine should then be considered, under tertiary mental health services. [S1]
- **Only around 40% respond to clozapine.** Adding ECT to standard treatment may improve response, at least over 8–12 weeks. [S1]
- **If augmenting clozapine, aripiprazole at a medium dose (9 to <16.5 mg/day) was the only combination linked to fewer admissions.** High-dose olanzapine, quetiapine or risperidone added to clozapine was linked to more psychosis admissions. [S1]
- **Avoid doses above the therapeutic range and antipsychotic polypharmacy** unless clinically necessary. If used, monitor benefit and tolerability closely, and stop the extra drug or return to a therapeutic dose if nothing has improved by 3 months. [S1]
- **If clozapine helps only partly (S1 Table 13),** the options are adding another antipsychotic (for example amisulpride or aripiprazole), ECT, or combining it with certain antidepressants such as fluoxetine, sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram or duloxetine. [S1]

## Clozapine: myocarditis prevention and monitoring

- ⚠️ **Clozapine-associated myocarditis (CAM) usually appears in the first month.** Risk rises with sodium valproate, fast up-titration, slow clozapine metabolism (for example Asian or Indigenous American ancestry, or interacting drugs), recent infection, and a history of myocardial infarction, pericarditis, cardiomyopathy or heart failure. Early symptoms are vague: flu-like illness, fever, fatigue, chest pain or breathlessness. [S2]
- **How common:** a meta-analysis found 0.7%, but 8.5% in part of Australia with mandatory screening; reported mortality after CAM is 21–50%. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Titrate more slowly than the product information.** The week-1 target is 100 mg under Australian national and manufacturer guidance (150 mg in the EU). The consensus starts at 12.5 mg a day and prefers 50 mg by day 7 and 100–125 mg by day 14. [S2]
- **Delay the start** for at least 4 weeks after an mRNA vaccine and 2–3 weeks after a viral or bacterial infection (once the fever has settled). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Minimum monitoring:** before the first dose, hs-troponin, CRP, full blood count, vital signs and an ECG. For the first 4 weeks, hs-troponin, CRP and full blood count weekly; ECG every 2 weeks; oxygen saturation and postural blood pressure weekly; heart rate and temperature twice a week. A clozapine level at day 14 can flag slow metabolisers. Baseline echocardiography is only in the enhanced scheme. [S2]
- **Australian, New Zealand and German guidelines require weekly CRP and troponin for the first 4–6 weeks.** The consensus core is 4–8 weeks; if monitoring continues past 4 weeks, vitals weekly, CRP, hs-troponin and full blood count every 2 weeks, and one ECG after 4 weeks. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Get a cardiac MRI** if hs-troponin is above the 99th percentile, NT-proBNP is 125 pg/mL or more, or there is a significant new rise from baseline. Consider false-positive troponin (heterophile antibodies in glandular fever, fibrin clots, kidney dysfunction, pulmonary embolism, rheumatoid factor). [S2]
- **Rechallenge after CAM is off-label** and needs informed consent and a joint psychiatry, cardiology and pharmacy decision with the patient and family. It is not recommended after cardiogenic shock or ventricular arrhythmia, or if the heart has not returned to its pre-CAM baseline. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Before rechallenge:** normal hs-troponin, CRP, full blood count, vital signs and ECG, and an echocardiogram with LVEF of at least 50%; stop valproate if justified; wait at least 8 weeks after the myocarditis resolves (often 6 months or more after a severe episode). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Rechallenge titration and monitoring:** 6.25 mg on day 1, 25 mg by day 7, 50 mg by day 14 and 100 mg by the end of week 4. Check hs-troponin and CRP twice a week, full blood count and ECG weekly, oxygen saturation and postural blood pressure twice a week, and heart rate and temperature daily; if uncomplicated, step down in weeks 5–8. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Diagnosing CAM (S2 Table 1, adapted from the ESC 2023 cardio-oncology criteria for checkpoint-inhibitor myocarditis):** a new or significantly changed cardiac troponin (I or T) plus one major or two minor criteria, once acute coronary syndrome and infectious myocarditis are excluded. Major: a cardiac MRI diagnostic of acute myocarditis (modified Lake Louise criteria). Minor: a compatible clinical syndrome (such as fatigue, myalgia, chest pain, diplopia, ptosis, breathlessness, orthopnoea, leg oedema, palpitations, dizziness, syncope, weakness or cardiogenic shock); ventricular arrhythmia or new conduction disease; a fall in LV systolic function; MRI features suggestive of myocarditis; or abnormal global longitudinal strain on echocardiography. [S2]

## Side effects

- **Screen metabolic health at baseline and then every 6–12 months** from the start of antipsychotic treatment. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Metformin has the strongest evidence for preventing and treating antipsychotic weight gain; start it early, and start it together with clozapine or olanzapine.** A GLP-1 receptor agonist may be considered where accessible. Offer exercise, diet and medication review to everyone. [S1]
- **Akathisia: first lower the dose or switch drug.** If that fails, propranolol or mirtazapine may help (low-certainty evidence). [S1]
- **Tardive dyskinesia: rationalise the antipsychotic first.** VMAT-2 inhibitors (valbenazine, deutetrabenazine) work, but they are not subsidised or routinely available in Australia or New Zealand; vitamin E has low-certainty evidence. [S1]
- **Hyperprolactinaemia: after rationalising, consider adding aripiprazole.** Weigh symptoms such as gynaecomastia and sexual dysfunction against the long-term risks of breast cancer and osteoporosis. [S1]
- **Other movement side effects (S1 Table 14):** treat acute dystonia as an emergency with an anticholinergic. For parkinsonism, rationalising the antipsychotic is the mainstay; avoid anticholinergics, or use them sparingly, for a limited time and with regular review. Vitamin E may help slow further worsening of tardive dyskinesia. Metformin also lowers total cholesterol and fasting glucose. [S1]
- **Physical-health monitoring schedule (S1 Table 15):** at baseline, vital signs with postural BP and pulse, weight and BMI, FBC, electrolytes, renal and liver function, prolactin, HbA1c and lipids, thyroid tests if indicated, lifestyle risk factors, urine drug screen and ECG, and consider a pregnancy test. After starting: weight and BMI within 4 weeks; a repeat ECG, and prolactin if the drug raises it, once the therapeutic dose is reached; other cardiometabolic measures at 3 months; after 3 months, cardiometabolic measures 6-monthly or yearly, prolactin yearly on a prolactin-raising drug (considering bone density testing with prolonged untreated hyperprolactinaemia), and lifestyle risks throughout. [S1]

## Stopping antipsychotics

- **Stopping raises relapse risk, even after 6 months to 2 years of stability in first-episode psychosis.** If a person chooses to try it after shared discussion, taper gradually over at least 3–6 months with close watch for early relapse, and keep support in place for 6–12 months after stopping, or longer. [S1]

## Psychosocial and lifestyle care

- **Offer everyone psychoeducation, CBT for psychosis and relapse prevention** (learning early warning signs, symptom monitoring and a crisis plan). [S1]
- **Add targeted therapy by need:** cognitive remediation for cognitive deficits with poor functioning; metacognitive therapy for persisting positive and negative symptoms. [S1]
- **Strong recommendation: build lifestyle programmes into routine care from the first episode and first prescription**, with preventing weight gain as a key goal. [S1]
- **Smoking: varenicline, bupropion and nicotine replacement all beat placebo; varenicline has the best evidence.** [S1]

## Strength of the main recommendations (S1 Table 11)

- **Strong, drugs:** an oral second-generation antipsychotic with a favourable cardiometabolic and prolactin profile first line (aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, lurasidone); a long-acting injection offered in the maintenance phase; clozapine for treatment resistance; lifestyle and other non-drug measures at every stage; and metformin for antipsychotic weight gain, used early and begun with olanzapine or clozapine to prevent it. **Weak, drugs:** long-acting injections early in the illness; ECT for treatment resistance; switching to a more metabolically neutral drug; a GLP-1 receptor agonist for weight gain; low-dose propranolol or mirtazapine for akathisia; VMAT-2 inhibitors followed by vitamin E for tardive dyskinesia; and add-on aripiprazole for persisting, significant hyperprolactinaemia. [S1]
- **Strong, other care:** psychoeducation, CBT for psychosis, cognitive remediation (especially with cognitive difficulties or poor functioning) and metacognitive therapy (especially with persisting positive and negative symptoms); involving family, whānau or carers only by negotiated, transparent agreement with the person's consent where possible, and giving them general information; an antidepressant for comorbid depression; physical activity for symptoms, cognition, functioning, fitness and strength; multimodal lifestyle programmes for weight from the first prescription; oral-health education; and behavioural support plus varenicline, bupropion or nicotine replacement for smoking. **Weak:** relapse prevention; family-based interventions and individual or group psychoeducation for families; psychosocial help for substance use; CBT for anxiety; trauma-focused therapy for PTSD; peer-led fitness programmes; and CBT for insomnia. [S1]
