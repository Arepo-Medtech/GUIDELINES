## The infection

- **Infectious period:** from 48 hours before symptoms (or a positive test if asymptomatic) until acute symptoms settle, which may take up to 10 days; a few people, and those with severe disease or marked immunocompromise, stay infectious longer. [S1]
- **A positive NAAT or RAT does not prove someone is still infectious** — both can detect non-viable viral shedding. [S1]
- **Incubation has shortened with each variant:** 5 to 7 days (median) for ancestral strains, and a pooled mean of 3.5 days (BA.1) to 4.1 days (BA.2) for Omicron. [S1]
- **Symptoms overlap with other respiratory infections** (fever, sore throat, cough, breathlessness, loss of smell or taste, fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms); COVID-19 cannot be told apart without a test. [S1]

## Who is at risk of severe disease

- ⚠️ **Age is the strongest risk factor, especially over 70 years.** Chronic illness, immunocompromise, Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander people, residential care, disability and pregnancy also raise the risk; vaccination substantially lowers it. [S1]
- **People at increased risk should agree a COVID-19 plan with their GP in advance,** to act on if they develop respiratory symptoms or test positive. [S1]
- **Vaccination remains the most important protection against severe outcomes,** though it does little to stop infection or spread. A primary course is recommended for everyone 18 and over, and for children from 6 months at increased risk; further doses every 6–12 months for some groups. [S1]

## Testing and early treatment access

- ⚠️ **Test early if at increased risk and symptomatic** — early testing opens access to oral antiviral treatment for eligible people. [S1]
- ⚠️ **People at increased risk with acute respiratory symptoms should seek medical advice promptly;** early use of appropriate antivirals can prevent severe infection. [S1]
- **Clinical care is the treating clinician's job, not the public health unit's;** high-risk cases should be encouraged to seek medical care early. [S1]
- **NAAT (e.g. PCR) is the diagnostic gold standard;** a RAT is a faster, more accessible alternative but less sensitive, so false negatives are more common. [S1]
- **No drug is approved in Australia for pre- or post-exposure prophylaxis.** [S1]

## Isolation and contacts

- **Cases should stay home until acute symptoms resolve (usually 5 to 7 days);** they can infect others for up to 10 days after symptom onset. [S1]
- **While symptomatic:** wear a mask indoors on necessary trips out, work or study from home where possible, keep up hand and respiratory hygiene, avoid crowds and follow workplace policies. [S1]
- **Highly immunocompromised people can stay infectious for a long time;** their treating physician should guide how long they stay home. [S1]
- **Contact tracing is not routine.** Asymptomatic contacts should watch for symptoms, keep up hygiene and follow workplace advice. [S1]

## High-risk settings

- ⚠️ **Keep cases away from aged care and hospitals** until acute symptoms have resolved **and** at least 7 days have passed since onset (or since a positive test if asymptomatic); follow local timeframes. [S1]
- **Household contacts should put off visiting high-risk settings for at least 7 days after last contact;** if a visit is unavoidable, wear a surgical mask and keep distance. [S1]
- **Public health response times:** a new variant of concern within 24 hours; outbreaks in high-risk settings within one working day; other cases are routine. [S1]
