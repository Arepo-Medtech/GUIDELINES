# Chronic, cyclic and congenital neutropenia — G-CSF and supportive care

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** G-CSF indications, targets, types, doses and risks; corticosteroids and granulocyte transfusion; infection management and prophylaxis; dental care, vaccination, psychological support and stem-cell transplant for severe congenital, cyclic, autoimmune and idiopathic chronic neutropenia in children and adults. Not covered: chemotherapy-induced neutropenia (`chemotherapy-induced-neutropenia`), fever in children on cancer treatment (`febrile-neutropenia-children`), phagocyte disorders within `primary-immunodeficiencies`, and MDS (`myelodysplastic-syndromes`). ## What these conditions are

- **Chronic neutropenia means a low neutrophil count lasting more than 3 months.** It is congenital, or acquired: primary/idiopathic (including autoimmune, with antineutrophil antibodies) or secondary to another disease. [S1]
- ⚠️ **The two big risks are infection and progression to MDS or acute leukaemia.** Infections are mostly bacterial; in congenital neutropenia their risk tracks how low the count is. Malignant change is seen mainly in congenital forms and in acquired forms with clonal haematopoiesis. [S1]
- **Duffy-null (ACKR1/DARC-associated) neutropenia, common in people of African or Middle Eastern descent, does not appear to raise infection risk,** so routine G-CSF has no clear role. [S1]

## G-CSF: who benefits (ANC values in ×10⁹/L)

- **In severe congenital and cyclic neutropenia, G-CSF has transformed outcomes:** registry data show better survival, fewer infections, less antibiotic use and fewer admissions than before G-CSF. [S1]
- **In autoimmune and idiopathic neutropenia, continuous G-CSF has not been shown to improve survival or quality of life,** but it is still useful for treating acute or chronic infections; only a minority need it. [S1]
- **The aim is infection control by raising the count, while limiting bone pain and splenomegaly.** [S1]
- **How to titrate:** in congenital neutropenia, use the trough (nadir) count taken before the next injection; in autoimmune and idiopathic forms, be guided by how often and how badly infections occur. [S1]
- **Target ANC is now 1.0,** the level generally thought protective (older targets were 1.5 or higher). Up to 90% of patients across subtypes respond. [S1]

## G-CSF: which product, what dose

- **Filgrastim (or a biosimilar) is the most studied product.** Pegfilgrastim gave similar counts and infection rates with better quality of life in severe congenital neutropenia, but it has not been studied in autoimmune or idiopathic neutropenia. [S1]
- **Typical daily doses:** around or above 5 mcg/kg in severe congenital neutropenia, under 5 mcg/kg in autoimmune or idiopathic forms, and 1–5 mcg/kg (median 2.5) in cyclic neutropenia. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Needing more than 8 mcg/kg/day signals high leukaemia risk:** 15-year cumulative MDS/AL incidence was 34% versus 15% in those needing less. [S1]
- **Genetics add to the risk:** both dose and duration of G-CSF matter, and particular ELANE variants (G214R, C151Y) carry a higher risk of leukaemic evolution. [S1]
- **Shwachman–Diamond syndrome:** myeloid malignancy risk is 9.8% to almost 30% by age 30, so monitor closely and use the lowest effective G-CSF dose (1–4 mcg/kg/day). [S1]
- **Glycogen storage disease Ib: keep G-CSF doses low** because of splenic enlargement; an SGLT-2 inhibitor such as empagliflozin improves the neutropenia in GSD-Ib and G6PC3 deficiency. [S1]
- **Minimise exposure with intermittent schedules** (alternate days, once or twice weekly) fitted to the patient; in idiopathic and autoimmune forms, courses can be short and sporadic. [S1]
- **Side effects are uncommon** (rash, bone pain, osteopenia, splenomegaly, occasional vasculitis) and rarely need stopping; if daily injections are a problem, pegfilgrastim is a reasonable alternative. [S1]

## Other treatments

- **Corticosteroids** may transiently raise the count in up to half of autoimmune or idiopathic cases (weak evidence); there is no evidence for them in severe congenital or cyclic neutropenia. [S1]
- **Granulocyte transfusion has no evidence base** in congenital, idiopathic or autoimmune neutropenia. [S1]
- **Stem-cell transplant is the only cure** and is considered for high-risk genetic or cytogenetic changes or poor responders needing more than 10 mcg/kg/day. Transplant mortality was 17% in the largest series; results are better under 10 years of age and after the year 2000. [S1]

## Infections and prophylaxis

- ⚠️ **Treat fever as for neutropenia after leukaemia chemotherapy:** blood cultures at onset; empirical piperacillin–tazobactam (especially with abdominal symptoms) or cefepime alone, keeping meropenem for non-response or resistance. [S1]
- **Clinically stable patients can have oral therapy** (amoxicillin–clavulanate or levofloxacin), adjusted to local resistance patterns. [S1]
- **Severe infection can kill even on G-CSF:** lethal sepsis reached 10% after 15 years of treatment in severe congenital neutropenia. [S1]
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis only for recurrent infections, decided case by case;** routine antifungal prophylaxis is not recommended. [S1]

## Supportive care

- **Regular professional dental care** is needed: untreated or refractory neutropenia causes chronic periodontal disease. [S1]
- **Vaccinate on the national schedule, including live vaccines,** when there is no other immune defect; ask an immunologist if there is. [S1]
