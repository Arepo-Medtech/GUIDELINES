# Chronic, cyclic and congenital neutropenia — G-CSF and supportive care

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** Definitions, work-up, genetic and antibody testing, bone-marrow surveillance, transition and pregnancy (S2); G-CSF indications, targets, types, doses and risks; corticosteroids and granulocyte transfusion; infection management and prophylaxis; dental care, vaccination, psychological support and stem-cell transplant for severe congenital, cyclic, autoimmune and idiopathic chronic neutropenia in children and adults. Not covered: chemotherapy-induced neutropenia (`chemotherapy-induced-neutropenia`), fever in children on cancer treatment (`febrile-neutropenia-children`), phagocyte disorders within `primary-immunodeficiencies`, and MDS (`myelodysplastic-syndromes`). PBS access (filgrastim) is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## What these conditions are

- **Chronic neutropenia means a low neutrophil count lasting more than 3 months.** It is congenital, or acquired: primary/idiopathic (including autoimmune, with antineutrophil antibodies) or secondary to another disease. [S1]
- ⚠️ **The two big risks are infection and progression to MDS or acute leukaemia.** Infections are mostly bacterial; in congenital neutropenia their risk tracks how low the count is. Malignant change is seen mainly in congenital forms and in acquired forms with clonal haematopoiesis. [S1]
- **Duffy-null (ACKR1/DARC-associated) neutropenia, common in people of African or Middle Eastern descent, does not appear to raise infection risk,** so routine G-CSF has no clear role. [S1]
- **Thresholds vary with age and ancestry (ANC in billions of cells per litre):** below 1.0 in infants up to 1 year (2.5 for term neonates 72–240 hours after birth; 1.0 if preterm), below 1.5 from age 1 to adulthood, and below 1.8 in Caucasian adults (WHO). Grading: mild 1.0–1.5 (1.8 in adults), moderate 0.5–1.0, severe below 0.5; agranulocytosis below 0.2 carries a high risk of life-threatening infection. [S2]
- **S2's age table:** neutropenia means an ANC below 1.0 from 14 days to 1 year, below 1.5 from over 1 year to adulthood, and below 1.8 in adults; a count under 1.5 is regarded as normal in people of African and Middle Eastern ancestry. [S2]
- **Some people of African or Middle Eastern descent normally run an ANC of 0.5–1.5, occasionally lower,** because of the Duffy-null ACKR1/DARC polymorphism; S2 prefers the name ACKR1/DARC-associated neutropenia (ADAN) to 'ethnic neutropenia'. Confirming it genetically avoids an unnecessary work-up, and testing for it is reasonable in anyone with chronic mild neutropenia. [S2]
- **Childhood autoimmune or idiopathic neutropenia that has not resolved by 24–36 months, or that begins after age 3, needs a full search for a genetic cause:** some of these children have low B and NK cells or variants in immune-dysregulation genes. [S2]

## Investigate (after specialist referral)

- **First-line tests,** once ADAN and post-infectious or drug-induced causes seem unlikely: FBC and film, liver and kidney function, immunoglobulins, CRP, B12 and folate, lymphocyte subsets, viral serology (hepatitis B and C, HIV, EBV, CMV, parvovirus), indirect antineutrophil antibodies, and thyroid function and antibodies. Children add double-negative TCR-α/β T cells; adults add antiphospholipid antibodies, LGL/TCR clonality, ferritin, autoimmune serology and ESR. [S2]
- **Second line, if first-line tests are inconclusive:** counts in family members, twice-weekly counts for 6 weeks to exclude cyclic neutropenia, copper and caeruloplasmin, coeliac serology and pancreatic isoamylase; adults add serum electrophoresis, complement and a myeloid-malignancy NGS panel. Genetic testing is strongly advised for children, young adults and selected adults, and brought forward to follow first-line tests in young children with a family history, typical anomalies or repeated severe infections. [S2]
- **Genetic approach:** Sanger-sequence ELANE first (mutated in about 45% of severe congenital neutropenia) or a gene the picture points to (TAZ with cardiomyopathy, G6PC3 with heart and genitourinary malformations, SBDS with poor growth, malabsorption and bone changes). Otherwise use an NGS panel of all 30-plus known genes, ideally as a trio, then exome, genome or RNA sequencing. Screen relatives for known variants, and always screen related stem-cell donors. [S2]
- **Antineutrophil antibodies are a first-line test in children and adults,** by indirect GIFT in a reference laboratory. A positive result supports autoimmune neutropenia but excludes nothing, including genetically proven congenital neutropenia; if it is negative and suspicion stays high, repeat it several times. In adults, rule out HLA class I alloantibodies (common after transfusion or pregnancy) before trusting a positive result. [S2]

## Bone marrow and surveillance

- ⚠️ **Diagnostic bone marrow (morphology, cytogenetics, myeloid NGS):** in children with severe or moderate chronic neutropenia (except antibody-positive primary autoimmune and drug-induced cases); in suspected autoimmune neutropenia with negative antibodies and recurrent infections; before any G-CSF; and in every adult with unexplained chronic neutropenia unless it is long-standing, mild, isolated and stable. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Surveillance:** a yearly marrow with cytogenetics in every congenital marrow-failure syndrome, whatever the ANC or G-CSF use (consider it in undefined severe chronic neutropenia on G-CSF); a repeat marrow if the ANC falls or other counts or red-cell indices change; and an FBC with differential and film every 3–4 months in all chronic neutropenia. [S2]
- **Clonal change in adults:** with one or more MDS/leukaemia-gene mutations, especially at a variant allele frequency above 10% and in younger patients, check counts and film more than 4 times a year, and do a full marrow work-up if cytopenias worsen, macrocytosis appears or morphology changes. Warning signs of transformation are dysplasia, trisomy 21 or monosomy 7, somatic CSF3R, RUNX1 or ASXL1 mutations, and biallelic TP53 in Shwachman–Diamond syndrome. [S2]
- **Transition: as patients with congenital neutropenia near adulthood, hand their care to a dedicated haematologist.** [S2]

## G-CSF: who benefits (ANC values in ×10⁹/L)

- **In severe congenital and cyclic neutropenia, G-CSF has transformed outcomes:** registry data show better survival, fewer infections, less antibiotic use and fewer admissions than before G-CSF. [S1]
- **In autoimmune and idiopathic neutropenia, continuous G-CSF has not been shown to improve survival or quality of life,** but it is still useful for treating acute or chronic infections; only a minority need it. [S1]
- **Consensus boxes (S1):** G-CSF is recommended in severe congenital and cyclic neutropenia, particularly with recurrent or severe infections (100% agreement). In idiopathic or autoimmune neutropenia, do not give continuous G-CSF because of the count alone: decide individually on how often and how badly infections occur, keep continuous use for the rare patient with frequent or severe infections, and consider occasional use during a severe episode. Do not use G-CSF in ACKR1/DARC-associated neutropenia, which carries no extra infection risk. [S1]
- **The aim is infection control by raising the count, while limiting bone pain and splenomegaly.** [S1]
- **How to titrate:** in congenital neutropenia, use the trough (nadir) count taken before the next injection; in autoimmune and idiopathic forms, be guided by how often and how badly infections occur. [S1]
- **Protective threshold (S1 box): an ANC above 1.0 × 10⁹/L, with a lower nadir acceptable in cyclic neutropenia.** Give the lowest G-CSF dose that controls infection (usually one that brings the ANC to 1.0 or more), to limit marrow stimulation, bone pain and splenomegaly. An ANC above 5.0 is usually not needed, although peaks run higher in cyclic neutropenia. [S1]
- **Target ANC is now 1.0,** the level generally thought protective (older targets were 1.5 or higher). Up to 90% of patients across subtypes respond. [S1]

## G-CSF: which product, what dose

- **Filgrastim (or a biosimilar) is the most studied product.** Pegfilgrastim gave similar counts and infection rates with better quality of life in severe congenital neutropenia, but it has not been studied in autoimmune or idiopathic neutropenia. [S1]
- **Products (S1 box):** filgrastim and lenograstim can be used interchangeably in severe congenital neutropenia; pegfilgrastim is an option when adherence is poor. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Starting doses (S1 box):** severe congenital neutropenia, the standard 5 mcg/kg/day. Cyclic neutropenia, lower: 3 mcg/kg/day or less continuously, or on alternate days, adjusted to avoid a nadir below 0.5 × 10⁹/L and mouth ulcers, fevers or infections. Shwachman–Diamond syndrome and GSD-Ib: the minimal dose that prevents infection. Autoimmune or idiopathic neutropenia (no study has set a dose): if needed, start at 1 mcg/kg/day. [S1]
- **Typical daily doses:** around or above 5 mcg/kg in severe congenital neutropenia, under 5 mcg/kg in autoimmune or idiopathic forms, and 1–5 mcg/kg (median 2.5) in cyclic neutropenia. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Needing more than 8 mcg/kg/day signals high leukaemia risk:** 15-year cumulative MDS/AL incidence was 34% versus 15% in those needing less. [S1]
- **Genetics add to the risk:** both dose and duration of G-CSF matter, and particular ELANE variants (G214R, C151Y) carry a higher risk of leukaemic evolution. [S1]
- **Shwachman–Diamond syndrome:** myeloid malignancy risk is 9.8% to almost 30% by age 30, so monitor closely and use the lowest effective G-CSF dose (1–4 mcg/kg/day). [S1]
- **Glycogen storage disease Ib: keep G-CSF doses low** because of splenic enlargement; an SGLT-2 inhibitor such as empagliflozin improves the neutropenia in GSD-Ib and G6PC3 deficiency. [S1]
- **Minimise exposure with intermittent schedules** (alternate days, once or twice weekly) fitted to the patient; in idiopathic and autoimmune forms, courses can be short and sporadic. [S1]
- **Sporadic or continuous (S1 box):** sporadic G-CSF suits patients (mostly autoimmune or idiopathic) who stay infection-free without it but need a temporary rise in count; continuous G-CSF is for those at persistent risk of severe infection from an intrinsic defect of neutrophil production. In autoimmune or idiopathic neutropenia on G-CSF, test for spontaneous remission by carefully pausing it for 1–3 weeks. [S1]
- **Side effects are uncommon** (rash, bone pain, osteopenia, splenomegaly, occasional vasculitis) and rarely need stopping; if daily injections are a problem, pegfilgrastim is a reasonable alternative. [S1]

## Other treatments

- **Corticosteroids** may transiently raise the count in up to half of autoimmune or idiopathic cases (weak evidence); there is no evidence for them in severe congenital or cyclic neutropenia. [S1]
- **Granulocyte transfusion has no evidence base** in congenital, idiopathic or autoimmune neutropenia. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Transplant indications (S1 box; weigh them case by case with the family, and consider the centre's experience):** *strong* — transformation to MDS or acute leukaemia; dysplasia with high-risk acquired cytogenetics (monosomy 7, trisomy 8 or 21) or several leukaemia-associated somatic mutations (such as RUNX1, ASXL1, SETBP1; CSF3R alone is not an indication); inherently high-risk genetics (GATA2, high-risk ELANE variants, biallelic TP53 clones in SDS); no response to G-CSF (over 20 mcg/kg/day needed to reach an ANC of 1.0), poor response (10–20 mcg/kg/day without reaching it) or poorly controlled infection at any dose. *Potential* — infections controlled on intermediate doses (10–15 mcg/kg/day) with an HLA-identical sibling or matched donor. *Weak* — response at up to 10 mcg/kg/day, good tolerance and adherence, infections controlled and no matched donor. [S1]
- **Stem-cell transplant is the only cure** and is considered for high-risk genetic or cytogenetic changes or poor responders needing more than 10 mcg/kg/day. Transplant mortality was 17% in the largest series; results are better under 10 years of age and after the year 2000. [S1]

## Infections and prophylaxis

- ⚠️ **Treat fever as for neutropenia after leukaemia chemotherapy:** blood cultures at onset; empirical piperacillin–tazobactam (especially with abdominal symptoms) or cefepime alone, keeping meropenem for non-response or resistance. [S1]
- **Clinically stable patients can have oral therapy** (amoxicillin–clavulanate or levofloxacin), adjusted to local resistance patterns. [S1]
- **Severe infection can kill even on G-CSF:** lethal sepsis reached 10% after 15 years of treatment in severe congenital neutropenia. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Assess infections promptly and treat at once with broad-spectrum antibiotics, admitting if needed;** pick agents by local policy, hospital flora, resistance patterns and any known colonisation. The S1 consensus box suggests avoiding antibiotic prophylaxis in every type of neutropenia (87% agreement), given no efficacy data and the risk of resistance. [S1]
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis only for recurrent infections, decided case by case;** routine antifungal prophylaxis is not recommended. [S1]

## Supportive care

- **Regular professional dental care** is needed: untreated or refractory neutropenia causes chronic periodontal disease. [S1]
- **Dental (S1 box):** periodontal inflammation is more common in severe congenital and cyclic neutropenia, so arrange frequent periodontal checks and good oral hygiene, and aim for an ANC of at least 1.0 × 10⁹/L for dental health. [S1]
- **Psychological support is strongly advised** to improve quality of life, with dedicated support and rehabilitation for patients with neurodevelopmental disabilities (S1 box). [S1]
- **Vaccinate on the national schedule, including live vaccines,** when there is no other immune defect; ask an immunologist if there is. [S1]
- **Vaccines (S1 box):** advise every patient with chronic neutropenia to have an approved influenza and COVID-19 vaccine, and consider immunising close contacts against all vaccine-preventable diseases. [S1]
- **Pregnancy:** give G-CSF to women already on it and to all with severe congenital neutropenia (offer it when the ANC is below 0.5), and consider it for untreated women; published experience finds G-CSF safe and well tolerated throughout pregnancy. Check the ANC often, especially in autoimmune neutropenia where counts can rise, offer genetic counselling before conception, and check the baby's neutrophil count after birth. [S2]
