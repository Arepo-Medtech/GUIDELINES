# Primary hyperoxaluria (focus on type 1)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosing primary hyperoxaluria (PH) and managing PH1 across kidney function: urine and plasma oxalate testing, genetics, hydration, citrate, pyridoxine, RNAi therapy (lumasiran), dialysis, transplantation, stone surgery and follow-up. ## Recognise

- **PH is a group of recessive disorders that overproduce oxalate,** causing stones and nephrocalcinosis; more than 70% of patients reach kidney failure. Once GFR drops below 30–40 mL/min/1.73 m², oxalate builds up in bone, heart, vessels, nerves and eyes. [S1]
- **Type 1 is the commonest and most severe.** Infantile oxalosis (kidney failure before age 1) is the worst form; more than 50% of infants diagnosed with PH1 already have stage 5 CKD, often with poor feeding and failure to thrive. Manage them in highly specialised paediatric centres. [S1]

## Diagnose

- ⚠️ **Everyone suspected of PH should have genetic testing,** the gold standard, because the type and genotype drive treatment. With eGFR under 30, get the result within 30 days. [S1]
- **With eGFR above 30, measure urine oxalate at least twice** (a 24-hour collection is preferred, or a spot sample corrected for creatinine). Above 1 mmol/1.73 m² per day strongly suggests PH. Rule out enteric causes (for example pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, bowel disease, bariatric surgery) first. [S1]
- **EAU urine oxalate bands (adults):** over 0.40 mmol a day confirms hyperoxaluria. Primary hyperoxaluria mostly exceeds 1 mmol a day; secondary (enteric or dietary) hyperoxaluria is usually between 0.5 and 1; 0.40–0.50 is the mild form common in idiopathic calcium oxalate stone formers. [S2]
- **In kidney failure, use plasma oxalate, not urine,** and compare results with dialysis patients who do not have PH rather than with healthy people. Plasma assays vary a lot between laboratories. [S1]

## Conservative treatment (all patients)

- **Hydration is the cornerstone:** at least 2–3 L/m² body surface area a day in children (a gastrostomy may be needed in infants), and 3.5–4 L a day in adults per EAU advice. [S1]
- **Add citrate,** though the evidence is weak (one small study used 0.1–0.15 g/kg). [S1]
- **A strict low-oxalate diet is not advised,** but limit very high-oxalate foods such as spinach, rhubarb, chocolate and nuts. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Start pyridoxine (vitamin B6) in everyone with suspected PH or proven PH1,** up to 5 mg/kg (higher doses risk neurotoxicity). Responsiveness is a fall in urine oxalate of more than 30%, judged on at least two samples after at least 2 weeks; if responsive, taper to the lowest effective dose. [S1]
- **EAU: pyridoxine for PH1 at 5–20 mg/kg a day,** set by the urine oxalate response and tolerance; its drug table gives a 5 mg/kg/day start, a 20 mg/kg/day maximum, and sensory peripheral neuropathy as the main risk. In about one-third of PH1 patients it normalises or markedly lowers urine oxalate. [S2]
- **EAU fluids and citrate:** adults 3.5–4.0 L a day and children 1.5 L/m2 of body surface, drunk on a round-the-clock schedule; alkaline citrate 3.25–9.75 g a day in adults (the children's dose is printed as 0.1–0.15 mg/kg a day; see Unresolved). Refer every patient to a specialised centre with an experienced interdisciplinary team. [S2]

## RNAi therapy (lumasiran)

- ⚠️ **RNAi therapy is indicated when genetics show pyridoxine-unresponsive PH1,** at any eGFR. Partial pyridoxine responders may qualify if hyperoxaluria persists (or, with eGFR under 30, depending on plasma oxalate). [S1]
- **Lumasiran silences glycolate oxidase.** In the Illuminate A trial (age over 6), urine oxalate fell by 65% versus 11% on placebo, and fifty-two per cent normalised. Children under 6 (Illuminate B) had a 72% fall in the oxalate-to-creatinine ratio. Side effects were minor injection-site reactions. [S1]
- **In reduced kidney function (Illuminate C, eGFR under 45),** plasma oxalate fell by an average of 42% in patients on dialysis. [S1]
- **Monitor on RNAi therapy (eGFR above 30):** every 3–6 months in year one, then 6-monthly for 5 years, then yearly: urine oxalate, glycolate, citrate, calcium and creatinine, kidney function, electrolytes, liver enzymes, bone and heart imaging and an eye check. With eGFR under 30 or on dialysis, check plasma oxalate and bloods every 3 months. [S1]
- **Long-term effect on kidney outcomes is still unproven:** RNAi drugs clearly lower oxalate production, but longer data are needed on stones and kidney failure. [S1]
- **EAU: prescribe lumasiran for PH type 1 that does not respond to pyridoxine** (strong; evidence level 1b that it lowers urine oxalate and stone recurrence). It may also stabilise GFR and improve nephrocalcinosis. Nedosiran was not yet EMA-approved when the EAU wrote this. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Lumasiran dosing (EAU), by subcutaneous injection:** loading doses once a month for three months, 6 mg/kg below 10 kg and at 10–20 kg, and 3 mg/kg above 20 kg; maintenance from one month later, 3 mg/kg monthly below 10 kg, 6 mg/kg every three months at 10–20 kg, and 3 mg/kg every three months above 20 kg. [S2]

## Kidney failure, dialysis and transplant

- **Consider dialysis early in stage 4–5 CKD,** before uraemia, when plasma oxalate stays high despite treatment or there are signs of systemic oxalosis. Standard dialysis removes far less oxalate than PH1 produces. [S1]
- **Prefer intensive haemodialysis over peritoneal dialysis** (high-flux, maximal blood flow, daily if tolerated, with more sessions rather than longer ones), aiming for pre-dialysis plasma oxalate of about 50–70 μmol/L. Watch phosphate closely. [S1]
- **Liver transplant is the only cure for PH1;** remove the native liver (auxiliary grafts are obsolete). In kidney failure, combined liver–kidney transplant gives much better kidney-graft survival than kidney alone (87% versus 14% at 15 years). Kidney-only transplant is an option for pyridoxine responders who normalise urine oxalate. [S1]
- **Skipping liver transplant on lumasiran is a careful, case-by-case decision** with intensive monitoring. Lumasiran does not clear oxalate already stored in the body, so after a kidney-only graft keep up hyperhydration and alkalinisation. [S1]

## Stones, surgery and follow-up

- **For stone removal, percutaneous nephrolithotomy has worked best in PH1;** shock-wave lithotripsy gives poor stone-free rates. Otherwise follow EAU stone algorithms. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Keep patients hyperhydrated throughout any operation and avoid pre-operative fluid fasting:** even mild dehydration can cause acute kidney injury. [S1]
- **Never discharge from follow-up.** Image the kidneys 6-monthly for 2 years, then at least yearly; ultrasound at least yearly before stage 5D, avoiding radiation where possible. [S1]
- **Routine checks on conventional therapy:** urine oxalate and creatinine every 3–12 months (plasma oxalate only in CKD 4 or worse), growth, bone chemistry, thyroid and vitamin D yearly, and yearly eye and heart checks when eGFR is under 30 or on dialysis. [S1]
