# Chronic disabling spasticity in adults

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising disabling spasticity from an upper motor neurone lesion (stroke, brain injury, cerebral palsy, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis) in adults, first-line physical management, and choosing between intrathecal baclofen (ITB), botulinum toxin type A (BoNT A) or both once oral antispasticity drugs fail or are not tolerated. The source does **not** give doses or a sequence for oral baclofen, dantrolene or diazepam, which are the PBS drugs for this condition. Related: `multiple-sclerosis`, `cerebral-palsy-children`. Botulinum toxin PBS conditions (upper-limb and post-acute-event spasticity) are separate listings. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **Spasticity is raised muscle tone caused by an upper motor neurone lesion.** Brain causes include stroke, acute brain injury and cerebral palsy; spinal causes include multiple sclerosis and spinal cord injury. [S1]
- **Drug treatment is only for disabling spasticity**: spasticity that the patient or their carers feel is getting in the way of body function, activities or participation. Spasticity that causes no problem should not be treated with drugs, but any grade, mild to severe, can become disabling. [S1]
- **Why it matters:** spasticity commonly brings pain, poor sleep, feeding problems and difficulty with positioning, transfers, dressing and self-care. Treatment aims to protect function and to prevent contractures and skin breakdown. [S1]

## First-line management

- **Start with a multidisciplinary physical management programme**, led by physiotherapy and occupational therapy: posture, positioning with equipment and splints, stretching and standing. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Look for and treat anything that is driving the spasticity**, such as bladder or bowel problems and damaged skin. [S1]
- **Oral antispasticity drugs are usually tried first**, but they often fail or cause side effects. The next step is then botulinum toxin type A (BoNT A) or intrathecal baclofen (ITB). [S1]

## Choosing ITB or botulinum toxin

- ⚠️ **The distribution of spasticity drives the choice.** Focal or segmental disabling spasticity is best treated with BoNT (98.7% consensus). Multi-segmental or generalised disabling spasticity that has not responded to oral drugs is best treated with ITB (96.1% consensus). [S1]
- **Multi-focal spasticity** (non-adjacent joints, such as ankle and hip, or wrist and shoulder) was also placed in the BoNT arm; the advisory board was unanimous. [S1]
- **Lower limbs only:** ITB is **not** recommended when only one leg is affected (87.0% negative consensus); that is focal or segmental disease, so use BoNT with walking aids, splints and rehabilitation, partly because ITB may weaken the unaffected side. When both legs are affected, ITB is a good option (97.4%), and when both legs plus back or abdominal muscles are affected, all respondents agreed (100%). [S1]
- **Arms and legs together:** when both sides are affected, ITB is the treatment of choice. When one side is affected (hemiplegia), ITB is supported only weakly and BoNT alone may also help. [S1]
- **Arms only, both sides:** BoNT rather than ITB. One arm alone falls in the focal arm, where BoNT is also first choice. [S1]
- **Combine ITB with BoNT** in generalised or multi-segmental spasticity when ITB alone does not meet the patient's goals (over 75% agreement). If arm goals are unmet after starting ITB, add BoNT early for the arms. [S1]
- **After BoNT, ITB is a second-line option** (90.9% consensus) when rehabilitation goals are still unmet after a trial of oral medication. [S1]
- **What tips the choice towards ITB:** the benefit-risk balance (84%), whether the patient will adhere to treatment (79%), BoNT needs reaching the maximum recommended dose (77%), and the patient's goals and expectations (76%). [S1]

## Intrathecal baclofen

- **ITB is delivered by an implanted programmable pump and catheter**, using under 1% of the oral dose directly at the spinal cord. Its best-proven effect is on leg spasticity of cerebral or spinal origin after maximum tolerated or recommended oral antispasmodics have failed. [S1]
- **ITB acts less on the arms and trunk:** roughly one-third of its leg effect, more at higher doses and depending on where the catheter tip sits. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Before ITB, the patient, family and carers must be engaged and willing** to have a test-dose trial, the implant operation, and regular follow-up for pump refills. [S1]
- ⚠️ **ITB has a relatively high complication rate:** infection, cerebrospinal fluid leak and catheter problems in particular. [S1]
- **Walking does not rule out ITB.** It has traditionally been kept for people who cannot walk, but early ITB may help people keep walking for longer, particularly in progressive disease such as multiple sclerosis. That group has more catheter complications. [S1]

## Botulinum toxin and timing

- **BoNT dose limits constrain widespread spasticity.** Multi-focal or multi-segmental treatment may need more toxin per session than the licensed doses, and higher doses raise concern about systemic spread and neutralising antibodies. [S1]
- **Start BoNT early:** most experts give it straight away (31.2%) or within 3 months of disabling spasticity starting (49.4%), to prevent secondary complications. [S1]
- ⚠️ **ITB is usually started later** (at least 4–6 months, or over 6 months), but that delay is inappropriate when the patient is losing range of movement, especially with dysautonomia after brain injury, where ITB helps. [S1]

## Monitor and decide together

- **Measure outcomes:** the Ashworth or modified Ashworth scale for tone and spasms, the visual analogue scale or Numeric Pain Rating Scale for pain, the Barthel Index for function, and the Goal Attainment Scale for goals. [S1]
- **Use shared decision-making at every step**, taking patient and carer preferences into account (96.1% agreed). The algorithm supports clinical judgement; it does not replace it. [S1]
