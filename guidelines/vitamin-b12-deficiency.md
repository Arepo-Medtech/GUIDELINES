# Vitamin B12 deficiency in adults

## Recognise

- **Symptoms are varied and mostly nonspecific**, and patients usually present in primary care. Neurological involvement affects about 30-50%; nervous-system and mental symptoms are the commonest and the hardest to link to B12. [S1]
- ⚠️ **A normal blood count does not exclude it:** macrocytic anaemia does not track the presence or severity of neurological symptoms. [S1]
- **Malabsorption is the main cause in adults**: pernicious anaemia, atrophic gastritis and other gut disease. Other risks include older age (food-cobalamin malabsorption), Crohn's and coeliac disease, H. pylori, gastric surgery and long-term PPIs. [S1]
- **Metformin lowers B12 from as early as 3 months** and is linked to more neuropathy. Up to 70% of patients after gastrectomy become deficient within 12-24 months without supplements. [S1]
- **Take a targeted history:** diet low in animal foods, past illnesses, regular medicines and recreational gas use (the source says 'nitric oxide'; nitrous oxide is the recognised cause). [S1]
- **In Australia, 5.2–6.3% of people over 50 are deficient,** and people of refugee background from Afghanistan, Bhutan, Iran, Iraq or Sudan are at particular risk (20–30%). In severe cases the spinal cord can be damaged (subacute combined degeneration), and this may be permanent even once B12 is restored. [S2]

## Diagnose

- **Serum B12 below 148 pmol/L suggests frank deficiency; 148-220 (or 260) pmol/L suggests mild deficiency.** But 30-40% of people with B12-related symptoms have a normal serum B12. [S1]
- **The GP can start with history, examination, a full blood count and serum B12**, adding advanced markers (for example intrinsic factor antibodies with gut symptoms) when indicated, then begin treatment after the preliminary work-up. [S1]
- **Methylmalonic acid and homocysteine help** to confirm deficiency and to guide treatment, and can clarify intracellular B12 status when serum B12 is unexpectedly high. [S1]
- **Suspect malabsorption** with autoimmune disease, previous gastric surgery, a family history of pernicious anaemia, or positive intrinsic factor or parietal cell antibodies. Check folate and iron too. [S1]
- **A high serum B12 with a deficiency picture** may reflect unreported supplements, assay interference, renal or liver disease, or malignancy. [S1]
- **When to test (Australia):** only with a reasonable clinical suspicion from risk factors or findings; to explain a blood count or film abnormality (macrocytic anaemia, pancytopenia, hypersegmented neutrophils); with unexplained neurological or psychiatric symptoms even without anaemia; and reasonably in older adults being worked up for cognitive decline. [S2]
- **Do not test** people without symptoms or risk factors, the general population, people already on B12 (unless checking adherence or suspected malabsorption), or anyone with a normal result in the past 12 months unless their condition has changed. Medicare rebates each B12, active B12, methylmalonic acid and homocysteine test only once every 11 months, with a separate item for patients needing more frequent tests. [S2]
- **Which test:** total serum B12 first (cheapest); a low value confirms deficiency with no further testing. In pregnancy, and when haptocorrin is low (pregnancy, oestrogen-containing drugs), use active B12 (holotranscobalamin). Total B12 can be falsely normal or high with inflammation, myeloproliferative neoplasms, liver disease or anti-intrinsic factor antibody interference. [S2]
- **Normal methylmalonic acid and homocysteine exclude deficiency;** raised values are not specific: homocysteine rises with folate or B6 deficiency, CKD, hypothyroidism and MTHFR variants, and methylmalonic acid with CKD, though it is usually normal in folate deficiency. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Suspected nitrous oxide use: test homocysteine or methylmalonic acid first,** because nitrous oxide inactivates B12 and total and active B12 can look normal despite tissue deficiency. [S2]
- **Suspect autoimmune gastritis** in unexplained deficiency, especially with upper GI symptoms or other autoimmune disease: test anti-intrinsic factor antibodies; if negative, consider parietal cell antibodies, gastrin and gastroscopy. If the cause is still unknown, test for coeliac disease and *H. pylori*. [S2]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **With suspected severe deficiency, start treatment without delay**, but draw blood for B12 markers first. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Acute or severe symptoms call for parenteral B12 first**, typically injections for several weeks initially. Prefer oral treatment where injections are contraindicated, for example on anticoagulants. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Dose:** there is no gold standard. For manifest deficiency or malabsorption, 1000-2000 µg/day orally or 1000 µg IM (for example daily, then weekly, then monthly for maintenance). In subacute combined degeneration, larger, more frequent doses work better. [S1]
- **Maintenance in malabsorption:** 1000-1500 µg/day orally or 1000 µg IM every 1-3 months keeps B12 in range. Low-dose supplements (3-6 µg/day) or 1000 µg orally once a week will not correct it. High-dose oral and IM are likely equivalent. [S1]
- **Long-term route should balance treatment goals with patient preference.** If treatment fails, reconsider the diagnosis and the dose, and consider parenteral B12, especially after oral treatment or if serum B12 has not normalised. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Australian advice: lifelong intramuscular B12 when the cause is pernicious anaemia, autoimmune gastritis, a total gastrectomy or resection of the terminal ileum,** because absorption is permanently impaired and oral B12 is unreliable even at high doses. This is more cautious than S1, which treats high-dose oral B12 as likely equivalent in malabsorption; S2 notes a 2024 meta-analysis finding oral and sublingual B12 as effective as injections but calls for better trials. [S2]
- **Indeterminate levels with risk factors:** a trial of B12 can be considered after careful assessment, with review and deprescribing where appropriate. Choose route, dose and duration by cause, severity, cost and preference. [S2]

## Monitor and prevent

- **Judge response mainly on symptoms.** No improvement after 4-8 weeks for anaemia or 6-12 months for neurological signs suggests another cause or an inadequate dose or route. A slow early response should not by itself stop treatment. [S1]
- **Recovery can be incomplete:** up to 25% keep severe neurological symptoms despite normal blood markers, and sensory symptoms can take up to one year to settle. Longer, more severe deficiency before treatment means slower and less complete recovery. [S1]
- **Recheck serum B12 case by case** (new symptoms, adherence or absorption of oral therapy); it tracks clinical response poorly. Periodic B12 checks can help find at-risk people such as long-term metformin users. [S1]
- **Give prophylactic B12** with atrophic gastritis, after bariatric surgery, with illnesses or medicines that cause deficiency, and with little or no animal food. Women with previous or at-risk deficiency should take it from before pregnancy to the end of breastfeeding. [S1]
- **Expected recovery (S2):** symptoms usually start improving within 2 weeks; reticulocytes rise in 3–5 days, haemoglobin normalises in 4–8 weeks, and neurological symptoms improve gradually over 6–12 weeks. [S2]
- **On oral B12, review at 2–3 months** for symptoms and adherence, and consider repeating B12, methylmalonic acid or homocysteine at 3–6 months, especially with malabsorption. Stop B12 only if a reversible cause has been found and corrected, and ask patients to return if symptoms recur. [S2]
