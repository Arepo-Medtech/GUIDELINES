# Haemorrhagic cystitis: prevention with mesna and hydration, and management

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** treatment-related haemorrhagic cystitis in adults having cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, blood and marrow transplant or pelvic radiotherapy: who is at risk, assessment and grading, prevention (hydration and mesna, including mesna dosing with ifosfamide and high-dose cyclophosphamide), treatment of established cystitis and patient education. Radiation-induced cystitis has its own eviQ page (ID 1764) and is covered here only briefly. Infective cystitis: see `uti-acute-cystitis-women`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Who is at risk

- **Cyclophosphamide and ifosfamide are the commonest cause.** The liver converts both to acrolein, which the kidneys excrete; the longer acrolein sits against the bladder lining, the more it injures the urothelium. [S1]
- **About 20–25% of patients develop it in the weeks or months after cyclophosphamide**, and the risk climbs with larger single and cumulative doses. [S1]
- **Other causes include** busulfan, cabazitaxel, antithymocyte globulin, intravesical therapy, pelvic radiotherapy, and infection (BK virus, adenovirus, CMV) in immunosuppressed allogeneic transplant recipients. [S1]
- **Roughly 30% of blood and marrow transplant patients get HC**, mostly after allogeneic transplant. Early-onset HC (within 72 hours of finishing conditioning) is prevented with mesna and hyperhydration; late-onset HC (from about day +30) is usually a viral reactivation. [S1]
- **Low-dose oral cyclophosphamide still carries risk:** take the dose as early in the day as possible so less active metabolite stays in the bladder overnight, drink well, empty the bladder often, and act on symptoms promptly. [S1]
- **Radiation-induced HC can appear years later:** onset averages about 31.8 months after pelvic radiotherapy, with a range from 6 months to beyond 10 years. [S1]

## Recognise and assess

- **Symptoms:** blood in the urine (microscopic to visible, with or without clots), painful, frequent or urgent voiding, incontinence, night-time voiding and suprapubic or vague abdominal pain. Anyone with symptoms should be seen by a medical officer. [S1]
- **Work-up:** examine the lower abdomen and do a post-void bladder scan, take a medication history, send urinalysis and culture to rule out infection, arrange cystoscopy and/or cytology to rule out malignancy, and check a full blood count. [S1]
- ⚠️ **CTCAE grading:** grade 3 is visible haematuria needing transfusion, IV medicines or admission; grade 4 has life-threatening consequences and needs urgent invasive intervention. [S1]

## Prevention

- **Prevention aims to dilute acrolein and shorten its contact with the bladder:** IV or oral hydration or hyperhydration, mesna, voiding often and at the first urge (sitting or standing rather than lying down), frusemide if urine production falls below 100 mL/m²/hr, and urine output kept above 100 mL/hr. [S1]
- **No clear guidance exists for preventing radiation-induced HC.** [S1]

## Mesna

- **Mesna binds acrolein**, is cleared quickly by the kidneys and leaves the anticancer action of both drugs intact. Routes: by mouth or IV, as a bolus or an infusion. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Mesna has to be in the bladder already when the chemotherapy is given.** Keep it going for the whole chemotherapy and for 12 to 24 hours afterwards, until the toxic metabolites have fallen away. [S1]
- **Dosing is not standardised, and the evidence is old:** ASCO last updated its mesna-with-ifosfamide advice in 2008, and no randomised trials had appeared since its 2002 version. In 2013 the eviQ Haematology Reference Committee chose to match mesna to ifosfamide at 1:1 in its protocols; other doses may be reasonable. [S1]
- **Ifosfamide under 2.5 g/m²/day as a short infusion:** the day's IV mesna totals 60% of the ifosfamide dose, split into three boluses: 15 minutes before each ifosfamide dose, then at 4 hours and at 8 hours after it. [S1]
- **Ifosfamide under 2.5 g/m²/day by continuous infusion:** an IV mesna bolus worth 20% of the daily ifosfamide dose alongside the ifosfamide, then a mesna infusion worth 40% running during the ifosfamide and for 12 to 24 hours after it ends. [S1]
- **Ifosfamide above 2.5 g/m²/day:** the evidence is too thin to set a mesna dose; because ifosfamide lingers longer at high doses, mesna may need to be given more often and for longer. [S1]
- **Oral mesna is dosed at double the IV dose and should not be used for the first dose.** Give IV mesna 20% of the daily ifosfamide dose with the ifosfamide, then oral mesna 40% at 2 and 6 hours after the infusion finishes, so total mesna equals 100% of the ifosfamide. Oral plus IV protects about as well as IV alone. If the patient vomits within 2 hours of an oral dose, repeat it or switch to IV. [S1]
- **High-dose cyclophosphamide (50 mg/kg or 2 g/m²) for stem-cell transplant:** hyperhydration alone or with mesna. Hyperhydrate with at least 3 L/m² over 24 hours; if mesna is added, give it IV at 1.0–1.5 times the daily cyclophosphamide dose. [S1]

## Treating established haemorrhagic cystitis

- **Treatment is individual and scaled to severity; no evidence-based standard exists.** Mild HC usually passes with fluids, bladder anticholinergics and analgesia; moderate to severe HC may need more active measures. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Rule out bacterial, fungal, viral and parasitic infection before treating**, and treat any infection found. Late viral HC needs treatment of the virus as well as the symptoms. [S1]
- **Options:** stop the offending drug, hyperhydrate, give analgesia and antispasmodics, irrigate clots by hand or with continuous bladder irrigation, and transfuse red cells and platelets as needed. For severe or refractory HC, cystoscopic clot evacuation, a suprapubic catheter, hyperbaric oxygen or intravesical agents have been reported. [S1]

## Patient education

- **Teach patients at risk** to keep well hydrated, avoid alcohol and caffeine, void regularly and as soon as they feel the urge, take oral cyclophosphamide in the morning and oral mesna on schedule, and report blood in the urine, urgency or frequency, painful voiding, vague abdominal pain or waking often at night to pass urine. [S1]
