# Infective endocarditis

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, blood cultures and echocardiography, modified Duke criteria, empirical IV antibiotics and surgical indications for suspected infective endocarditis in adults (child doses where the source gives them). Organism-directed therapy is not covered. Endocarditis **prophylaxis** is in `drug-choice-for-selected-infections` (AMH); rheumatic heart disease is in `acute-rheumatic-fever-and-rhd`.

> ✅ **COMPILED FROM 2 SOURCES.** 38 claims; **60 fragments or anchors re-checkable by machine; 6 doses.** **S1** Life in the Fast Lane (LITFL); author Chris Nickson (published 29 January 2019, last modified 3 November 2020; AU): 21 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored · **S2** Imazio M (published 19 July 2024; international): 17 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored

> ⚠️ **#FOAM education page, not a guideline.** No freely readable endocarditis guideline could be used: the ESC 2023 guideline is subscription-only (and reserves AI use), CARPA covers prevention only, and the WHO AWaRe book has no endocarditis treatment section. LITFL's empirical regimens follow Australian *Therapeutic Guidelines* style dosing, but the page was **last modified November 2020** — check current local guidance. **S2** (a 2024 review summarising the ESC 2023 guideline's changes) now adds current imaging, oral step-down, surgical timing and team care; it is a summary of the guideline, not the guideline.

> ⚠️ International guideline (ESC 2023, via the S2 review). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **Vancomycin is the PBS-listed drug here.** In this source it is the empirical choice for hospital-acquired disease, prosthetic valves, penicillin hypersensitivity or suspected community-acquired MRSA.

## Recognise

- ⚠️ **Staphylococcus aureus drives most fulminant endocarditis — suspect endocarditis in every S. aureus bacteraemia and arrange a TOE.** The aortic valve is now the commonest site (formerly the mitral). [S1]
- **Usual organisms:** S. aureus (MSSA or MRSA), coagulase-negative staphylococci, viridans and bovis streptococci, enterococci, the HACEK group and fungi. **Culture-negative causes** include Coxiella (Q fever), Bartonella, Brucella, Legionella, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma and Tropheryma whipplei. [S1]
- **Risk factors:** structural heart disease (congenital, rheumatic, mitral prolapse, regurgitant or degenerative valves), prosthetic valves (1–5%, early if under 60 days), injecting drug use, haemodialysis, long lines, marrow transplant and immunosuppression. [S1]
- **ESC risk classes (S2 Table 1):** high risk means previous endocarditis, a surgical prosthetic valve or any valve-repair material, congenital heart disease that is untreated or repaired with prosthetic material, or a ventricular assist device as destination therapy. Intermediate risk means rheumatic heart disease, non-rheumatic degenerative valve disease, a congenital valve abnormality such as a bicuspid aortic valve, an implanted electronic device, or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Everyone else is low risk. [S2]
- **Half of cases arise on normal valves.** The picture ranges from no symptoms, through malaise, night sweats, anaemia and weight loss, to cardiogenic shock and sepsis; emboli (stroke, septic lung emboli, splenic infarcts) and haematuria from glomerulonephritis may be the clue. [S1]
- **Look for** splinter and conjunctival haemorrhages, Osler nodes (tender) and Janeway lesions (painless) on fingers and toes, Roth spots, splenomegaly, new neurological signs, a new regurgitant murmur and left heart failure. [S1]

## Investigate and diagnose

- **ECG:** a lengthening PR interval, P mitrale, T-wave inversion or new arrhythmia should raise concern. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Blood cultures are positive about 90% of the time.** Take at least 2 sets 12 hours apart, or enough that 3 of 4 are positive with the first and last more than 1 hour apart — before antibiotics where possible. [S1]
- ⚠️ **The current ESC guideline (S2) sets a different blood-culture schedule:** at least three sets 30 minutes apart before antibiotics, each 10 ml into aerobic and anaerobic bottles, drawn from a peripheral vein. [S2]
- **Culture-negative endocarditis is most often due to earlier antibiotics,** otherwise fungi or fastidious bacteria; ESC advises routine serology for Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella, Aspergillus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Brucella and Legionella pneumophila. [S2]
- **Echocardiography:** TTE picks up only about 60% of cases, TOE 90–99%, with specificity around 90%. Look for an oscillating mass on a valve or implanted material, an abscess, or new prosthetic dehiscence. [S1]
- ⚠️ **TOE is now Class I even after a positive TTE** (to find perivalvular complications and small vegetations), except isolated right-sided native-valve disease with a clear, good-quality TTE; it is also required before switching from IV to oral antibiotics. With high suspicion and a normal or inconclusive study, repeat echo in 5–7 days. [S2]
- **Multimodality imaging is now a major diagnostic criterion,** because with prosthetic material echo misses or cannot settle up to 30% of cases. CT beats TOE for perivalvular and periprosthetic complications; echo beats CT for vegetations under 10 mm, leaflet perforation and fistulae. Enterococcus faecalis now counts as a typical endocarditis organism. [S2]
- **Nuclear imaging (FDG-PET/CT or labelled white-cell SPECT/CT) is recommended for suspected prosthetic-valve endocarditis when echo is inconclusive,** with sensitivity and specificity near 85%; on native valves sensitivity is only about 30% (specificity about 98%). [S2]
- **What each scan is for (ESC, S2 Table 3):** CT to diagnose endocarditis and perivalvular or periprosthetic complications (abscess, pseudoaneurysm, fistula), find distant lesions and the source of bacteraemia, assess the coronary arteries before surgery, and weigh other diagnoses; MRI for cardiac, neurological and spinal complications; FDG-PET/CT or white-cell SPECT/CT to diagnose, find distant lesions and sources, and follow the response to antibiotics. [S2]
- **If cultures stay negative,** send serology for the likely organisms and 16S ribosomal RNA PCR on any excised valve tissue. [S1]
- **Modified Duke criteria:** definite endocarditis needs two major criteria, one major plus three minor, or five minor. [S1]
- **Major criteria:** a typical organism in 2 separate blood cultures (viridans streptococci, S. bovis, HACEK, or community-acquired S. aureus or enterococci without another focus) or persistently positive cultures; plus echocardiographic evidence or new valvular regurgitation (a change in an existing murmur is not enough). [S1]
- **Minor criteria:** a predisposing heart condition or injecting drug use; temperature above 38.0 °C; vascular signs (arterial emboli, septic lung infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial or conjunctival haemorrhage, Janeway lesions); immunological signs (glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, rheumatoid factor); and supportive microbiology. [S1]

## Treat

- **Resuscitate first,** then IV antibiotics with or without surgery, supportive care and treatment of complications, involving infectious diseases, cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery early. [S1]
- **Empirical therapy, community-acquired native-valve disease:** benzylpenicillin 1.8 g IV 4-hourly (children 45 mg/kg, maximum 1.8 g) **plus** flucloxacillin 2 g IV 4-hourly (children 50 mg/kg, maximum 2 g) **plus** a first IV dose of gentamicin 4–6 mg/kg (children: 7.5 mg/kg below age 10, 6 mg/kg from 10). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Empirical therapy if hospital-acquired, a prosthetic valve, penicillin hypersensitivity or suspected community-acquired MRSA:** vancomycin 1.5 g IV 12-hourly (children under 12: 30 mg/kg, maximum 1.5 g) **plus** gentamicin dosed as above. [S1]
- **Gentamicin:** after the first dose, set the interval from renal function and give at most 1 or 2 more doses, once daily. It is normally stopped after three doses unless streptococcal or enterococcal endocarditis is proven, when it is given eight-hourly and continued. [S1]
- **Empirical cover generally continues for a minimum of 1 to 2 weeks** (home-based treatment is an option for some at low risk); after that, treatment is directed by the organism and the valve involved. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not use clindamycin or lincomycin** — failure rates are high. [S1]
- **ESC empirical targets (S2):** native-valve and late prosthetic-valve disease, cover staphylococci, streptococci and enterococci; early prosthetic-valve and healthcare-associated disease, also cover methicillin-resistant staphylococci and non-HACEK Gram-negative bacteria; always cover coagulase-negative staphylococci on prosthetic valves. Prefer bactericidal combinations; treat native valves for 4–6 weeks and prosthetic valves for more than 6 weeks. [S2]
- ⚠️ **ESC empirical doses, community-acquired native-valve disease or late prosthetic-valve disease (12 months or more after surgery) (IIa, C; S2 Table 4):** ampicillin, combined either with ceftriaxone or with (flu)cloxacillin and gentamicin. Daily doses: ampicillin 12 g IV split four to six ways; ceftriaxone 4 g IV or IM split in two; (flu)cloxacillin 12 g IV split four to six ways; gentamicin 3 mg/kg IV or IM as a single daily dose. [S2]
- ⚠️ **ESC empirical doses, early prosthetic-valve disease (within 12 months of surgery) or healthcare-associated disease (IIb, C; S2 Table 4),** aimed also at methicillin-resistant staphylococci and non-HACEK Gram-negatives: vancomycin 30 mg/kg a day IV in two doses, or daptomycin 10 mg/kg once a day IV, each with gentamicin 3 mg/kg once a day and rifampicin 900–1200 mg a day IV or by mouth in two or three doses. [S2]
- ⚠️ **ESC empirical doses with beta-lactam allergy, community-acquired native-valve or late prosthetic-valve disease (IIb, C; S2 Table 4):** cefazolin 6 g a day IV in three doses, or vancomycin 30 mg/kg a day IV in two doses, either one with gentamicin 3 mg/kg once a day. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Partial oral or outpatient therapy is new in the current ESC guideline.** In the POET trial, switching to oral antibiotics after at least 10 days IV was noninferior (primary outcome 9% oral vs 12.1% IV). ESC: do a TOE at day 10 in stable patients; stable, uncomplicated patients may then go home from day 10 of IV therapy or day 7 after surgery, on the same drugs or an oral step-down, with a nurse daily and a doctor one to three times a week. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Not for outpatient or oral step-down:** heart failure, severe valve regurgitation, vegetations over 10 mm, progression or local complications, neurological involvement, renal impairment, malabsorption, or injecting drug use. The patient also needs to be stable with a stable home, ideally a live-in carer. POET was Danish; where resistance is higher, the endocarditis team decides case by case. [S2]

## Surgery

- **Consider valve surgery** for haemodynamic instability, a root, paravalvular or intracardiac abscess (or one that is enlarging), recurrent emboli, or infection with S. aureus, Q fever or fungi. [S1]
- **Operating early (within 48 hours) cut later embolic events but did not change mortality** in the trial the source cites. [S1]
- **Complications to watch for:** emboli to brain, limbs, lungs, spleen and other organs; sepsis with local or metastatic abscesses; valve failure with heart failure or cardiogenic shock; arrhythmias; and death. [S1]
- **ESC surgical timing (S2):** the three main indications are heart failure, uncontrolled infection and prevention of emboli; operate as an emergency inside 24 h, urgently at 3–5 days, or later in the same admission. Uncontrolled infection means blood cultures still positive after more than 1 week of appropriate therapy, resistant or virulent organisms, or growing vegetations, abscess, pseudoaneurysm, fistula or new AV block. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Emboli and stroke:** ESC advises considering urgent surgery from a vegetation of 10 mm (preventive surgery with no other indication is Class IIb). Do not delay needed surgery after an ischaemic stroke: haemorrhagic conversion (2–7%) is less likely than another embolus. After a significant haemorrhagic stroke, surgery is generally deferred for more than 4 weeks. [S2]
- **Cardiac devices:** remove the whole device when the leads are infected, and consider it in valve endocarditis even without visible lead involvement. If a new device is needed, place it at another site after symptoms settle and cultures stay clear for 72 h or more without vegetations, or 2 weeks or more with them. [S2]
- **Manage through an endocarditis team** (cardiology, cardiac imaging, cardiac surgery, infectious diseases, microbiology). Uncomplicated cases can stay in a referring centre in regular contact with a heart valve centre; refer complicated cases early. [S2]
