# Penetrating eye injury in children

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition and first ED management of open-globe (penetrating or ruptured) eye injury in children: protection of the globe, fasting, analgesia, antibiotic prophylaxis and referral. Non-penetrating eye trauma is in `acute-eye-injury-children`; the non-traumatic red eye is in `acute-red-eye-children`.

> ⚠️ **Suspected open globe: stop examining, shield the eye, nil by mouth, no eye drops, no pressure on the globe — refer urgently to ophthalmology.**

---

## Recognise it

- **Open globe = the cornea or sclera is injured through its full thickness**, from rupture (usually blunt) or laceration (sharp object or high-velocity projectile). **It is a serious threat to vision.** [S1]
- **Look for it alongside serious head injury or multiple trauma.** Ask about mechanism and the composition of any possible intraocular foreign body (soil, dirt, metal). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Signs of perforation:** protruding missile (**leave it alone — don't touch or remove it**) · loss of red reflex · severe loss of vision · RAPD · squashed or distorted globe · swollen haemorrhagic lids · chemosis · distorted, irregular or peaked pupil · **360 degree subconjunctival haemorrhage** · extruding ocular contents · altered anterior chamber depth. [S1]

## Examine with care

- **A cursory examination may be enough**, but attempt visual acuity and check for a relative afferent pupillary defect. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Avoid any pressure on the globe** — through examination or padding — because eye contents may be extruded. **Do not force the eyelids open.** [S1]
- **In young children with a highly suggestive mechanism, an ophthalmologist examines under procedural sedation or GA.** [S1]

## Manage

- ⚠️ **Urgently notify ophthalmology, place an eye shield and keep the child fasted from first contact.** Do not remove a protruding foreign body. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not give any eye drops.** [S1]
- **Analgesia — consider NSAIDs — plus an antiemetic such as ondansetron**, because vomiting raises intraocular pressure and can expel ocular contents. [S1]
- **Bed rest with the head of the bed at 30 degrees** if haemodynamically able. **Check tetanus status.** [S1]
- **Prophylaxis, no signs of infection:** oral ciprofloxacin 20 mg/kg (max 750 mg) twice daily. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Suspected endophthalmitis or signs of infection:** IV ceftazidime 50 mg/kg (max 2 g) 8-hourly **and** vancomycin 15 mg/kg (max 750 mg) 6-hourly. [S1]
- **Image the orbit (X-ray or CT) after discussion with ophthalmology** when an intraocular foreign body is suspected. [S1]

## Escalation and discharge

- **Involve the paediatric team if child abuse is suspected; transfer** if open-globe management is beyond local capability. [S1]
- **Discharge when** an open-globe injury has been excluded or dealt with **and** the child is symptom-free or has a clear follow-up plan with early-review criteria. [S1]
