# Strongyloidiasis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** testing, treatment and follow-up of *Strongyloides stercoralis* infection, including people starting immune-suppressing treatment. Written for remote and Aboriginal primary health care in central and northern Australia. Other worms (hookworm, threadworm, whipworm, roundworm, liver fluke) and the AMH comparative drug table: see `worm-infections`.

> ⚠️ **Remote-practice source.** CARPA is written for remote and Aboriginal health services in central and northern Australia. Its 'high-prevalence area' rules (remote communities, some town camps) are about that population and do not carry over unchanged to urban practice.

> ⚠️ **Hyperinfection is the reason this matters.** Strongyloides is the one worm that multiplies in the host. Corticosteroids, chemotherapy, transplant drugs and HTLV-1 can turn a silent infection into a fatal one, so test or treat **before** immunosuppression starts.

## Recognise

- **Strongyloides is tiny (up to 2 mm) but can keep reproducing inside the body for years unless it is treated.** It is often silent; when it does cause symptoms these include foul-smelling diarrhoea, abdominal pain, poor appetite and constipation. [S1]
- **Other signs are a rash that visibly moves from hour to hour, itchy hives over the lower back or buttocks, cough, wheeze or coughing up blood, and low potassium (particularly in young children).** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Death is rare but happens two ways:** severe secondary infection (bloodstream, pneumonia, meningitis) or disseminated hyperinfection, where a massive worm load spreads through the body in someone whose immunity is weakened: HTLV1 infection, corticosteroids for over 2 weeks, chemotherapy or transplant drugs. [S1]
- **HTLV-1 is associated with severe strongyloides and scabies infections,** as well as with bronchiectasis, uveitis and infective dermatitis. [S1]

## Who to test, and how

- **Test for strongyloides only when:** symptoms suggest it; before starting any treatment that may weaken immunity (including corticosteroids planned for at least 2 weeks); or when there is a systemic infection with a gut organism and no other cause has been found. [S1]
- **Serology is the simplest test in adults with symptoms that could be chronic strongyloidiasis,** but immunosuppressed people with disseminated infection can test serology-negative. [S1]
- **In children a faeces test usually works better:** a child with bowel symptoms probably has a recent infection and may not yet be seropositive. [S1]
- **Send faeces as fresh as possible and keep it cool, not refrigerated** (heat and cold kill the worms), requesting OCP, MC&S and a strongyloides culture, which not every laboratory offers. [S1]

## Treat

- **Positive faeces test: treat, with ivermectin as the drug of choice. Positive serology: get a medical consult before treating,** because the result can be hard to interpret and underlying or complicating factors need to be looked for. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ivermectin, for adults and children aged 5 years or more who weigh at least 15 kg: 200 microgram/kg by mouth as a single dose, repeated in 1–2 weeks.** Give it with full-cream milk or fatty food. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Albendazole, for children aged 6 months to 4 years: by mouth once a day for 3 days, then the 3-day course repeated in 1–2 weeks.** 200 mg if 6–11 months and under 10 kg; 400 mg if 6–11 months and 10 kg or over, or 1–4 years and under 15 kg, or pregnant after the 1st trimester. Give with breastmilk (under 1 year), full-cream milk or fatty food. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Get a medical consult before giving albendazole to a child under 6 months or in the first trimester, or ivermectin to a child under 5 years or under 15 kg or in pregnancy;** use a urine pregnancy test if pregnancy is uncertain. [S1]

## Before and during immunosuppression

- ⚠️ **For people from a high-prevalence area (remote communities, some town camps) who are on, or about to start, immune-weakening treatment: treat for strongyloides even when blood and faeces tests are negative.** Treat before the treatment starts and then every 3 months for as long as it continues. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Someone whose immunity is already weakened may need 4 or more ivermectin doses;** that is a medical-consult decision. [S1]
- **Before oral prednisolone for longer than 2 weeks (for example in COPD), treat for strongyloides every 3 months while the steroid continues,** alongside TB and hepatitis B screening. [S1]

## Follow-up and prevention

- **Follow up everyone who has been treated.** A person from a high-prevalence area with past strongyloides who is on immune-weakening treatment gets single-dose ivermectin every 3 months. For everyone else, repeat the faeces test at 6 months if symptoms continue, and consult if it is still positive. [S1]
- **Asymptomatic eosinophilia (eosinophils 0.7 to 1.5, no gut symptoms or urticaria, not on a drug that causes eosinophilia, not pregnant):** after a medical consult has considered other causes such as atopy, allergy, scabies or medicines, treatment may be advised with albendazole for 3 days plus single-dose ivermectin, taken with full-cream milk or fatty food. [S1]
- **Prevention:** keep nails short, wash daily, don't scratch, hot-wash clothes, towels and bedding, and wear shoes so larvae cannot enter through the soles. [S1]
