# Eosinophilic oesophagitis (EoE)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, diagnosis, maintenance treatment (PPI, swallowed corticosteroid, elimination diet), rescue and food bolus obstruction in children and adults with eosinophilic oesophagitis, from ASCIA's patient information, management plan and action plan, plus ASCIA's two-food elimination diet (2FED) guide (S2). These are patient-facing plan documents completed by a specialist, not a clinical practice guideline. Food bolus in children generally: see `foreign-body-ingestion-children`.

## Recognise

- **Children with EoE tend to eat slowly, gag or choke, regurgitate food, get stuck food or have abdominal pain.** Adolescents and adults report trouble swallowing, food impaction, chest pain on eating, heartburn that does not respond to medicines, and chewing longer or drinking water with solids. [S1]
- **Mild reflux with vomiting is usually not EoE.** Most people with EoE have other allergy, such as hay fever or asthma (around 75%). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Untreated EoE leads to food impaction in around 30-50%,** sometimes needing removal in hospital, and can scar and narrow the oesophagus. Onset in older children and adults usually means lifelong disease. [S1]

## Diagnose

- **Confirm the diagnosis by endoscopy with biopsy** (usually by a gastroenterologist). Repeat endoscopy and biopsy are also used to judge treatment response, because symptoms alone may not reflect control. [S1]
- **Do not do allergy testing for EoE** unless there were rapid allergic symptoms after a food; tests may not predict the effect of diet changes. Pollen immunotherapy is not recommended for EoE. [S1]

## Treat

- **Care is usually shared between a specialist doctor and a specialist dietitian.** Options: dietary elimination, swallowed corticosteroid (fluticasone puffer, budesonide slurry or dissolving budesonide tablet), a PPI, and dilation for a very narrow oesophagus. [S1]
- **PPI dose (omeprazole, esomeprazole or lansoprazole, for example):** 10mg twice daily for children of 10-20 kg; 20mg twice daily for children > 20kg; adults 20mg twice daily for the first 2 months, then 20mg once daily. [S1]
- **Swallowed fluticasone starting dose:** 50 ug twice daily at ages one to four; 125 ug twice daily at five to ten; 250 ug twice daily from age ten, including adults. Try halving the dose after the first two to three months. [S1]
- **Swallowed budesonide starting dose:** 0.5 mg twice daily in children and 1mg twice daily in adults; try halving after two to three months, for example by keeping only the night-time dose. [S1]
- **After swallowed corticosteroid, nothing by mouth for 30 minutes, then rinse** to reduce thrush. Take the evening dose just before bed so the drug stays longer in the oesophagus. [S1]
- **Diet changes are started by a specialist and supervised by a dietitian.** The usual culprits are cow's milk, wheat and egg (soy, seafood and nuts also occur). Approaches: common-allergen elimination (milk, soy, egg, wheat), step-up diets starting with one or two foods, or amino acid formula in babies. [S1]
- **Adrenaline and antihistamines have no role in EoE.** People who also have a food allergy with anaphylaxis risk need a separate anaphylaxis action plan and adrenaline for that allergy. [S1]

## Two-food elimination diet (2FED)

- **The 2FED diet cuts out cow's milk (dairy) and wheat, the two commonest EoE triggers, strictly and even in trace amounts in food and drink,** for a trial of 8 to 12 weeks. It is a test of whether foods are driving symptoms, not necessarily a long-term treatment. [S2]
- **During the trial, keep a diary of foods and symptoms.** The result is judged on symptoms and/or a repeat endoscopy with oesophageal biopsies; then foods go back in one by one, as planned with the doctor and dietitian. [S2]
- **Keep avoiding any food the person is already allergic to.** Apart from that, meat, chicken and fish, eggs, legumes, fruit and vegetables, nuts and seeds, and grains other than wheat are all allowed; the specialist may also exclude every gluten grain (wheat, barley, rye, oats). [S2]
- **Read every label.** In Australia and New Zealand, milk, wheat and other gluten grains must be declared however small the amount. 'May contain' traces statements are voluntary, and such products are allowed on the diet as long as milk or wheat is not an actual ingredient. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Goat, sheep, camel and buffalo milk are too similar to cow's milk to use instead, and lactose-free milk still carries the usual cow's milk protein,** so neither is allowed. Lactose used in medicines is pure sugar and is fine. [S2]
- **Replacing milk:** under one to two years, breast milk or a formula chosen by the child's specialist; older children and adults can use soy, rice, oat, nut or pea-protein drinks, with calcium-fortified soy preferred for its protein and fat. A dietitian can help a child who refuses the substitute. [S2]
- **Daily calcium targets on the diet:** 500 mg at 1–3 years, 700 mg at 4–8, 1000 mg at 9–11 and 1300 mg at 12–18; adults 1000 mg, rising to 1300 mg for men over 70 and women over 50. The dietitian decides whether a supplement is needed. [S2]

## Worsening symptoms and follow-up

- **If symptoms worsen, the specialist is contacted first;** the plan may allow a double dose of swallowed corticosteroid for a set period. Review maintenance therapy after rescue treatment or any food impaction. [S1]
- **EoE is chronic:** review the plan at every specialist visit. [S1]

## Food bolus obstruction

- ⚠️ **Signs of food impaction (with vomiting):** food stuck on the way down, chest or oesophageal pain or squeezing, inability to swallow, or needing to spit out saliva or drool. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Call an ambulance (000 in Australia, 111 in New Zealand) or go to an emergency department** when food is still stuck after 1 to 2 hours, or if chest pain is severe with difficulty talking or breathing. [S1]
- **Food impaction options:** nitroglycerin 0.4 mg in 10ml of water by mouth; oral salbutamol 100ug/kg (maximum 5mg); a fizzy drink; or endoscopic removal, ideally with oesophageal biopsies. If it passes on its own, the hospital should still tell the treating doctor. [S1]
