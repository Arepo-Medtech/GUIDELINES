# Methylmalonic and propionic acidaemia (MMA and PA)

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** isolated methylmalonic acidaemia (MMA) and propionic acidaemia (PA): when to suspect them, diagnosis, first treatment, avoiding catabolism, the low-natural-protein diet and precursor-free amino acid mixtures, levocarnitine, vitamin B12 responsiveness, metronidazole, liver and kidney transplantation, and surveillance for neurological, renal, cardiac, haematological, bone, growth and pancreatic complications. Isovaleric acidaemia is not covered by this source. First steps for any suspected inborn error in an unwell child: `metabolic-disorders-children`. Hyperammonaemia management detail: `urea-cycle-disorders`.

> ✅ **COMPILED FROM 2 SOURCES.** 31 claims; **70 fragments or anchors re-checkable by machine; 7 doses.** **S1** Forny P (vol. 44, pp. 566–592, published 9 March 2021; international): 18 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored · **S2** Servais A (published 6 September 2024; international): 13 claims, quoted

> 🌐 **Two sources.** S1 (European MMA/PA guideline, 2021) covers both disorders. **S2** (ERKNet and MetabERN kidney consensus, 2024) covers **MMA only**: chronic kidney disease, dialysis and transplantation. Its points are graded B, C or X by the American Academy of Pediatrics scheme. Where they differ, both are shown.

> ⚠️ International guideline (European MMA/PA guideline group, first revision, 2021). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **The PBS product and this guideline pull in different directions.** The PBS rows for MMA and PA list a **precursor-free amino acid formula** (no methionine, threonine or valine, low isoleucine). This guideline warns that such mixtures can cause low isoleucine and valine and poorer growth, and says they should not replace adequate natural protein.

## Suspect and diagnose

- **Think of MMA and PA in any acute or intermittent neurological deterioration and in suspected neonatal sepsis.** (very strong recommendation) [S1]
- **Urine organic acids give the reliable diagnosis** (very strong); genetic testing is suggested afterwards to confirm it, assign the subtype, and allow counselling and prenatal testing. [S1]
- **Start specific treatment as soon as MMA or PA is suspected** and refer to a specialist centre, because some treatments and expertise exist only there. [S1]

## Acute decompensation

- **A decompensation with severe acidosis may need intensive care, whether or not ammonia or lactate is also high.** The acute approach rests on expert opinion rather than trials. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Avoid sodium phenylbutyrate as the first-line ammonia scavenger in MMA and PA:** it can lower glutamine and impair Krebs-cycle replenishment. Large IV glucose loads can also cause lactic acidosis. [S1]
- **Mild intercurrent illness:** a time-limited oral or enteral glucose-polymer emergency feed, if tolerated without vomiting or diarrhoea, while protein (including the amino acid mixture) is stopped or reduced, for example to 50%. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Emergency feed strength by age (S1 Table 4):** up to 1 year, glucose polymer at 10% carbohydrate with 3.5% fat emulsion, giving about 71.5 kcal per 100 mL; 1–2 years, 15% with 5% fat (105 kcal); 2–9 years, 20% with 5% fat (125 kcal); over 10 years, 25% with 5% fat (145 kcal). The table gives no volumes or feeding intervals. [S1]

## Long-term treatment

- **Prevent catabolism:** infection, fever and long fasts trigger decompensation. (strong recommendation) [S1]
- **A low-natural-protein diet that still meets age-appropriate total protein needs is suggested;** the natural protein allowance is set individually by clinical and biochemical monitoring, and breastfeeding is possible. (weak) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Centres prescribe precursor-free amino acid mixtures inconsistently; the mixtures carry little valine and isoleucine and a lot of leucine,** so they can cause deficiencies and poor growth. They should not stand in for enough natural protein. [S1]
- **Levocarnitine is suggested, usually about 100 mg/kg/day;** it is well tolerated. (weak) [S1]
- **Metronidazole (10 to 20 mg/kg/day, split into two or three doses) has been standard for reducing gut propionate, on weak evidence;** it can cause peripheral neuropathy, QTc prolongation and pancreatitis, so use it cautiously. [S1]
- **Test every suspected MMA patient for vitamin B12 responsiveness** (strong): off hydroxocobalamin for at least 1 month, three baseline samples, then 1 mg IM on three consecutive days; a mean fall of >50% in methylmalonic acid is a response. Biotin is not recommended in PA. [S1]
- **Consider liver transplantation (MMA or PA) or combined liver-kidney transplantation (MMA) for frequent decompensations;** decompensations can still happen afterwards, so metabolic follow-up continues. Never transplant during an acute decompensation. (weak) [S1]

## Monitor for complications

- **Neurological:** standardised developmental, cognitive and behavioural testing; full stroke work-up for stroke-like episodes; watch for seizures; check vision and hearing (optic neuropathy, sensorineural hearing loss). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Kidney and heart:** monitor kidney function (chronic kidney disease), and examine the heart regularly: cardiomyopathy occurs in both, and **long QT in PA** can cause sudden cardiac arrest. (very strong) [S1]
- **Blood, bone and growth:** full blood count at follow-up and during decompensation (anaemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia), bone health assessment, and regular growth measurements. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Evaluate promptly for pancreatitis when it is suspected;** both acute and chronic forms occur. Treat health-related quality of life as an outcome that matters. [S1]
- **Checks at stable MMA follow-up (S2 Table 1):** ammonia and lactate; quantitative plasma amino acids after 3–4 hours' fasting; plasma (and, depending on kidney function, urine) MMA; acylcarnitine profile; diet history; growth including head circumference; full clinical and neurological examination with developmental milestones; albumin, prealbumin and total protein; bone health (Ca, P, ALP, Mg, PTH and 25-OH vitamin D, urine Ca and P); FBC, ferritin, folate and B12; kidney function including cystatin C and urinary protein loss, with iohexol GFR for important decisions; lipase and pancreatic amylase; ECG and echocardiography; formal developmental or cognitive testing; brain MRI (EEG, EMG if indicated); eye assessment and a formal hearing test. The published table carries no visit frequencies. [S2]

## MMA: kidney disease, dialysis and transplantation (S2)

- **Chronic kidney disease is common in isolated MMA and depends on subtype:** 47% of 30 cobalamin-unresponsive patients; in 82 patients, mut0 61% and cblB 66% but cblA 21% and mut− 0%. Median age at CKD was 8 years (mut0), 13.5 years (cblB) and 11 years (cblA). Function can fall within a few years, so monitor closely. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Do not rely on creatinine-based eGFR:** low muscle mass and the low-protein diet make CKD-EPI and Schwartz overestimate kidney function (bias 16 ± 15 and 37 ± 22 ml/min per 1.73 m2 against measured GFR). Use cystatin C equations (grade B, strong) and measured GFR by iohexol clearance when possible. Kidney biopsy is generally not required. [S2]
- **Check tubular function and kidney growth:** plasma electrolytes, uric acid, acid-base balance, low-molecular-weight proteinuria and calcium-phosphate balance. Kidney growth runs at about one-third to one-half of normal; repeated kidney ultrasound could be proposed in children. [S2]
- ⚠️ **No treatment is proven to preserve kidney function, so follow standard CKD care** (grade C). S2's standard long-term treatment includes oral L-carnitine 100–200 mg/kg/d in 2–4 doses, hydroxocobalamin if cobalamin-responsive, carglumic acid for hyperammonaemia, growth hormone in selected children with CKD, and bicarbonate by acid-base balance. [S2]
- **Pancreatitis risk:** because pancreatitis may be triggered by hypercalcaemia, monitor serum calcium and vitamin D closely and keep calcium in the low-normal range. [S2]
- **With CKD, follow plasma MMA rather than urinary MMA** (urinary excretion falls with GFR), together with ammonia, lactate, amino acids and acid-base status, with expert dietetic support (grade B, strong). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Acute dialysis when initial medical management fails** in a decompensation with severe neurological symptoms, a change in consciousness, or persistent ketoacidosis with high or rising ammonium or lactate. Do not wait for MMA results. Continuous veno-venous or intermittent haemodialysis is preferred to peritoneal dialysis for rapid control. [S2]
- **Long-term dialysis:** start it when plasma MMA rises with metabolic or clinical instability despite optimised protein intake and medicines, often at a higher eGFR than usual. Do not cut protein to control plasma MMA; on dialysis natural protein can be increased. Haemodialysis needs 4 or 5 hours per session, at least 4 sessions a week; use bicarbonate-based fluids (grade X, strong). [S2]
- **Which transplant:** consider liver transplantation early, before CKD progresses; prefer combined liver-kidney transplantation at CKD stage 3b, 4 and 5. Kidney transplant alone lowers plasma MMA less, and may be discussed in adults (especially cblA), late-onset forms or limited life expectancy. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Transplant surgery:** replace fasting with 10% glucose infusion with electrolytes (and lipids) at age-appropriate calories, plus IV L-carnitine (grade B, strong). Glucose from 4 to 8 mg/kg/min by age needs close monitoring for high lactate or glucose. After transplant check lactate every 4 hours and give thiamine. [S2]
- **After transplant, continue L-carnitine for life** (grade B, strong); natural protein can usually rise, to about 0.8 g/kg in adults and up to 1.5 g/kg in children, guided by lactate, plasma and urine MMA and amino acids. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Tacrolimus is particularly poorly tolerated in MMA:** calcineurin-inhibitor neurotoxicity can look exactly like a metabolic stroke, and needs prompt dose reduction or withdrawal. Early after transplant a calcineurin inhibitor is recommended, with cyclosporine better tolerated than tacrolimus; mTOR inhibitors could replace them for maintenance in adults. [S2]
