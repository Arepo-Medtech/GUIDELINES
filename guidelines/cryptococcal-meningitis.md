# Cryptococcal meningitis (HIV-associated)

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosis, cryptococcal antigen screening and pre-emptive therapy, induction–consolidation–maintenance antifungal treatment, raised intracranial pressure, ART timing and relapse in adults, adolescents and children **living with HIV**. Cryptococcosis without HIV (transplant, haematology/oncology, *C. gattii* in immunocompetent hosts) is not covered. Related: `invasive-candidiasis`, `invasive-aspergillosis`, `hiv-antiretroviral-therapy`.

## Recognise and diagnose

- **Meningitis is the dominant form,** making up as much as 90% of cryptococcal disease linked to HIV; the infection is rare in children with HIV. [S1]
- **Suspected first episode: do a lumbar puncture promptly,** measuring CSF opening pressure and running a rapid cryptococcal antigen test (strong recommendation). Send CSF for fungal culture at the same time where feasible. [S1]
- **If lumbar puncture is unavailable or contraindicated,** a rapid antigen test on serum, plasma or whole blood (result within 24 hours) is preferred; without one, refer promptly for work-up and treatment. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Raised intracranial pressure is not a reason to avoid lumbar puncture here.** The contraindications are significant coagulopathy, a suspected space-occupying lesion (focal signs other than a sixth-nerve palsy, or recurrent seizures, ideally confirmed on CT), major spinal deformity and informed refusal. [S1]

## Screen and prevent

- **Screen for cryptococcal antigen before starting or restarting ART when CD4 is below 100 cells/mm³,** and treat antigen-positive people pre-emptively (strong). A threshold below 200 may be considered (conditional). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Every antigen-positive person needs assessment for meningitis,** with lumbar puncture where feasible. India ink misses cases, so confirm a negative India ink with CSF antigen testing or culture. [S1]
- **Pre-emptive therapy for antigen-positive screening:** fluconazole 800–1200 mg/day in adults (12 mg/kg per day in adolescents) for two weeks, then consolidation and maintenance fluconazole exactly as for treatment. [S1]
- **Where antigen screening is not available,** give adults and adolescents with CD4 below 100 cells/mm³ fluconazole primary prophylaxis instead (strong). [S1]

## Induction treatment

- ⚠️ **Preferred induction (new in this edition):** one dose of liposomal amphotericin B at 10 mg/kg, plus flucytosine 100 mg/kg daily split into four doses for 14 days, plus fluconazole — 1200 mg daily in adults, or 12 mg/kg daily (up to 800 mg) in children and adolescents (strong). [S1]
- **Without liposomal amphotericin:** amphotericin B deoxycholate 1 mg/kg daily with flucytosine 100 mg/kg daily (four divided doses) for seven days, then seven days of fluconazole at the induction dose. [S1]
- **Without any amphotericin:** 14 days of oral fluconazole 1200 mg daily plus flucytosine 100 mg/kg daily. It is the only oral combination WHO endorses, and it carried lower mortality than deoxycholate amphotericin with fluconazole. [S1]
- **Without flucytosine:** give fluconazole 1200 mg daily with either liposomal amphotericin B at 3–4 mg/kg daily or deoxycholate amphotericin at 1 mg/kg daily, for 14 days. Regimens that include flucytosine work better, so access to it should be secured. [S1]
- **Do not give routine adjunctive corticosteroids during induction** (strong). In the trial, dexamethasone did not lower mortality, added disability and serious adverse events, and slowed CSF fungal clearance. [S1]

## Consolidation, maintenance and ART

- **Consolidation:** fluconazole 800 mg daily in adults (6–12 mg/kg per day in children and adolescents, maximum 800 mg) for eight weeks after induction. [S1]
- **Maintenance:** fluconazole 200 mg daily in adults (6 mg/kg per day in children and adolescents) until the immune system recovers — CD4 above 200 cells/mm³ with a suppressed viral load on ART (strong, high certainty). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not start ART straight away:** early ART raised mortality, so defer it 4–6 weeks from the start of antifungals — four weeks after an amphotericin-based induction, 4–6 weeks after fluconazole with flucytosine. [S1]

## Raised intracranial pressure and monitoring

- ⚠️ **Everyone with suspected cryptococcal meningitis gets an initial and an early repeat lumbar puncture with opening pressure,** whether or not they have symptoms of raised pressure. [S1]
- **Therapeutic lumbar puncture:** drain enough CSF to bring the pressure under 20 cm H₂O, or to half the baseline if it is very high (20–30 mL is usually enough). Repeat daily while symptoms persist, until they resolve or the pressure has been normal for at least two days. [S1]
- **Review the clinical response every day for the first two weeks of induction.** Antigen titres in CSF, serum or plasma should not be used to monitor response in any setting. [S1]
- **On relapse:** restart induction, relieve raised pressure by therapeutic lumbar puncture, reinforce adherence, begin ART after 4–6 weeks of effective antifungals if not already on it, and test fluconazole susceptibility where possible. [S1]

## Special situations

- **Pregnancy:** amphotericin B may be used when benefit outweighs harm. Fluconazole and flucytosine have been linked to birth defects in animal and some uncontrolled human studies, so weigh their use case by case. [S1]
- **Localised disease outside the meninges:** a suggested (expert-opinion) course is fluconazole at 800 mg/day for two weeks, stepping to 400 mg/day for eight weeks, then 200 mg/day as maintenance. Disseminated disease shown by a positive blood culture is treated as meningitis. [S1]
