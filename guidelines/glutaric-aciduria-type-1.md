# Glutaric aciduria type 1 (GA1): diet, carnitine and emergency treatment

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** confirming GA1 after newborn screening or a suggestive presentation, the low-lysine diet with lysine-free, tryptophan-reduced amino acid mixtures, carnitine, emergency treatment during illness and surgery in the vulnerable first 6 years, neurological care, monitoring and transition to adult care. First steps for any suspected inborn error in an unwell child: `metabolic-disorders-children`. Other organic acidurias: `organic-acidaemias`.

## Recognise and diagnose

- ⚠️ **Most untreated children suffer an acute striatal injury before age 3 (to 6) years,** set off by infection, fever or surgery, leaving a permanent, mainly dystonic movement disorder. [S1]
- **Confirm an abnormal newborn screen or suggestive findings** by measuring glutaric and 3-hydroxyglutaric acid in urine and/or blood, then, if abnormal, GCDH gene or enzyme testing. (strong) [S1]
- **Subdural haematoma or hygroma together with frontotemporal hypoplasia and widened anterior temporal CSF spaces warrants targeted testing for GA1.** (strong) [S1]
- **Work-up, treatment planning and family training belong in a specialised metabolic centre;** transfer patients diagnosed elsewhere promptly. (strong) [S1]

## Maintenance treatment

- ⚠️ **Low-lysine diet for every patient until 6 years of age, with a lysine-free, tryptophan-reduced, arginine-enriched amino acid mixture to cover protein needs.** (strong) [S1]
- **After 6 years, move to an age-adapted, protein-controlled diet** built on safe protein intakes that limits high-lysine foods, with regular dietetic support through the change. [S1]
- **Extra single-amino-acid arginine is not recommended** beyond what food and the amino acid mixture provide; no clinical benefit has been shown. [S1]
- **Give carnitine for life,** keeping the free carnitine level (plasma or blood spot) within normal limits. [S1]
- **Vaccinate on the national schedule.** [S1]

## Emergency treatment

- ⚠️ **Up to age 6, start emergency treatment at once, and intensively, for any febrile illness, alarming symptom or surgery.** (strong; very high clinical relevance) [S1]
- **Home emergency treatment is possible only if the child is clinically well despite fever.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Transfer without delay to the metabolic centre (or the nearest hospital under its supervision) for IV treatment** if there is recurrent vomiting or diarrhoea, poor feed tolerance, high fever or any suspicious neurological sign. [S1]
- **After 6 years, emergency treatment can still be used for severe illness or surgery,** adapting glucose and fluids to the individual; crises have not been reported at this age, but silent damage cannot be excluded. [S1]

## Neurological care

- **Movement disorder, seizures and subdural collections are managed by a neuropaediatrician or neurologist and/or neurosurgeon working closely with the metabolic team.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **In the first 3 years, admit and observe for at least 24 h after even a minor head injury,** because of the subdural haematoma risk. [S1]
- **Image the brain at any age if neurological symptoms appear or clearly worsen;** routine MRI for extrastriatal changes may start from 10 years, for example every 2–5 years. [S1]

## Monitor

- **Do not use urine glutaric or 3-hydroxyglutaric acid levels to monitor or adjust treatment.** [S1]
- **On the low-lysine diet, check plasma amino acids regularly, 3–4 h after a meal, keeping them in the age-specific normal range;** keep trough free carnitine (at least 12 h after the last dose) in range. [S1]
- **Check kidney function every year from 6 years of age;** assess IQ or developmental quotient, motor function and language regularly. [S1]
- **Plan a structured move to adult care starting from 14 years of age,** with regular age-appropriate education and quality-of-life review by an interdisciplinary team. [S1]
