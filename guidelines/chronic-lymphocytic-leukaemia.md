# Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and small lymphocytic lymphoma

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** genomic work-up before treatment, first-line targeted therapy (continuous BTK inhibitor or fixed-duration venetoclax combinations), treatment of relapsed and double-refractory disease, cellular therapy and supportive care for adults with CLL or SLL in Australia and New Zealand. This is a companion update to the 2023 Australasian statement. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Diagnosis and when to treat (S2)

- **Diagnose CLL by flow cytometry:** at least 5 billion per litre clonal B cells expressing CD19, CD5 and CD23. SLL has fewer than 5 billion per litre circulating clonal cells with lymphoid tissue involvement and is managed as CLL. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Start treatment only for active disease by iwCLL 2018 criteria; a high-risk prognostic score alone is not a reason.** At least one of: marrow failure (haemoglobin under 100 g/L or platelets under 100, though stable low platelets may be watched); nodes over 10 cm or progressive/symptomatic nodes; spleen over 6 cm below the costal margin or progressive/symptomatic; lymphocytes rising over 50% in 2 months or doubling in under 6 months; autoimmune anaemia or thrombocytopenia not responding to steroids; symptomatic extranodal disease; or symptoms (weight loss over 10% in 6 months, ECOG 2 or worse fatigue, fever of 38.0°C for 2 weeks or more, or night sweats for over 1 month, without infection). [S2]
- **Before venetoclax, image the neck, chest, abdomen and pelvis by CT or MRI** to gauge tumour bulk and tumour lysis risk; routine CT or marrow biopsy is otherwise not needed for response assessment in practice. [S2]
- **Grade for the start-treatment rule (S2 recommendation box):** begin therapy only once iwCLL criteria are fulfilled (I, A). The source adds that this was properly tested only before first-line treatment, with very limited evidence at relapse. [S2]

## Work-up before treatment

- **Before first-line treatment, test the blood for IGHV mutation status and run NGS plus FISH or microarray.** (II, A) [S1]
- **Repeat the genomic testing before every later line of therapy**, looking for TP53 changes and for resistance mutations in the BTK and BCL2 pathways, which can steer the choice at relapse. (III, C) [S1]
- **Graded genetics before treatment (S2):** find IGHV somatic hypermutation status by ERIC methods, once, before first-line therapy, since it does not change (II, B); test for TP53 aberrations, both del(17p) by FISH and TP53 mutation by NGS, before every line (II, B); do not assess complex karyotype outside trials, because it is poorly reproducible (III, B). [S2]
- **Pre-treatment checklist (S2 table):** ask about B symptoms, past infections and vaccines, heart, liver and kidney disease, and anticoagulant, antiplatelet or interacting drugs; record ECOG status, node, spleen and liver size and a full examination; check blood count with differential, direct antiglobulin test, renal and liver function, LDH, urate, β2-microglobulin, IgA/IgG/IgM, and hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV serology. Marrow biopsy is for severe or unexplained cytopenia. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Before any BTK inhibitor (S2 table):** ECG, cardiac history and a cardiovascular risk assessment for everyone; refer to a cardiologist if there is past heart disease or uncontrolled cardiac comorbidity. Imaging is needed only in particular situations, such as CT from neck to pelvis to judge tumour lysis risk when venetoclax is planned. [S2]

## First-line treatment

- ⚠️ **Do not use chemoimmunotherapy routinely in first-line CLL, even with mutated IGHV.** (I, A) Its DNA-damage toxicity includes therapy-related myeloid neoplasms (6.3% after FCR); keep it for rare cases where no targeted drug can be obtained. [S1]
- **First-line options are a continuous covalent BTK inhibitor, or a fixed-duration venetoclax combination** (VenO, I + V, AV or AVO). (I, A) [S1]
- **Tailor the first-line choice** to comorbidity (cardiovascular and bleeding risk, kidney function), genomic risk such as TP53 and IGHV status, and whether the patient prefers a fixed course or ongoing therapy. (II, B) [S1]
- **For continuous BTK inhibition, choose acalabrutinib or zanubrutinib ahead of ibrutinib.** Both cause less atrial fibrillation (acalabrutinib also less hypertension), and their PFS in relapsed disease matched or beat ibrutinib. [S1]
- **Continuous BTK inhibitors never stop,** so harms accumulate: bleeding, hypertension, arrhythmia and serious (grade 3 or higher) infection at about 5–7% a year, plus ongoing pressure for resistant clones. [S1]
- **Adding six cycles of obinutuzumab to acalabrutinib improved 6-year PFS (78 vs 62%)** but raised neutropenia and serious infection, with no clear gain in TP53-altered disease; it suits younger patients who can better withstand infections. [S1]
- **VenO is a fixed course: 6 months of obinutuzumab with 12 months of venetoclax.** It gives deep remissions at any age or fitness (76% undetectable MRD at the end), can be repeated later, and stays reasonable in TP53-mutated or unmutated-IGHV disease, although PFS is shorter there. [S1]
- **VenO drawbacks:** intravenous infusions and infusion reactions, the logistics of tumour lysis prevention, and possibly more infection than all-oral fixed-duration combinations. Undetectable MRD at the end of treatment strongly predicts outcome. [S1]
- **Ibrutinib plus venetoclax runs for 15 cycles** (3 of ibrutinib alone, then 12 of both). ⚠️ In the GLOW trial of unfit patients, 4 of 106 (3.8%) died suddenly from cardiac causes, so it is less attractive for older or comorbid patients with cardiac risk factors. [S1]
- **In TP53 wild-type disease, acalabrutinib plus venetoclax (AV) beat chemoimmunotherapy on PFS and OS,** and the panel prefers AV over ibrutinib plus venetoclax because of its better safety. [S1]
- **A triplet (adding obinutuzumab) may be chosen for younger patients with unmutated IGHV,** provided the extra infection risk is clearly explained. [S1]
- **Where the sources differ, acalabrutinib plus obinutuzumab (S2):** S2 advises against preferring it over acalabrutinib alone because of the added toxicity and cost-benefit balance, while S1 sees a role in younger patients who tolerate infections better. S2 also favours time-limited over continuous therapy in both IGHV groups where kidney function and the venetoclax ramp-up allow; AV with or without obinutuzumab runs for 14 cycles (AMPLIFY). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Heart check before any BTK inhibitor (S2):** do a thorough cardiovascular assessment first and optimise blood pressure treatment. Generally avoid BTK inhibitors after ventricular arrhythmia or with uncontrolled heart failure; with cardiovascular disease or a need for anticoagulation, a BCL-2 inhibitor is favoured. [S2]
- **TP53-disrupted disease (S2):** until longer data mature, a continuous second-generation BTK inhibitor may be the preferred choice (mind cardiac comorbidity). Triplets (acalabrutinib or ibrutinib with venetoclax and obinutuzumab) are an option for patients who can bear the extra infection risk; idelalisib with rituximab or continuous venetoclax alone remain for those unsuited to BTK inhibitors. [S2]
- **Graded first-line choices (S2):** chemoimmunotherapy is no longer recommended, as bendamustine-rituximab and chlorambucil-obinutuzumab are inferior to targeted drugs and FCR brings more harm, including myeloid neoplasms (I, A). Without TP53 aberrations, prefer a time-limited targeted regimen to continuous therapy (I, A); pick the combination by disease biology, drug side effects, comorbidity, co-medication, infection history and convenience, and avoid continuous therapy when IGHV is mutated (II, A). [S2]
- **With TP53 disruption (S2):** continuous therapy is suggested over a fixed course unless shared decision-making favours a fixed course (I, A); triplets may lengthen treatment-free intervals compared with doublets for those who can bear the added infection risk (III, B). [S2]
- **Venetoclax on its own is an acceptable first-line option** but is not preferred when other targeted drugs or combinations can be given, because supporting data are thin. [S1]

## Relapsed CLL

- **At relapse, choose by prior therapy:** separate mere exposure from intolerance and from true refractoriness, and weigh patient and disease factors. (I, A) [S1]
- **Standard second-line choices are venetoclax plus rituximab (VenR) or a covalent BTK inhibitor.** (II, B) After progression on a continuous BTK inhibitor, VenR is preferred (a non-covalent BTK inhibitor is an alternative); after fixed-duration venetoclax, a covalent BTK inhibitor or another venetoclax course are both reasonable. [S1]
- **Switching BTK inhibitor works for intolerance, not refractoriness:** acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib are tolerated after ibrutinib toxicity but do not work in genuinely ibrutinib-refractory disease. [S1]
- **Resistance mutations help pick a BTK inhibitor:** the BTK L528W and A428D variants make disease resistant to both covalent and non-covalent agents. [S1]
- **Re-treating after a fixed-duration course is a viable strategy** (II, B). In 46 relapsed patients, venetoclax re-treatment gave an 80% response rate and a median PFS of 25 months. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Double-refractory CLL** (progression on, or within 6 months of finishing, both a BTK inhibitor and venetoclax) carries a poor prognosis: prioritise a clinical trial, and outside a trial use a non-covalent BTK inhibitor. (I, A) Pirtobrutinib gave a 73% response rate but median PFS of only 19.6 months. [S1]
- **After progression on a covalent BTK inhibitor, do not give another covalent one (S2);** move to a venetoclax regimen or pirtobrutinib (69% response, median PFS 14 months after covalent BTK inhibitors). If the covalent drug was stopped for side effects and the disease later relapsed, the same class can be used again. After fixed-duration venetoclax, re-treatment works best when the treatment-free gap was over 2 years; an earlier relapse calls for a change of class. [S2]
- **Double-refractory disease (S2):** pirtobrutinib is preferred, with idelalisib plus rituximab where it cannot be obtained. Consider allograft, bridging with either to maximal response; about 50% of eligible patients with controlled disease gain long remissions, though data in this group are few. Trials of BTK degraders, bispecifics or CAR T-cells are strongly advised. [S2]
- **Graded relapse choices (S2):** after chemoimmunotherapy, fixed-duration venetoclax with an anti-CD20 antibody or a continuous second-generation covalent BTK inhibitor suits most patients (I, A). Intolerant of ibrutinib: offer acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib or a venetoclax regimen; intolerant of acalabrutinib or zanubrutinib: the other second-generation drug may be tried (II, B). Progressing on a covalent BTK inhibitor: venetoclax-based therapy or a non-covalent BTK inhibitor (II, B). Relapse after fixed-duration venetoclax: a covalent BTK inhibitor or venetoclax again, guided by prior response and time off treatment (IV, B). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Double-refractory disease (S2):** keep the current therapy going until the next line is ready, to avoid a flare of disease (III, B). [S2]
- **Allogeneic transplant is the only proven cure.** Consider it for young, fit patients once their second targeted regimen has started, especially with adverse genetics (II, B), and for young patients with Richter transformation. [S1]
- **CD19 CAR T-cell therapy** can give very durable remissions in relapsed CLL, but only in those reaching complete response; complete responses are uncommon and cytokine release and neurotoxicity are more frequent than in other B-cell cancers. [S1]

## Richter transformation (S2)

- ⚠️ **Suspect Richter transformation (2–10% of patients) and confirm it by biopsy, ideally excisional,** guided by high FDG-PET uptake; tell it apart from accelerated CLL and from pseudo-Richter flare after stopping a BTK inhibitor. Test whether it is clonally related to the CLL (IGHV-IGHD-IGHJ sequencing) and check del(17p)/TP53. [S2]
- **Treat clonally unrelated Richter DLBCL as de novo DLBCL and Hodgkin-type Richter as classical Hodgkin lymphoma;** clonally related disease has no standard, so a trial is preferred, with R-CHOP-type chemoimmunotherapy as the default otherwise (complete response 5–38%). Consider transplant for eligible patients with clonally related DLBCL who respond. [S2]
- **Graded Richter recommendations (S2):** check whether DLBCL in a patient with CLL is clonally related, because unrelated DLBCL follows DLBCL guidelines (III, B); offer a clinical trial wherever possible (II, A); outside trials chemoimmunotherapy is the standard (III, C); fit patients with clonally related Richter DLBCL in remission should be offered an allograft as consolidation if a donor exists (III, B). [S2]

## Supportive care and survivorship

- **Infection prevention during watch-and-wait (S2):** pneumococcal conjugate vaccine 20-valent at diagnosis, then the polysaccharide vaccine (PPV23) at least 2 months later, repeated every 5 years; recombinant zoster vaccine (no live vaccines, including the old live zoster vaccine); influenza and SARS-CoV-2 per national schedules. Give immunoglobulin replacement for recurrent or acute infections with IgG below 4 g/L, preferably subcutaneous. [S2]
- **Autoimmune cytopenias (S2):** if CLL itself does not need treatment, treat warm autoimmune haemolysis with immunosuppression (steroids plus rituximab beat steroids alone) and cold agglutinin disease with warmth and rituximab alone; treat immune thrombocytopenia first with steroids or IV immunoglobulin (50–60% respond) or weekly rituximab for 4 weeks, and treat the CLL before second- or third-line ITP therapy. [S2]
- **Graded infection measures (S2):** vaccinate against pneumococcus, varicella zoster and hepatitis B even during watch-and-wait and even if the response may be poor (III, B); repeat influenza and SARS-CoV-2 vaccines each season (III, B); never give live vaccines (III, B); routine antimicrobial prophylaxis is generally not advised (IV, D); immunoglobulin replacement only for recurrent symptomatic or active infections needing hospital care together with laboratory-proven hypogammaglobulinaemia (II, B). [S2]
- **Survivorship (S2):** tell every patient about cancer screening programmes and encourage them to take part (III, B); vitamin D and other supplements need not be routinely prescribed, as any benefit against progression is unproven (III, C). [S2]
- **Plan supportive care around** immune suppression and infection, frequent cardiovascular disease, second cancers (in Australia notably skin cancer, and myeloid neoplasms after chemoimmunotherapy), osteoporosis and fragility fractures, frailty, and psychosocial harm, ideally within a person-centred survivorship model. [S1]
