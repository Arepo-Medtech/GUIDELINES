# Oral and labial herpes (cold sores)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition and primary-care management of herpes simplex cold sores and oral herpes in adults and children, with the mouth-ulcer red flags that apply. Primary herpetic gingivostomatitis in children is in `hsv-gingivostomatitis` (RCH); general herpes stomatitis is in `mouth-and-throat-conditions`; genital HSV is in `genital-herpes`. Herpes simplex keratitis is not covered.

> ✅ **COMPILED FROM 2 SOURCES.** 19 claims; **21 fragments or anchors re-checkable by machine; 2 doses.** **S1** Central Australian Aboriginal Congress (8th edition 2022, V1.03 reprinted with corrections May 2026; AU): 14 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored · **S2** Ocampo (published 30 April 2024 (guideline search June 2022 to March 2023); international): 5 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored

> ⚠️ **Remote-practice source.** CARPA is written for remote and Aboriginal health services in central and northern Australia; its escalation points ("medical consult") assume a remote clinic with doctor support by phone.

> ⚠️ **No oral antiviral regimen for cold sores is given here.** CARPA stops at topical aciclovir and refers severe or recurrent cases for a medical consult. Famciclovir, the PBS-listed drug for these conditions, appears in CARPA only for recurrent **genital** herpes. S2 confirms that international guidelines favour oral nucleoside antivirals (aciclovir, valaciclovir, famciclovir) but its dose table was stripped, so there is still no oral dose here.

> ⚠️ **S2 is an international review of guidelines, not a guideline** (AGREE II appraisal, 2024). It reports what 12 guidelines from the USA, UK, Spain and Brazil agree on, and how reliable they are; it makes no recommendations of its own.

## Recognise

- **Cold sores are caused by herpes simplex virus,** the same virus that causes genital herpes. [S1]
- **Typical picture:** clusters of small fluid-filled blisters, usually around the mouth or on the face. **The first infection can be severe.** [S1]
- **Oral herpes** shows as blisters and ulcers on the lips, gums, tongue and palate, often with fever. [S1]
- **Rule out look-alikes:** impetigo (school sores), and hand, foot and mouth disease, which gives small blisters in the mouth plus on the hands and feet. [S1]
- **What antivirals can and cannot do:** they cut the chance of passing the virus on and make episodes shorter and milder, but they do not cure it; recurrences and viral shedding remain possible for life. [S1]

## Treat

- **Relieve pain on the skin:** ice, or lidocaine (lignocaine) gel. [S1]
- **Keep the person well hydrated;** a severe case may need IV fluids. [S1]
- **Wash the sores with normal saline** to stop them becoming secondarily infected. [S1]
- **Topical antiviral option:** apply aciclovir 5% cream 5 times daily for 5 days, starting the moment symptoms begin, ahead of the blister. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Severe or recurrent cold sores need a medical consult;** oral antiviral treatment may be required. [S1]
- **With kidney disease, discuss with a doctor first** — antiviral doses may need to be reduced. [S1]

## What international guidelines say (review of guidelines)

- **Recurrences are set off by things that weaken local or general immunity:** long ultraviolet exposure, immunosuppressive medicines, HIV, stress, premenstrual tension and surgery. Most people with recurrent HSV-1 have fewer than two episodes a year; 5% to 10% have six or more. [S2]
- **Guidelines from the USA, UK, Spain and Brazil agree that the nucleoside antivirals (aciclovir, valaciclovir and famciclovir) are the drugs of choice for herpes labialis.** [S2]
- ⚠️ **Most guidelines favour oral over topical antivirals, and they do not agree on whether topical antiviral treatment should be recommended at all.** A few add oral pain relief and topical anaesthetic according to how severe the episode is and what the person needs. [S2]
- **The guidelines themselves are weak:** of the 12 appraised, only two ('Oral herpes simplex', and an infection management guide for primary care) were rated as recommended, one as recommended with modifications and nine as not recommended. Just eight of their 83 recommendations carried a strength grade. [S2]
- **Few guidelines give specific advice on diagnosing or screening for labial herpes;** most fold it into HSV infection generally, leaving gaps in differential diagnosis and risk stratification. [S2]

## Painful mouth ulcers

- **For sore mouth ulcers:** paracetamol and lidocaine gel for pain, plus a saltwater or 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthwash (10 mL held in the mouth for 1 minute, used 3 times daily) to keep the mouth clean. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Red flags for urgent medical consult:** a mouth ulcer lasting over 2 weeks (possible cancer), ulcers that keep coming back, non-traumatic ulcers in a child (possible systemic disease), or severe ulcers in a young child. [S1]
- ⚠️ **A young child with severe mouth ulcers:** talk to a doctor about antiviral treatment, check for dehydration because a sore mouth stops drinking, and send to hospital if the child is not eating. [S1]
