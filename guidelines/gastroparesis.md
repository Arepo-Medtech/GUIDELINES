# Idiopathic gastroparesis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with idiopathic gastroparesis (IGP): assessment, gastric emptying testing, nutrition, prokinetic and antiemetic drugs, neuromodulators, psychological care and interventional options. Diabetic and postsurgical gastroparesis are outside the source's scope. Vomiting in children is in `vomiting`; terminal-phase nausea is in `palliative-care-terminal-phase-medicines`. The PBS row 'Nausea or gastric stasis' (metoclopramide) is broader than IGP. PBS access is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **Gastroparesis classically means nausea and vomiting after meals, early satiety, fullness and bloating, caused by delayed gastric emptying without mechanical obstruction.** Idiopathic gastroparesis (IGP) is the subtype with no identifiable cause. [S1]
- **Treat IGP as a sensorimotor disorder, not just slow emptying.** It overlaps heavily with functional gastroduodenal disorders (functional dyspepsia is clinically indistinguishable) and with eating disorders. (Low-quality evidence; strong recommendation) [S1]

## Assess

- **Take a full medical, surgical, nutritional and psychosocial history, and review every medicine:** opioids, anticholinergics, antipsychotics, centrally acting and weight-loss drugs, cannabinoids and illicit substances can change emptying or symptoms. If they cannot be stopped, explain how that limits motility testing. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Screen for disordered eating and co-assess with an eating-disorder clinician when present.** Between 20% and 80% of people with an eating disorder may have delayed emptying, and up to 98% have functional gut symptoms. (Low-quality evidence; strong recommendation) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Exclude structural disease first with upper endoscopy;** alarm features warrant urgent endoscopy and cross-sectional imaging. Consider brain imaging for persistent unexplained nausea or focal neurological signs. [S1]
- **Baseline bloods:** haemoglobin, electrolytes, glucose, coeliac serology, thyroid function, haematinics, and macro- and micronutrient screens. Mucosal biopsies for eosinophils or mast cells are not recommended. [S1]
- **Gastric emptying scintigraphy over 4 hours with a standardised low-fat egg meal; more than 10% retained at 4 h is abnormal.** 13C-breath testing is an accepted alternative. Emptying correlates poorly with symptoms, so it is only one part of the assessment. [S1]
- **Optimise confounders (drugs, malnutrition, disordered eating) before testing;** if modifiable factors were present, consider repeating an abnormal study 3–12 months later. (Very low-quality evidence; conditional; borderline endorsement) [S1]
- **Do not routinely test for vascular compression, hypermobility, mast cell disorders, autonomic dysfunction or dysbiosis;** involve a subspecialist if one is suspected. [S1]

## Nutrition

- **Every patient needs a full assessment by a gut-focused dietitian when diagnosed, and again whenever clinically needed.** Micronutrient deficiencies are common, for example potassium (86%), vitamin E (80%) and magnesium (72%). [S1]
- **Most patients should be managed by eating, not tubes:** up to 60% improve with diet plus a prokinetic. Use 6–10 small, well-chewed or blended meals a day, oral supplements if intake falls short, and stay upright 1–2 h after eating. A low-fat diet is unproven and contraindicated in malnutrition. [S1]
- **A 6-week low-FODMAP trial may help epigastric symptoms, but restrictive diets are contraindicated with malnutrition or disordered eating.** Treat constipation with standard therapy. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Avoid tube feeding where possible, and avoid parenteral nutrition.** Temporary nasogastric (not post-pyloric) feeding only for a malnourished patient who keeps losing weight and is medically unstable despite intensive oral support; long-term enteral feeding only after formal multidisciplinary review, because it does not reliably help and risks harm. There is no evidence for parenteral nutrition, which adds infection risk. [S1]

## Drug treatment

- ⚠️ **First line is a prokinetic: metoclopramide or domperidone,** the only two approved for gastroparesis in Australia. Evidence is limited and antiemetic use is largely empirical. (Low-quality evidence; conditional) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Metoclopramide adverse effects** include acute dystonia, QT prolongation, tardive dyskinesia, raised prolactin and galactorrhoea. Domperidone stays out of the brain, so it causes fewer neurological effects. [S1]
- **Off-label options:** prucalopride (improved symptoms and emptying over 4 weeks, no cardiac effects) and erythromycin (short-term benefit; tachyphylaxis limits it). Cisapride was withdrawn for arrhythmias. [S1]
- **Antiemetics** (prochlorperazine; promethazine or cyclizine) are used empirically; intravenous cyclizine can cause euphoria and dependence. Haloperidol beat placebo for nausea in emergency presentations, and granisetron helped in open-label studies. [S1]
- **Neuromodulators are second-line adjuncts, chosen for the dominant symptom.** Nortriptyline 10–75 mg did not beat placebo in its one RCT; amitriptyline 50 mg helped functional dyspepsia; mirtazapine 15 mg improved nausea, vomiting and appetite in an open trial; buspirone modestly eased bloating. (Low-quality evidence; conditional) [S1]
- **Cannabinoids:** evidence is insufficient to recommend them; they slow emptying yet may ease symptoms. [S1]

## Psychological care and procedures

- **Offer evidence-based brain-gut psychological therapies early,** and include mental health clinicians in the team when there is significant psychosocial or psychiatric comorbidity. Anxiety (49%) and depression (39%) are common. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Procedures are not recommended on current evidence:** intrapyloric botulinum toxin, surgical pyloroplasty, gastric electrical stimulation and G-POEM should be tried only after multidisciplinary consensus in refractory IGP. [S1]
- **If first-line treatment fails, specialist tertiary multidisciplinary input is the standard of care.** [S1]
