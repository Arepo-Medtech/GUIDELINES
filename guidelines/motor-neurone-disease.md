# Motor neurone disease (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** disease-modifying drugs (riluzole, edaravone, tofersen), multidisciplinary care, communication, nutrition and gastrostomy, cough, secretions and ventilation, symptom management (cramps, spasticity, saliva, emotional lability, pain, sleep), psychological support and end-of-life planning for adults with ALS/MND. S1 does not cover diagnosis, gene testing or frontotemporal dementia care; S2 adds diagnostic criteria, the mimic work-up and genetic counselling, while FTD care is still not covered. Related: `spasticity` (chronic spasticity). PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ⚠️ International guideline (European Academy of Neurology, 2024). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **The sources differ on edaravone.** The PBS lists edaravone for ALS (authority required). S1 (EAN) does **not** recommend intravenous or oral edaravone outside a clinical trial (interim recommendation, pending a European phase III trial of the oral form); S2 (PACTALS) suggests considering it for milder, early patients (weak recommendation); S3 (Australian Prescriber) limits its expected benefit to early ALS like the pivotal trial's population. Tofersen, recommended in S1 and S2 for SOD1 ALS, is not on the PBS list for this condition and in Australia is available only through the Special Access Scheme (S3).

> ℹ️ **S2 is the Asia-Pacific (PACTALS) guideline, 2025.** Its members include Australia and New Zealand and its lead authors are Australian, but it is written for very different health systems across the region. **S3 is an Australian Prescriber new drug comment**, not a guideline.

## Recognise

- **In ALS, motor neurons die progressively** in the brainstem, spinal cord and motor cortex, causing worsening weakness, stiffness and wasting. Median survival is about 3 years from onset, and death is usually from respiratory failure. [S1]
- **Cognition and behaviour matter:** up to 50% have frontal or temporal extra-motor involvement, and about 10% meet criteria for frontotemporal dementia (FTD) at diagnosis. With FTD, the neurologist should assess capacity to decide and consent before treatment decisions. [S1]
- **A gene mutation is found in about 20%.** Rapid gene testing for everyone with ALS is increasingly favoured, because treatments now target specific genetic subtypes. [S1]

## Diagnosis (Asia-Pacific guideline)

- **Refer promptly to a neurologist experienced in ALS** once a progressive motor weakness without explanation is found. [S2]
- **Diagnose with the Gold Coast criteria,** which are simpler and more sensitive than El Escorial or Awaji: progressive motor loss after previously normal function; combined upper and lower motor neuron dysfunction in one region, or lower motor neuron dysfunction in two or more; and tests that exclude mimics. [S2]
- **Exclude mimics with nerve conduction studies and EMG, MRI of brain and spinal cord, and relevant blood and CSF tests.** In selected patients add: vitamin B12, parathyroid hormone and thyroid function; a heavy-metal screen (lead especially); HIV and syphilis serology; vasculitis, paraneoplastic and myasthenia antibodies; and gene tests for SMA and Kennedy disease. [S2]
- **Genetics differ across the region:** SOD1 variants are the commonest genetic cause in many Asia-Pacific countries but not in Australia or New Zealand, and C9orf72 expansions dominate in European and North American populations. Genetic testing needs counselling that weighs risks and benefits, including a variant found without any family history. [S2]

## Disease-modifying treatment

- ⚠️ **Offer riluzole to everyone with ALS at diagnosis, for life** (strong). The dose is 50 mg twice daily. If side effects occur, consider a lower dose and reassess; stop if they persist (weak). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Edaravone, intravenous or oral, is not recommended outside a clinical trial** (interim recommendation, to be reviewed after the European phase III oral trial). [S1]
- **Progressive ALS from a pathogenic SOD1 mutation: offer tofersen first line** (strong). Warn about possible serious adverse events and the burden of intrathecal dosing; weigh benefit and harm carefully when progression is slow. [S1]
- **Not recommended outside trials:** cell-based therapies and AMX0035, until positive phase III data are available. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Riluzole monitoring (S2):** watch for side effects clinically and with liver function tests; if they occur, lower the dose and reassess. Stop it for persistent adverse events, at the terminal (palliative) stage, or after tracheostomy. Common effects are nausea, weakness, vomiting, diarrhoea, poor appetite, dizziness and a rise in ALT. A liquid form helps when bulbar weakness makes swallowing hard. [S2]
- **Edaravone, the Asia-Pacific view (S2, weak recommendation):** consider intravenous or oral edaravone at diagnosis for milder patients like those in the pivotal trial, where available and affordable, preferably with riluzole. IV dose: 60 mg/day for 14 days then 14 days of observation; from cycle 2, treatment on 10 of every 14 days. [S2]
- **Oral edaravone evidence is mixed:** the phase III ADORE trial showed no benefit on function or survival (with a different product and broader entry criteria), whereas the approved oral suspension (105 mg/5 mL) matched intravenous exposure in a small study. [S2]
- **Tofersen regimen (SOD1 ALS):** 100 mg as a 15 mL intrathecal bolus on days 0, 14 and 28, then monthly. Expect lumbar-puncture pain, low-pressure headache, limb and back pain and falls; myelitis, aseptic meningitis, radiculopathy and raised intracranial pressure are uncommon. Do not give it to symptom-free SOD1 carriers until the ATLAS trial reports. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **Australian access (S3):** riluzole is approved for ALS in Australia; tofersen can be obtained only through the Special Access Scheme. [S3]
- 🇦🇺 **Who may benefit from edaravone (S3):** it may slow progression only in early ALS (onset within 2 years) matching the pivotal trial: living independently with mild disease, forced vital capacity of at least 80%, and at least 2 points on every ALSFRS-R item. Benefit outside that group is unproven, and a trial in more advanced (grade 3) disease was negative. [S3]
- ⚠️ **Edaravone infusion (S3):** 60 mg (2 ampoules) in 100 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride IV over 60 minutes, daily for 14 days then 14 days off; later cycles give 10 daily doses within 14 days, then 14 days off. No dose change is needed for kidney or liver impairment, but it is not recommended on renal replacement therapy. The ampoules contain sodium bisulfite, which can provoke allergic or asthmatic reactions, and hypersensitivity and anaphylaxis have been reported. [S3]

## Multidisciplinary care

- **Care should come from a coordinated specialist ALS multidisciplinary team (MDT)**, in clinic or community, with planned appointments and home or remote visits for people who cannot attend. Reassess usually every 3–6 months, depending on how fast the disease progresses, and track function with the ALSFRS-R. [S1]
- **Communication:** have a speech and language therapist assess without delay, supply augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) equipment promptly, and refer to a specialised AAC hub for complex needs such as voice banking or eye-gaze access. [S1]

## Nutrition and gastrostomy

- **Look for why weight or intake is falling** (swallowing, breathing, depression, appetite, wasting, arm weakness) and involve a dietitian, speech therapist or occupational therapist. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Raise gastrostomy early and revisit it as ALS progresses**, explaining the extra risks of placing it late. If breathing is failing, start non-invasive ventilation (NIV) first and insert the gastrostomy with NIV in place. Once referred, it should happen without delay; a nasogastric tube can bridge the wait. [S1]

## Breathing

- ⚠️ **Offer NIV to every patient with symptoms, signs or tests pointing to respiratory insufficiency** (strong), whatever their bulbar function. [S1]
- **Weak cough:** start with unassisted breath stacking or manual assisted cough; move to assisted breath stacking (for example a lung volume recruitment bag) and then a mechanical cough assist device if needed. [S1]
- **Plan invasive ventilation discussions in advance** as part of advance care planning, to avoid emergency ventilation. Diaphragmatic pacing should not be used. [S1]
- **Breathlessness when ventilation is not tolerated, fails or disease is end stage:** consider opioids, and benzodiazepines when anxiety makes breathlessness worse. [S1]
- **Monitor breathing regularly (S2):** check for hypercapnia where resources allow; the interval may be every 3 months for the ALSFRS-R and every 6 months for supine vital capacity. Consider starting NIV early according to FVC; maximal inspiratory and sniff nasal pressures can also guide it. A peak cough flow below 270 L/min marks a weak cough with more retained secretions and infection. [S2]
- **Laryngospasm (S2):** look for triggers (reflux, postnasal drip or drooling, swallowing problems, smoke, strong smells, cold or dry air, emotion, spicy food), advise avoiding them, consider speech pathology, and reassure patient and carers that episodes resolve on their own. Treat reflux, with a prokinetic at night if needed. [S2]

## Symptoms

- **Cramps:** consider sodium-channel blockers (ranolazine, quinine sulfate, mexiletine, carbamazepine), gabapentin, pregabalin or baclofen. Start quinine low (100 to 200 mg per day) and check for cardiac side effects before and after prescribing. With coexisting spasticity, try baclofen first. [S1]
- **Spasticity:** physical therapy, then consider cannabinoids, baclofen, tizanidine or gabapentin; for focal spasticity not helped by these, consider botulinum toxin. [S1]
- **Drooling (sialorrhoea):** anticholinergics first line (such as amitriptyline, atropine, glycopyrrolate); consider dextromethorphan/quinidine with emotional lability, botulinum toxin from a specialist centre if drugs fail, then radiotherapy. Surgery is not suggested. [S1]
- **Emotional lability:** consider an SSRI, a tricyclic or dextromethorphan/quinidine, after distinguishing it from depression and FTD. [S1]
- **Deep vein thrombosis:** explain the higher risk from immobility and that evidence on anticoagulant prevention is insufficient; advise passive physiotherapy, leg elevation and compression stockings. [S1]

## End of life

- **Offer end-of-life discussions at trigger points:** at diagnosis, when breathing changes significantly, and when gastrostomy or NIV is needed. Start earlier if communication or cognition is expected to decline. [S1]
- **Near the end of life, make sure these are in place promptly:** a way to communicate, specialist palliative care, equipment, and anticipatory medicines (opioids and benzodiazepines for breathlessness and anxiety, antimuscarinics for saliva and secretions). [S1]
