# Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and families with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: diagnosis, sudden-death risk stratification, genetic testing, general management and screening of relatives. Fabry disease, one HCM phenocopy, is covered in `fabry-disease`. PBS access is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **HCM is left ventricular hypertrophy arising from the heart muscle itself,** not explained by aortic stenosis, hypertension, thyroid disease or other loading. It may affect as many as 1 in 200 people, making it the most common inherited heart condition. [S1]
- **Inheritance is autosomal dominant with variable penetrance:** each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of carrying the mutation, regardless of sex. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Under age 35, HCM is the leading structural cause of sudden cardiac death,** competitive athletes included. Death happens most often during or just after exertion, though it can occur at rest. [S1]
- **Symptoms range from none to heart failure and sudden death,** and include angina-type chest pain, breathlessness (including orthopnoea and nocturnal dyspnoea), fatigue, syncope or near-syncope, and palpitations. [S1]
- **On examination:** a jerky, rapidly rising pulse, a prominent LV impulse, and an apical systolic murmur that gets louder with Valsalva (dynamic obstruction); a fourth heart sound is common. The ECG may show LVH voltage, T-wave inversion and Q waves. [S1]

## Confirm the diagnosis

- ⚠️ **Echocardiography is the key test:** in adults a maximal LV wall thickness of 15 mm or more is diagnostic, while 13–14 mm counts as borderline except in a family with definite HCM. It also shows cavity size, systolic and diastolic function, outflow tract obstruction and mitral regurgitation. [S1]
- **Consider phenocopies, especially when hypertrophy is concentric rather than asymmetric:** infiltrative disease such as Fabry disease and amyloidosis, and glycogen or lysosomal storage disorders. [S1]
- **Cardiac MRI uses the same 15 mm threshold** and adds quantification of fibrosis by late gadolinium enhancement and better views of regions echo misses, such as apical HCM. [S1]
- **Exercise testing and Holter monitoring, plus the family's cardiac history,** help confirm the diagnosis and build a sudden-death risk profile. [S1]

## Sudden-death risk and ICD

- ⚠️ **Major risk factors:** previous cardiac arrest or ventricular tachycardia; family history of premature sudden cardiac death; LV wall thickness of 30 mm or more; documented non-sustained VT (3 or more beats at 120 bpm or faster); and unexplained syncope. [S1]
- **Most clinicians advise an ICD if a patient has even one of the five major factors,** although some argue for requiring at least 2. [S1]
- **Other factors add risk:** abnormal blood pressure response to exercise, myocardial ischaemia, LVOT obstruction of 30 mmHg or more (at rest or provoked), late gadolinium enhancement, presentation before age 21, and a large left atrium. Early data suggest LGE above 15% of LV myocardium matters. [S1]
- **When only minor factors are present, the ICD decision is hard;** weigh clinical judgement with what the patient wants. [S1]
- **The HCM Risk-SCD score estimates 5-year sudden-death risk** from these factors plus LVOT gradient, left atrial size and age: under 4% low, 4–6% intermediate, over 6% high. At the time it was still being validated in other cohorts. [S1]

## Genetic testing

- **At least 13 genes cause HCM, mostly sarcomeric** (notably MYH7 and MYBPC3). One mutation is enough; people carrying two or three mutations may have more severe disease. [S1]
- **Testing the 10 commonest genes finds a cause in 40–50%, rising to 70–80% with a definite family history.** [S1]
- **Finding the family mutation allows cascade testing:** relatives who test negative can be spared lifelong surveillance, and the result can inform preimplantation and antenatal testing. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Genetic counselling must come before any genetic test,** and testing is best done in a specialised multidisciplinary clinic. [S1]

## Treat

- **Options run from no treatment and lifestyle change (every patient should avoid competitive sport),** through drugs (beta-blockers, diuretics or calcium channel blockers), to surgical septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation for significant outflow obstruction that stays symptomatic despite drugs. [S1]
- ⚠️ **An ICD is the most effective protection against sudden death for those at highest risk,** clearly better than the older empirical use of amiodarone or beta-blockers. [S1]

## Screen the family

- ⚠️ **Screen every first-degree relative:** at minimum a cardiologist's examination, an ECG and a transthoracic echo; cardiac MRI is increasingly used too. [S1]
- **Suggested screening intervals, tailored to each person:** ages 12–20 every 1–1.5 years; 21–40 every 2–3 years; over 40 every 3–5 years. Under 12 (0–11) it is optional unless there is suspicion, symptoms or a malignant family history. [S1]
- **Surveillance is lifelong but less frequent with age;** screening second-degree relatives is decided family by family. [S1]
- **Take a detailed family history,** including the age and circumstances of any death, and retrieve medical records and post-mortem reports; sudden cardiac death in a relative counts as a major risk factor. [S1]
