# Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and families with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: diagnosis, imaging, sudden-death risk and ICDs, genetic testing and family screening, drug treatment of outflow obstruction including mavacamten and its echo monitoring, septal reduction, atrial fibrillation, pregnancy and exercise. Fabry disease, one HCM phenocopy, is in `fabry-disease`; heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is in `heart-failure`.

## Recognise

- **HCM is left ventricular hypertrophy arising from the heart muscle itself,** not explained by aortic stenosis, hypertension, thyroid disease or other loading. It may affect as many as 1 in 200 people, making it the most common inherited heart condition. [S1]
- **Inheritance is autosomal dominant with variable penetrance:** each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of carrying the mutation, regardless of sex. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Under age 35, HCM is the leading structural cause of sudden cardiac death,** competitive athletes included. Death happens most often during or just after exertion, though it can occur at rest. [S1]
- **Symptoms range from none to heart failure and sudden death,** and include angina-type chest pain, breathlessness (including orthopnoea and nocturnal dyspnoea), fatigue, syncope or near-syncope, and palpitations. [S1]
- **On examination:** a jerky, rapidly rising pulse, a prominent LV impulse, and an apical systolic murmur that gets louder with Valsalva (dynamic obstruction); a fourth heart sound is common. The ECG may show LVH voltage, T-wave inversion and Q waves. [S1]

## Confirm the diagnosis

- ⚠️ **Echocardiography is the key test:** in adults a maximal LV wall thickness of 15 mm or more is diagnostic, while 13–14 mm counts as borderline except in a family with definite HCM. It also shows cavity size, systolic and diastolic function, outflow tract obstruction and mitral regurgitation. [S1]
- **Current guidelines agree:** an end-diastolic wall thickness of 15 mm or more in any LV segment, or 13 mm or more when a relative has confirmed HCM. Per AHA/ACC, 13–14 mm can be diagnostic with a pathogenic or likely pathogenic sarcomere variant; per ESC it needs other features weighed. [S2]
- **Start with transthoracic echocardiography, using provocation (such as Valsalva) to find the peak LVOT gradient.** If symptomatic with a resting gradient of 50 mmHg or less, do an exercise echocardiogram. [S2]
- **Consider phenocopies, especially when hypertrophy is concentric rather than asymmetric:** infiltrative disease such as Fabry disease and amyloidosis, and glycogen or lysosomal storage disorders. [S1]
- **Cardiac MRI uses the same 15 mm threshold** and adds quantification of fibrosis by late gadolinium enhancement and better views of regions echo misses, such as apical HCM. [S1]
- **When to do cardiac MRI:** the ESC recommends it with late gadolinium enhancement at initial evaluation; the AHA/ACC reserves it for inconclusive echocardiography or a suspected alternative diagnosis (athlete's heart, storage or infiltrative disease). [S2]
- **Exercise testing and Holter monitoring, plus the family's cardiac history,** help confirm the diagnosis and build a sudden-death risk profile. [S1]

## Sudden-death risk and ICD

- ⚠️ **Secondary prevention: an ICD after cardiac arrest from ventricular arrhythmia or documented sustained VT.** All guidelines agree. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Major risk factors:** previous cardiac arrest or ventricular tachycardia; family history of premature sudden cardiac death; LV wall thickness of 30 mm or more; documented non-sustained VT (3 or more beats at 120 bpm or faster); and unexplained syncope. [S1]
- **Most clinicians advise an ICD if a patient has even one of the five major factors,** although some argue for requiring at least 2. [S1]
- **Other factors add risk:** abnormal blood pressure response to exercise, myocardial ischaemia, LVOT obstruction of 30 mmHg or more (at rest or provoked), late gadolinium enhancement, presentation before age 21, and a large left atrium. Early data suggest LGE above 15% of LV myocardium matters. [S1]
- **When only minor factors are present, the ICD decision is hard;** weigh clinical judgement with what the patient wants. [S1]
- **The HCM Risk-SCD score estimates 5-year sudden-death risk** from these factors plus LVOT gradient, left atrial size and age: under 4% low, 4–6% intermediate, over 6% high. At the time it was still being validated in other cohorts. [S1]
- **ESC approach: the HCM Risk-SCD calculator** (age, maximum wall thickness, left atrial diameter, maximum LVOT gradient, family history of sudden death, non-sustained VT, unexplained syncope), repeated every 1–2 years. Consider an ICD at a 5-year risk of 6% or more; individually at 4–6%; and below 4% if LGE is 15% or more or LVEF is below 50%. [S2]
- ⚠️ **AHA/ACC approach: offer an ICD if one or more major risk factors is present:** HCM sudden death in a first-degree or close relative aged 50 or under; LV hypertrophy of 30 mm or more; recent syncope thought to be arrhythmic; an LV apical aneurysm with scar; or LVEF below 50%. It reassesses risk every 1–2 years and gives more weight to longer, faster, more frequent non-sustained VT. [S2]
- **Where they disagree:** ESC does not treat an apical aneurysm alone as a strong enough predictor to justify a primary-prevention ICD; AHA/ACC does. [S2]
- ⚠️ **An ICD is the most effective protection against sudden death for those at highest risk,** clearly better than the older empirical use of amiodarone or beta-blockers. [S1]
- **A subcutaneous ICD is a reasonable alternative** to a transvenous device when no bradycardia pacing, resynchronisation or anti-tachycardia pacing is expected. [S2]

## Genetic testing

- **At least 13 genes cause HCM, mostly sarcomeric** (notably MYH7 and MYBPC3). One mutation is enough; people carrying two or three mutations may have more severe disease. [S1]
- **Testing the 10 commonest genes finds a cause in 40–50%, rising to 70–80% with a definite family history.** [S1]
- **Finding the family mutation allows cascade testing:** relatives who test negative can be spared lifelong surveillance, and the result can inform preimplantation and antenatal testing. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Genetic counselling must come before any genetic test,** and testing is best done in a specialised multidisciplinary clinic. [S1]
- **Offer genetic testing to the patient to enable cascade screening of relatives,** and in borderline or atypical cases or suspected phenocopies. Do not test phenotype-negative relatives unless a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant has been found in the proband. [S2]

## Screen the family

- ⚠️ **Screen every first-degree relative:** at minimum a cardiologist's examination, an ECG and a transthoracic echo; cardiac MRI is increasingly used too. [S1]
- **Suggested screening intervals, tailored to each person:** ages 12–20 every 1–1.5 years; 21–40 every 2–3 years; over 40 every 3–5 years. Under 12 (0–11) it is optional unless there is suspicion, symptoms or a malignant family history. [S1]
- **Surveillance is lifelong but less frequent with age;** screening second-degree relatives is decided family by family. [S1]
- **Take a detailed family history,** including the age and circumstances of any death, and retrieve medical records and post-mortem reports; sudden cardiac death in a relative counts as a major risk factor. [S1]

## Treat outflow obstruction

- **Options run from no treatment and lifestyle change (every patient should avoid competitive sport),** through drugs (beta-blockers, diuretics or calcium channel blockers), to surgical septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation for significant outflow obstruction that stays symptomatic despite drugs. [S1]
- ⚠️ **First line for symptoms from LVOT obstruction: a non-vasodilating beta-blocker; if not tolerated, a non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker.** All three current guidelines agree. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Mavacamten (a cardiac myosin inhibitor) or disopyramide is added when symptoms of obstruction persist despite a beta-blocker or calcium channel blocker.** It is a Class 1 recommendation in the AHA/ACC guideline and Class IIa in the ESC guideline; the older Japanese guideline predates myosin inhibitors. [S2]
- **Septal reduction therapy (myectomy or alcohol septal ablation) belongs in experienced centres with a multidisciplinary team.** ESC and JCS: gradient of 50 mmHg or more (rest or provoked) with NYHA class III–IV despite maximal drugs. AHA/ACC: symptoms from obstruction despite optimal therapy, a gradient around 50 mmHg or more with septal hypertrophy and systolic anterior motion, and enough septal thickness. [S2]
- **Choose myectomy when other lesions also need surgery; choose alcohol septal ablation when surgery is contraindicated or too risky.** [S2]

## Mavacamten: echo monitoring

- ⚠️ **Mavacamten can reduce LV systolic function and cause heart failure in a small percentage of patients, so safety monitoring with echocardiography is required.** [S3]
- **Before starting:** a full baseline echocardiogram on standard treatment, and CYP2C19 pharmacogenomic testing. [S3]
- ⚠️ **Poor (slow) CYP2C19 metabolisers are exposed to higher than intended doses, so titration is adjusted;** while CYP status is pending, start at 2.5 mg. [S3]
- ⚠️ **Echo schedule (UK NICE, per the product information): weeks 4, 8 and 12 after starting, then every 24 weeks long term, and 4 weeks after any dose change.** [S3]
- ⚠️ **Interrupt treatment at any point if LVEF is below 50%.** [S3]
- **Use echo contrast when two or more segments cannot be seen:** contrast LVEF has a minimal detectable difference of about 4%, against 9–11% without contrast, which matters when LVEF sits near a decision boundary. [S3]
- **Stress echocardiography is not part of routine mavacamten surveillance.** [S3]

## Non-obstructive HCM

- **For angina-like chest pain or breathlessness without obstruction, use a beta-blocker or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker.** ESC adds oral nitrates or ranolazine for angina; AHA/ACC and JCS support diuretics for exertional breathlessness, used cautiously if obstruction is present. [S2]

## Atrial fibrillation

- ⚠️ **Anticoagulate every patient with HCM and atrial fibrillation for life, whatever the CHA2DS2-VASc score.** AHA/ACC names direct oral anticoagulants first line over warfarin. [S2]

## Pregnancy and exercise

- ⚠️ **Mavacamten is contraindicated in pregnancy because it is teratogenic** (AHA/ACC). Offer genetic and reproductive counselling before conception, review medicines in advance, plan vaginal delivery for most women, and anticoagulate with low-molecular-weight heparin or a vitamin K antagonist. [S2]
- **Mild to moderate exercise benefits all able patients,** and genotype-positive, phenotype-negative people are not barred from high-intensity exercise or competitive sport. [S2]
- **High-intensity sport is where they differ:** ESC advises against it in high-risk patients and in obstruction with exercise-induced complex ventricular arrhythmias; AHA/ACC does not restrict most patients universally, asks for expert shared decision-making, and advises against an ICD placed only to allow competitive sport. [S2]

## Newer evidence

- **The guidelines mention only mavacamten among myosin inhibitors.** Phase 3 data for aficamten (shorter half-life) and longer-term and real-world mavacamten data were published after them, and myosin inhibitors are being tested in non-obstructive HCM. [S2]
