# Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria — clone monitoring and treatment, with a focus on aplastic anaemia

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** how PNH arises and presents, flow-cytometric diagnosis, laboratory markers, clone monitoring and complement-inhibitor treatment, as set out by a seven-country Asia–Pacific panel whose recommendations focus on PNH arising in aplastic anaemia. S1 does not cover proximal complement inhibitors, breakthrough haemolysis or anticoagulation; edition 2.0 adds S2 (Singapore consensus, 2024), which covers screening, meningococcal vaccination, monitoring on eculizumab, breakthrough haemolysis, proximal inhibitors, anticoagulation and pregnancy. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **PNH is caused by somatic mutations in haematopoietic stem cells.** Blood cells lose some or all of their complement-regulatory proteins, which leads to intravascular haemolysis, thrombosis and bone marrow failure. [S1]
- **PNH clones are common in aplastic anaemia (AA):** across seven studies they were found in 25–62% (median 40%) of patients with AA. [S1]
- **Clone size tracks the clinical picture.** About two-thirds of clones are small (granulocyte clone usually <30%), seen with marrow failure, without clinical haemolysis and with low thrombotic risk. About one-third are large (usually >50%), with classical haemolytic PNH and a high thrombotic risk. [S1]
- **Myeloid neoplasia is a late risk:** it was estimated at about 12% of patients with PNH/AA over 10 years, and generally carries a poor prognosis. [S1]
- **When to test for PNH (Singapore panel):** Coombs-negative haemolytic anaemia with haemoglobinuria, kidney dysfunction, LDH of 1.5 times the upper limit or more, raised reticulocytes, low haptoglobin or unexplained iron deficiency; unexplained or unusual-site thrombosis (such as cerebral, portal or hepatic veins), or clots despite adequate anticoagulation, especially under 45; and marrow failure (aplastic anaemia, MDS) with signs of haemolysis. [S2]

## Diagnose

- **Diagnosis is by peripheral-blood flow cytometry.** It needs deficiency of at least two different GPI-anchored proteins in two different cell lines (granulocytes, monocytes, red cells). White-cell (granulocyte and monocyte) results estimate clone size better than red-cell results, which haemolysis or transfusion can distort. [S1]
- **Match assay sensitivity to the question.** A low-sensitivity assay (detects cells at 1%) is acceptable for classical haemolytic PNH; a high-sensitivity assay (0.01%) is needed to find the small clones seen in AA. [S1]
- **LDH marks intravascular haemolysis:** it can rise up to 4–5 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), and untreated PNH generally shows LDH ≥1.5 times ULN. [S1]
- **A normal reticulocyte count does not exclude PNH when AA coexists:** the count is usually raised in PNH, but marrow failure can blunt it. [S1]
- **Individualise assessment and refer** to a specialised centre with expertise in PNH and AA. [S1]
- **Also screen selected MDS patients:** those with haemolysis, a hypoplastic marrow or refractory cytopenia. [S1]
- **After diagnosis (S2), confirm and stage the haemolysis:** full blood and reticulocyte counts, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, urine haemosiderin, D-dimer and clotting times, creatinine and proteinuria; the direct antiglobulin test is negative in untreated PNH. Image for clots (CT or ultrasound), do echocardiography for pulmonary hypertension and brain MRI for headache or neurological symptoms, and examine the marrow if aplastic anaemia or MDS is suspected. [S2]

## Monitor

- **Panel recommendation for AA:** screen for PNH at AA diagnosis. If no PNH cells are found, repeat flow cytometry at least every 2–3 years and check LDH and reticulocytes at every visit. Do on-demand flow cytometry for a high LDH or new significant symptoms such as thrombosis. Once PNH cells are detected, repeat flow cytometry at least yearly. [S1]
- **Other guidelines set intervals of** every 3–6 months for at least 2 years, then yearly once stable. There is no consensus on the best frequency. [S1]
- **A D-dimer can help** detect subclinical thrombosis when LDH is raised. [S1]
- ⚠️ **The 'clinically meaningful' clone-size cut-off depends on the country's reimbursement rules**, e.g. a red-cell clone of 1% in Japan but 10% in many countries. [S1]
- **Subclinical PNH (S2): no treatment, but review every 6–12 months** (less often when stable), because the clone can expand into haemolytic or thrombotic disease; small clones in marrow failure are reviewed on the same 6–12-month cycle. [S2]
- **On eculizumab (S2), check full blood and reticulocyte counts, LDH and bilirubin monthly for the first 3 months, then every 3 months,** and reassess the benefit every 6 months. Stopping may be considered for a non-adherent patient after a full assessment. [S2]

## Treat

- **C5 complement inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab) are the main treatment for classical PNH.** They are generally indicated for symptomatic patients with haemolysis and high disease activity. [S1]
- **Subclinical PNH needs no PNH-specific treatment.** When PNH occurs within another marrow disorder, treat the marrow disorder first. [S1]
- **Small clones in AA without haemolysis do not benefit from complement inhibitors;** monitor them for change instead. [S1]
- **If a clone expands in AA and causes haemolysis or thrombosis,** manage it as classical PNH with a complement inhibitor. [S1]
- **Let the dominant problem decide:** if haemolysis predominates, follow PNH guidance; if marrow failure is severe, follow AA guidance even when haemolysis is mild or moderate. [S1]
- **Eculizumab can be given alongside immunosuppressive therapy:** observational studies found no loss of effect and no safety concerns, whatever the order. [S1]
- **Allogeneic HSCT can cure PNH but is not first-line** because of transplant-related morbidity and mortality. In AA with PNH it is reserved for severe AA with no other options, after discussion with the patient and family. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Vaccinate against meningococcus before a C5 inhibitor (S2):** quadrivalent ACWY plus serogroup B (where available) at least 2 weeks before the first eculizumab dose. Eculizumab raises the risk of life-threatening Neisseria infection, estimated at 0.5% a year (5% over 10 years). [S2]
- **Ravulizumab** has about four times eculizumab's half-life, allowing 8-weekly dosing, and in trials it proved non-inferior to eculizumab in controlling haemolysis and avoiding transfusion. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Breakthrough haemolysis affects 11–27% of patients on eculizumab.** Once confirmed, raise the dose (for example 1200 mg every 14 days), shorten the interval (for example every 12 days), or switch to ravulizumab, which keeps free C5 lower. Rare C5 variants (mainly in Japanese patients) stop both drugs binding, and C5 inhibitors can leave C3-mediated extravascular haemolysis. [S2]
- **Proximal (C3) inhibition with pegcetacoplan targets extravascular haemolysis.** In the 16-week PEGASUS trial it raised haemoglobin by 3.84 g/dL more than eculizumab, and 85% versus 15% were transfusion-free, though 3 of 41 stopped because of breakthrough haemolysis. Oral iptacopan (a factor B inhibitor) was approved on phase II data. The panel's criteria for switching from C5 to C3 therapy are in a table not read. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Anticoagulation (S2):** with a large clone and no complement inhibitor, consider primary prophylaxis unless thrombocytopenia or bleeding risk forbids it; it may be stopped once a complement inhibitor starts. After a clot, several guidelines advise long-term anticoagulation, but 3–6 months may be enough when disease is well controlled on a complement inhibitor; consider lifelong treatment with extra risk factors or after a life-threatening clot. [S2]
- **Supportive care (S2):** folic acid, vitamin B12 and red-cell transfusion for symptomatic anaemia; oral or IV iron for losses through haemoglobinuria; steroids only short-term, for haemolytic episodes; analgesia for smooth-muscle dystonia. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Pregnancy is high risk** (maternal mortality estimated at 12–21%, plus miscarriage and preterm birth). The panel supports eculizumab in pregnancy, with frequent monitoring because higher doses may be needed, and anticoagulation, which may be extended through at least 6 weeks after delivery at the clinician's discretion. [S2]
