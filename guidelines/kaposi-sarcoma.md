## Recognise

- **Kaposi sarcoma is a multicentric tumour of lymphatic-endothelial cells infected with HHV-8.** Its four subtypes are classic, endemic (sub-Saharan Africa), epidemic (HIV) and iatrogenic (immunosuppression, especially transplant); the degree of immunosuppression drives onset and progression. [S1]
- **Lesions are purple, red-blue or brown macules, plaques or nodules** that can bleed, ulcerate or become warty; lymphoedema is common and may appear first. [S1]
- **Classic KS is usually indolent; HIV-related and post-transplant KS can be extensive and life-threatening.** [S1]
- **In HIV, KS remains the second most common cancer in Western countries.** Low CD4 is the main risk factor, but risk stays above the general population even with normal CD4 and suppressed viral load. [S1]
- **After organ transplant, KS risk is 50- to 500-fold higher,** peaking in the first 2 years; median onset is 13 months after transplant. [S1]

## Diagnose and stage

- **Biopsy is mandatory.** Staining for the HHV-8 LANA protein is the best way to tell KS from its mimics. [S1]
- **HHV-8 viral load in blood is not usable** to monitor KS or predict it in transplant recipients. [S1]
- **Only AIDS-related KS has a validated staging system (ACTG: tumour, immune, systemic symptoms);** since antiretroviral therapy, the tumour and symptom components carry the prognosis. Otherwise, sort patients into localised non-aggressive, locally aggressive or disseminated disease. [S1]
- **For classic KS, Brambilla's staging is now preferred over Krigel's:** stage I small macules confined to the lower limbs, II larger plaques still on the lower limbs, III multiple plaques and nodules on the lower limbs, IV lesions beyond the lower limbs. [S2]
- **Work-up for classic KS (NCCN, via S2):** clinical photography at baseline and follow-up and a chest X-ray; CT only for new gut or chest symptoms or unexpected findings. FDG PET/CT finds extracutaneous spread but misses some gut mucosal lesions. [S2]

## Local treatment

- **Because KS is multifocal, treating one lesion does not stop new ones and aggressive sarcoma-style surgery is not indicated.** Watchful observation suits asymptomatic immunocompetent patients; treat symptomatic lesions locally, and use systemic therapy for florid, disseminated or rapidly progressive disease or when local treatment fails. [S2]
- **Radiotherapy in classic KS: overall response 88–100%, complete response 58–93%, symptom relief in 95–100%;** randomised data show hypofractionated schedules work as well as conventional ones. Brachytherapy suits small lesions. Side effects (dry desquamation, pigmentation, lymphoedema) often improve. [S2]
- **No randomised trial compares the local options.** [S1]
- **Radiotherapy is among the most effective treatments for localised KS** (response 47% to 99%). Avoid more than 3 Gy per fraction and concurrent systemic therapy to limit late effects. [S1]
- **Surgery recurs often;** keep it for a few small, well-defined superficial lesions. [S1]
- **CO2 laser or superficial cryotherapy can work for a while on superficial lesions;** warn about lasting hypopigmentation. [S1]
- **Intralesional chemotherapy** (vinblastine, response about 70%) and **electrochemotherapy** (bleomycin with electroporation) are effective local options. [S1]
- **Topical options:** imiquimod helped about half of 17 patients with classic or endemic KS (local itch and redness in 53%); alitretinoin 0.1% gel gave a 37% response in HIV-related KS on antiretrovirals. [S1]
- **Other local response rates (S2):** imiquimod 5% cream about one-third complete and one-third partial response (47% in an HIV-negative phase I–II study); intralesional vincristine complete response up to 76%; electrochemotherapy with bleomycin complete response 65–89%; liquid-nitrogen cryotherapy complete remission in 63% (19/30). [S2]

## Systemic treatment

- **The aim is control, symptom relief and quality of life, not cure.** The preferred first-line drugs are paclitaxel and pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (PLD). [S1]
- ⚠️ **PLD 20 mg/m² every 3 weeks:** response 76% in HIV-related KS on antiretrovirals and higher in classic KS; responses last a median of about 5 months (HIV) and 25 months (classic). Grade IV neutropenia and hand–foot syndrome each occur in about 5%. [S1]
- **Paclitaxel 100 mg/m² every 2 weeks** gives about 60% response in HIV-related KS. Against PLD in a randomised trial, progression-free survival was similar (17.5 vs 12.2 months) but toxicity was greater, so it is second line in HIV-related KS. [S1]
- **For non-AIDS KS, the preferred paclitaxel schedule is 80 mg/m² weekly,** continuously or 3 weeks on and 1 off. [S1]
- **Vinblastine, oral etoposide and bleomycin are alternatives,** not first-line choices. [S1]
- ⚠️ **PLD (S2): 20–30 mg/m² intravenously every two to three weeks.** In a randomised trial it beat bleomycin-vincristine (response 46% vs 25%). Hand-foot syndrome and mucositis limit the dose; NCCN advises an echocardiogram before starting and periodically after. [S2]
- **S2 ranks PLD as the preferred first-line systemic therapy for classic KS, keeping taxanes for PLD resistance or intolerance;** no randomised trial compares systemic therapies head to head in classic KS. [S2]
- **Weekly paclitaxel in the largest classic-KS cohort:** complete response 15.9%, partial 63.7%, stable 13.6% (disease control over 90%). Nab-paclitaxel needs no steroid premedication and is better tolerated by older patients; neutropenia is the main dose-limiting toxicity. [S2]
- **Pomalidomide gave an 80% response in HIV-negative KS (phase I/II)** and is FDA-approved for KS in HIV-negative patients and in HIV-positive patients after antiretroviral failure; watch for liver and bone-marrow toxicity. Lenalidomide is less active (up to 40%). [S2]
- **Checkpoint inhibitors are active in classic KS:** nivolumab plus ipilimumab gave 78–93% response in 18 elderly patients (grade 3–4 events 22%), and pembrolizumab 71% in classic and endemic KS. Indirect evidence suggests higher and more durable responses than antiangiogenic drugs, but they have not been compared directly. [S2]
- **Low-dose interferon for classic KS is 3–5 million IU subcutaneously three times a week,** now rarely used. Sirolimus works in post-transplant KS but has shown no meaningful benefit in classic KS. [S2]

## By subtype

- **HIV-related KS: start antiretroviral therapy first;** most lesions regress. Add chemotherapy for poor-prognosis (T1) or rapidly progressive disease, and to prevent or treat IRIS. [S1]
- **With antiretrovirals, liposomal anthracyclines are first line and taxanes second.** The herpesvirus (KSHV) cannot be cleared and KS can recur; if long-term cytotoxics are not tolerated, pomalidomide or lenalidomide may be discussed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Post-transplant KS: reduce immunosuppression to the lowest level and switch to an mTOR inhibitor such as sirolimus** while protecting the graft; add systemic therapy for extensive or life-threatening disease. Avoid interferon alfa (rejection risk). [S1]
- **Classic/endemic KS needs systemic treatment when aggressive** (nodal or visceral disease, severe oedema, complications or rapid spread): usually liposomal anthracycline, less often weekly taxane. Low-dose interferon alfa is an option under 70 with normal cardiac function. [S1]

## Follow-up

- **Life-threatening disease (extensive HIV-KS, IRIS, severe post-transplant KS): review at least monthly and image at least every 3 months until stable.** Indolent classic KS can be seen every six months to a year, mainly clinically. Repeat biopsy only for atypical course or to confirm nodal or visceral spread. [S1]
