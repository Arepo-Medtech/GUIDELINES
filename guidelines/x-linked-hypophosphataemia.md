# X-linked hypophosphataemia (XLH)

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, biochemical and genetic diagnosis, conventional therapy (oral phosphate with calcitriol or alfacalcidol), burosumab, multidisciplinary referral, monitoring and transition to adult care for children and adults with XLH. The source's numbered consensus statements live in tables and figures that were stripped; claims come from its narrative text. Tumour-induced osteomalacia (also a burosumab PBS condition) is not covered. Related: `hypophosphataemia-children` (acquired hypophosphataemia and phosphate replacement), `vitamin-d-deficiency-children` (nutritional rickets, the main differential). PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ⚠️ International guideline (Asia-Pacific XLH Working Group, 2023; Australian co-authors, endorsed by ANZSPED and ANZBMS). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **S2 and S3 are the 2025 International Working Group GRADE guidelines** (children and adults; 50 and 43 experts from the Americas, Europe and Asia; J Clin Endocrinol Metab). They are newer than S1 and **recommend burosumab first line in children from 12 months**, where S1 reserves it for refractory disease or intolerance. Both views are shown; Australian access follows the PBS listings below.

> ℹ️ **Australia is ahead of much of the region on access:** the source notes that burosumab is subsidised on the PBS for both children and adults with XLH, whereas elsewhere in Asia-Pacific it is inconsistently available and oral phosphate may need compounding.

## Recognise

- **Features usually appear at 1 to 2 years, but diagnosis tends to come later, at 2 to 3 years.** [S1]
- **Short stature, bone deformities and radiological rickets are the commonest first signs** and should prompt biochemical testing and early treatment. Genetic testing is not mandatory to make the diagnosis. [S1]
- **The main differential is nutritional rickets;** severe, chronic or persisting signs despite vitamin D and calcium point to XLH. [S1]
- **Recurrent dental abscesses without caries or trauma are typical.** Hypophosphatasia also causes early tooth loss but has low ALP, whereas XLH in children has persistently high ALP. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Craniosynostosis can be an early infant sign (abnormal head shape), and about 10% of children also have a Chiari malformation.** [S1]
- **Adults:** pseudofractures in 29%-52%, plus enthesopathies and osteoarthritis that become more common after 30-40 years. [S1]
- **Childhood features (S2 Table 1):** lower-limb deformity, below-average height, bone pain, muscle weakness and fatigue, dolichocephaly and craniosynostosis, dental abscesses, and tinnitus or hearing loss. [S2]
- **Adult features (S3 Table 1):** lower-limb deformity, fractures and pseudofractures, muscle weakness and fatigue, bone and joint pain, dental infection and periodontal disease, early joint damage, enthesopathies, spinal stenosis, osteophytes, and tinnitus or hearing loss. [S3]

## Confirm the diagnosis

- **Hypophosphataemia with renal phosphate wasting is the biochemical hallmark,** but low phosphate may not show in the first 3-6 months of life. [S1]
- **Measure TmP/GFR from fasting second-morning paired blood and urine samples,** and exclude generalised tubular wasting (Fanconi syndrome). [S1]
- **Use total ALP in children and bone-specific ALP in adults.** [S1]
- **Concurrent vitamin D deficiency is common in Asian cohorts and can mask XLH;** FGF23 assays are not widely available and need cautious interpretation. [S1]
- **Single-gene PHEX sequencing is the first genetic test** (a pathogenic variant is found in about 90%-100%); if testing is unavailable, a positive family history supports the diagnosis. [S1]
- **Expected biochemistry in a child at diagnosis (S2 Table 2):** serum phosphate low for age because of renal wasting, with a low TmP/GFR; high ALP; normal calcium; PTH normal or a little raised; 25-hydroxyvitamin D normal (low if deficiency coexists); 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D normal or inappropriately low; FGF23 high or inappropriately normal; urine calcium to creatinine normal or low. Use age-specific reference ranges, or the diagnosis can be missed. [S2]

## Treat

- **Children: start oral phosphate with active vitamin D as soon as XLH is diagnosed.** Starting doses: elemental phosphorus 20-60 mg/kg given 4-6 times daily, plus calcitriol 20-40 ng/kg split into two doses a day or alfacalcidol 30-50 ng/kg once daily (doses as stated in the source). [S1]
- **Adults: treat only when symptomatic** (dental problems, musculoskeletal pain, pseudofractures, raised ALP, or planned orthopaedic or dental surgery). Doses: elemental phosphorus 750-2000 mg daily in 2-4 doses, with calcitriol 0.50-0.75 μg or alfacalcidol 0.75-1.5 μg daily. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Conventional therapy harms:** gastrointestinal upset (56%), hyperparathyroidism and nephrocalcinosis, the last linked to longer treatment and higher doses. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Burosumab (anti-FGF23) in children: for radiographically proven rickets refractory to conventional therapy, or intolerance or complications of it.** Start 0.8 mg/kg subcutaneously every 2 weeks; stop conventional therapy 2 weeks beforehand, and serum phosphate should be below the age-adjusted lower limit. [S1]
- **Burosumab in adults: for refractory symptoms or complications of conventional therapy,** starting at 1.0 mg/kg subcutaneously every 4 weeks. Never combine it with conventional therapy. [S1]
- **Burosumab does not cause nephrocalcinosis or hyperparathyroidism;** injection-site reactions are the commonest side effect, mostly mild and short-lived. [S1]
- **Growth hormone for short stature in XLH:** no clear recommendation can be made on current evidence. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Pregnancy and lactation:** intensify monitoring. Burosumab labelling advises against use in pregnancy and in women of childbearing potential not using contraception. [S1]
- ⚠️ **International Working Group, children: burosumab is recommended over conventional therapy from 12 months of age** (strong recommendation, moderate certainty) and suggested from 6 to 12 months (conditional, low certainty). Start treatment promptly once XLH and hypophosphataemia are confirmed. [S2]
- **Burosumab dosing in children (S2):** starting doses vary by regulator (0.4–0.8 mg/kg, fortnightly) and titration is often needed; US FDA-approved paediatric doses reach 2 mg/kg fortnightly, capped at 90 mg. Adjust to serum phosphate (low- to mid-normal at peak, just below or low-normal at trough), not simply to weight gain, and avoid hyperphosphataemia. [S2]
- **If burosumab is unavailable, children should still be treated with conventional therapy** rather than nothing. Never give phosphate alone; do not aim for a normal fasting phosphate; more than 60 mg/kg/day of elemental phosphorus is rarely tolerated; lower the active vitamin D if hypercalciuria persists. (S2) [S2]
- **High PTH on conventional therapy:** reduce phosphate or raise active vitamin D (if no hypercalciuria or hypercalcaemia). Tertiary hyperparathyroidism with hypercalcaemia may need off-label cinacalcet with calcium monitoring, or subtotal parathyroidectomy. (S2) [S2]
- ⚠️ **Do not use burosumab in acute kidney injury or when eGFR is under 30 mL/min;** use it cautiously, with close monitoring, at eGFR 30–60. (S2) [S2]
- **Growth hormone is not advised routinely for short stature in XLH** (S2: limited benefit in randomised trials). S1 found the evidence too weak for any recommendation. [S2]
- ⚠️ **International Working Group, adults: burosumab is recommended over no therapy for fractures or pseudofractures** (strong, moderate certainty) and suggested over conventional therapy in that group (conditional, low certainty); it is also suggested over no therapy for adults without fractures (conditional, low certainty). [S3]
- **Adults (S3): treat symptomatic or osteomalacic disease** (for example pain, weakness, osteomalacic fractures, a high ALP or biopsy-proven osteomalacia), optimise treatment before orthopaedic or dental implant surgery, and keep treating for several years after the growth plates close, since peak bone mass comes at about 20 to 25 years. [S3]
- **Conventional therapy in adults (S3):** phosphate salts 750-2000 mg/day in 3 to 4 doses plus either alfacalcidol 0.75-1.5 μg a day or calcitriol 0.5 to 1 µg a day, lowered for maintenance; active vitamin D alone may do for mild symptoms. If burosumab is unavailable, symptomatic adults should get conventional therapy, and fasting phosphate is not a target. [S3]
- **Hypercalciuria (S3):** cut the active vitamin D dose; if it persists, hydrochlorothiazide or potassium citrate may be used, with sodium below 1500 mg/day and fluids up to 3 L/24 hours. [S3]
- **Pregnancy (S3):** offer genetic counselling to all women and adolescent girls of childbearing age; check corrected or ionised calcium each trimester, creatinine and eGFR monthly on treatment, and fasting phosphate at least once. Stop thiazides and ACE inhibitors or ARBs before conception; cinacalcet is not recommended in pregnancy. [S3]

## Refer and monitor

- **The lead physician should build a team including at least orthopaedics, dentistry, rehabilitation and allied health,** and arrange formal audiology from age 8. [S1]
- **Review children and adolescents about every 3 months and adults every 6-12 months.** Check head circumference, skull shape and headache at each childhood visit (craniosynostosis, Chiari), and monitor blood pressure on conventional therapy. [S1]
- **ALP normalisation is the main biochemical treatment target** (some accept below 1.5 times the upper limit). Serum phosphate guides burosumab dosing; conventional therapy does not normalise phosphate. [S1]
- **Renal ultrasound yearly for nephrocalcinosis; dental review twice a year at every age.** [S1]
- **Transition to adult care:** introduce it to families 2-4 years before transfer, individualised to readiness. [S1]
- **Across transition (S2):** continue medical therapy for at least several years after epiphyseal closure to optimise peak bone mass. If burosumab continues into adulthood, switching to monthly injections in the first 1–2 years after the growth plates fuse is reasonable; the best approach has not been studied. [S2]
- **Dental care in children (S2):** a dentist belongs on the team; visits every 6 months, with sealing of pits and fissures in primary and permanent teeth. [S2]
- **Physiotherapy (S2):** orthoses are generally not indicated (they increase in-toeing) and bulky footwear increases tripping; balance higher-impact or high-torsion sports with lower-impact activities such as swimming or cycling. [S2]
