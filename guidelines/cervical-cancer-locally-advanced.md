# Locally advanced cervical cancer

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, staging, chemoradiation with brachytherapy, pembrolizumab with chemoradiation, induction and adjuvant chemotherapy, and follow-up for adults with locally advanced cervical cancer (FIGO IB2–IVA). Early-stage surgery, fertility-sparing treatment and recurrent or metastatic disease are mentioned only in passing. Screening and the first steps after an HPV result: see `cervical-cancer-screening`. HPV vaccination: see `human-papillomavirus-vaccination`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **HPV is found in around 99% of cervical cancers.** Early disease is often silent, so screening matters; abnormal cytology or a positive high-risk HPV test leads to colposcopy and biopsy. [S1]
- **Locally advanced disease can cause abnormal bleeding or discharge, pelvic pain and dyspareunia,** easily mistaken for vaginitis or cervicitis. Bowel or urinary symptoms or fistula suggest advanced disease. [S1]
- **A visible or palpable lesion is diagnosed by biopsy;** examine under anaesthesia if pelvic examination is inadequate or vaginal or parametrial spread is uncertain. [S1]

## Stage

- **Stage with FIGO 2018, which now includes imaging and pathology.** Use pelvic MRI for local extent and PET/CT for stage IB3 and above (chest and abdominal CT if PET is unavailable). Cystoscopy or proctoscopy only if invasion is suspected. [S1]
- **Para-aortic node dissection may be considered when imaging shows no para-aortic disease,** to find occult metastases that would change the radiotherapy field. [S1]
- **Five-year survival is about 60% for locally advanced disease,** under 50% with pelvic node metastases and under 20–30% with para-aortic node metastases. [S1]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **Locally advanced means FIGO IB2, II, III and IVA. Standard treatment is external beam radiotherapy with weekly cisplatin, then brachytherapy;** it adds about 10% to overall survival and halves relapse risk. Weekly carboplatin AUC2 is the alternative in renal impairment. [S1]
- **Weekly cisplatin dose: 40 mg/m², capped at 70 mg a week.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Pembrolizumab with chemoradiation (KEYNOTE-A18)** for high-risk disease (FIGO 2014 IB2–IIB node-positive, or III–IVA) improved progression-free survival (HR 0.70) and 24-month survival (87% vs 81%). The guideline expects it to become standard. [S1]
- **Induction chemotherapy (INTERLACE: 6 weekly carboplatin AUC 2 plus paclitaxel 80 mg/m² before chemoradiation)** is an option for selected patients. [S1]
- **Do not give adjuvant chemotherapy after chemoradiation, and do not use neoadjuvant chemotherapy before radical surgery** as a standard. [S1]
- **After radical surgery with high-risk findings** (positive nodes, margins or parametrium), give pelvic radiotherapy with concurrent platinum. [S1]

## Recurrence

- **Up to 70% of locally advanced cancers recur, mostly within two years.** Confirm recurrence histologically. Central pelvic recurrence without sidewall, extrapelvic nodal or peritoneal disease: pelvic exenteration in a specialised centre. [S1]
- **Metastatic or recurrent disease:** platinum–paclitaxel plus pembrolizumab for fit patients with PD-L1 CPS 1 or more, with bevacizumab once fistula risk is discussed; cemiplimab after platinum if no prior immunotherapy. [S1]

## Follow-up

- **Image high-risk stage II+ patients 3–6 months after treatment** (PET/CT preferred, plus pelvic MRI). Then review every 3–6 months for two years and every 6–12 months in years 3–5, with specialist vaginal and pelvic-rectal examination; return to annual checks after five recurrence-free years. [S1]
- **Routine cytology after chemoradiation has low predictive value.** Teach patients recurrence symptoms, and start vaginal dilators with lubricant and topical oestrogen early for sexual rehabilitation. [S1]
