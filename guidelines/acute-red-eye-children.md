# Acute red eye in children

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** assessment of the acute red eye in children, conjunctivitis (including neonatal), and recognition and escalation of sight-threatening causes. > ⚠️ **Severe eye pain, photophobia, reduced visual acuity or possible penetrating trauma = sight-threatening until proven otherwise: specialist ophthalmology assessment.**

## Recognise it

- **Conjunctivitis is the most common cause of an acute red eye.** Good analgesia helps the examination; a younger child may need procedural sedation to tell benign from serious conditions. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Sight-threatening red flags: photophobia, severe pain in the eye, decreased visual acuity, or a history that could mean penetrating eye trauma.** All of these need specialist ophthalmology assessment. [S1]
- **Uncommon but serious causes:** scleritis, uveitis, infectious keratitis, endophthalmitis, acute angle closure glaucoma, penetrating eye injury. [S1]
- **Ask about systemic inflammation** (e.g. Kawasaki disease, PIMS-TS); **in a neonate, the birth history and any maternal STI.** [S1]
- **Be cautious diagnosing unilateral conjunctivitis.** [S1]

## Conjunctivitis

- **Bacterial** (purulent discharge, not itchy, clear cornea without infiltrates): **eye toilet and chloramphenicol 0.5% eye drops 4–6 times a day for 5–7 days.** Advise infection control, such as hand washing before and after touching the eyes. [S1]
- **Hypersensitivity to chloramphenicol drops: stop and consult ophthalmology.** [S1]
- **The most common type** presents with watery discharge, burning and bilateral inflammation, often with URTI or gut symptoms. **It self-resolves over 1–3 weeks**: eye toilet, lubricants, cool compresses, infection control; **contagious until the eye stops tearing.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Suspect herpes simplex if there are lid vesicles, reduced vision or photophobia:** swab for HSV PCR; aciclovir likely; seek specialist advice. [S1]
- **Bilateral watery discharge with itch or burning and lid swelling, especially in an atopic child:** consider oral or topical antihistamines and artificial tears. [S1]

## Neonatal conjunctivitis

- **Neonatal pathogens: gonococcus, chlamydia, herpes simplex, staphylococcus, streptococcus, haemophilus.** Think of gonococcus when **purulent discharge is severe and the conjunctiva and lids are oedematous.** [S1]
- **Swab the conjunctiva** for Gram stain, Giemsa stain and culture, plus gonococcal and chlamydia PCR if suspected. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Invasive disease is possible with gonococcus, chlamydia or herpes simplex, so a septic work-up may be needed.** Seek specialist advice. [S1]
- **Chlamydial conjunctivitis: oral azithromycin 20 mg/kg once daily for 3 days**, plus eye toilet. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Gonococcal conjunctivitis: ceftriaxone 25–50 mg/kg stat.** Where possible, do not use ceftriaxone in a neonate under 41 weeks' gestation, especially one who is jaundiced or on calcium-containing solutions. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Herpes simplex infection or keratitis: septic work-up and IV aciclovir likely**; seek specialist advice. [S1]
- **Other organisms: chloramphenicol 0.5% eye drops 4–6 times a day for 5–7 days.** [S1]

## Other specific conditions

- **Subconjunctival haemorrhage** is painless with no discharge. **Observe: an isolated one usually resolves within 1–3 weeks.** If recurrent, look for a cause such as a bleeding disorder. ⚠️ **In infants, it can signal non-accidental injury.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Posterior edge of a subconjunctival haemorrhage not visible → think of a fracture of the orbit or skull base.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Chemical or thermal burn: give local anaesthetic, then irrigate copiously and urgently**; sedation or an urgent GA may be needed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Infectious keratitis** (purulent discharge, severe pain, vision loss; often contact-lens related): **contact ophthalmology urgently** and keep the contact lens for culture if possible. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Endophthalmitis** (severe pain, purulent discharge, vision loss, hypopyon; after eye surgery, penetrating injury or keratitis): **contact ophthalmology urgently.** Likely IV **ceftazidime 50 mg/kg (max 2 g) 8-hourly and vancomycin 15 mg/kg (max 750 mg) 6-hourly.** [S1]
- **Scleritis** (watery discharge, severe pain, vision loss): **contact ophthalmology urgently**; consider systemic inflammatory or autoimmune disease. [S1]
- **Iritis/uveitis:** pain, photophobia, blepharospasm, tearing; the pupil can be small and irregular, reacting poorly. **Contact ophthalmology.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Acute angle closure glaucoma:** severe pain, cloudy vision and cornea, fixed, mildly dilated pupil, sometimes headache and vomiting. **Contact ophthalmology urgently.** [S1]

## Escalation and discharge

- **Involve the paediatric team** if child abuse or STI-related conjunctivitis is suspected, or systemic illness is possible. [S1]
- **Consider transfer** for a sight-threatening condition or management beyond the local team. [S1]
- **Discharge when** sight-threatening conditions are excluded **and** treatment is started or referral made **and** follow-up or clear early-review advice is in place. [S1]
