# Bacterial keratitis: when to culture, antibiotic choice, steroids and follow-up

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** specialist diagnosis and treatment of bacterial keratitis in adults and children: who needs smears and cultures, empiric and fortified antibiotics, adjunctive corticosteroids, follow-up, reculture and contact-lens counselling. Primary-care recognition and same-day referral are in `conjunctivitis-and-eye-infections` (adults) and `acute-red-eye-children`; herpes simplex keratitis is in `herpes-simplex-keratitis`. ## Recognise

- **Suspect it with** pain, redness, blurred vision, light sensitivity and discharge, plus corneal infiltrate or ulcer and inflammation in the anterior chamber. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Contact lenses are the top risk factor in the US, and sleeping in them (orthokeratology included) is a major one;** in much of the rest of the world, trauma leads. [S1]
- **Slit-lamp picture:** a suppurative stromal infiltrate (especially one over 1 mm) with blurred edges, surrounding oedema and white-cell infiltration, usually an epithelial defect, and often an anterior-chamber reaction. [S1]

## Prevent after abrasion

- ⚠️ **Give a topical antibiotic for any corneal abrasion in a contact-lens wearer or after trauma,** ideally within 24 hours, which has been shown to prevent ulceration; it covers fungal as well as bacterial infection. **Do not patch** a lens-related abrasion or fit a bandage lens. [S1]

## Decide whether to culture

- **Most community-acquired cases (small, off-centre ulcers) settle on empiric treatment** without smears or cultures. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Take smears and cultures before starting antibiotics when:** the infiltrate is central and large (within 3 mm of the centre and 2 mm or more across), there are 2 or more adjacent lesions, the stroma is deeply involved or melting, the anterior chamber shows 1+ cells or more, the cornea has had surgery, features suggest fungus, Acanthamoeba or mycobacteria, or there are several infiltrates. [S1]
- **Appearance alone is unreliable:** shown photographs, 15 cornea specialists told bacterial from fungal keratitis correctly in less than 70% of cases. [S1]
- **Scrape under proparacaine 0.5%, not tetracaine,** because tetracaine has antimicrobial activity. [S1]
- **A hypopyon in bacterial keratitis is usually sterile;** do not tap the aqueous or vitreous unless endophthalmitis is strongly suspected (after intraocular surgery, penetrating trauma or sepsis). [S1]

## Treat

- **Antibiotic drops are the treatment of choice,** reaching high tissue levels. Ointments penetrate the cornea poorly, so keep them for bedtime in milder cases or as an add-on. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Central or severe keratitis** (deep stroma, or infiltrate over 2 mm with heavy suppuration): **load with drops every 5 to 15 minutes, then give them about hourly.** [S1]
- **A single fluoroquinolone works as well as a fortified combination.** Consider fortified antibiotics for large or sight-threatening infiltrates, particularly with a hypopyon, and for eyes not responding. [S1]
- **Ciprofloxacin 0.3%, ofloxacin 0.3% and levofloxacin 1.5% are the FDA-approved agents;** fourth-generation fluoroquinolones are widely used off-label. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Fluoroquinolone resistance is rising.** Risks include recent fluoroquinolone use, hospital admission, age and recent eye surgery. They work poorly against MRSA, which is generally sensitive to vancomycin. [S1]
- **A cycloplegic can ease pain and reduce synechiae** from anterior-segment inflammation. [S1]
- **Systemic antibiotics are seldom needed,** except where infection spreads to the sclera or the cornea is perforating; they are mandatory for gonococcal keratitis. [S1]

## Corticosteroids

- **Adjunctive steroid is optional, not routine.** SCUT found no overall benefit from prednisolone phosphate 1% with antibiotics (and no extra harm); subgroups hinted at benefit for Pseudomonas, severe ulcers, and when started within 2 to 3 days of antibiotics. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Never add a steroid for Nocardia, fungus or Acanthamoeba.** Where the central cornea is involved, a steroid may be added after at least 2 to 3 days of steady improvement on antibiotics, usually once the organism is known and fungus excluded. Check IOP and review within 1 to 2 days of starting it. [S1]
- **If the patient is already on steroid drops at presentation, cut back or stop them until the infection is controlled;** warn that inflammation may flare briefly as they come off. [S1]

## Follow up

- **See severe cases daily at first,** until they are stable or clearly improving. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Judge treatment by the clinical response.** Change the regimen if the eye has not improved or stabilised within 48 hours, but expect Pseudomonas and other gram-negative infections to look more inflamed for the first 24 to 48 hours despite correct therapy. [S1]
- **Poor response, especially with negative cultures, calls for reculture or biopsy;** stopping antibiotics for 12 to 24 hours beforehand may raise the yield. Drug toxicity or steroid withdrawal can mimic treatment failure. [S1]
- **Admit when the keratitis is severe or threatens sight, adherence is unlikely, or pain is severe,** and for patients who cannot instil drops at home. [S1]
- **Counsel lens wearers:** microbial keratitis affects up to 20 per 10,000 extended-wear users a year against 1 to 2 per 10,000 daily-wear users. Reassess lens type and wear before resuming, and consider daily disposables. [S1]
