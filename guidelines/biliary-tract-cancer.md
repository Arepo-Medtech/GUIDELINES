## Recognise

- **Biliary tract cancer is rare (about 1% of cancers) and usually presents at 65–84 years.** Jaundice from biliary obstruction is the commonest symptom (less so in gallbladder cancer); weight loss, poor appetite or abdominal pain suggest advanced disease. [S1]
- **Risk factors** include chronic biliary inflammation, primary sclerosing cholangitis, liver flukes and hepatolithiasis (Asia), cirrhosis, hepatitis B and C, fatty liver disease, smoking and alcohol; gallstones and porcelain gallbladder predispose to gallbladder cancer. [S1]

## Diagnose and stage

- **CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis for nodal and distant spread; MRI for local extent of perihilar and distal tumours and for liver metastases.** FDG-PET is not for primary diagnosis. Stage by UICC 8th edition, per subtype. [S1]
- **Check liver function and look for underlying liver disease** (HBV, HCV, fatty liver risk, sclerosing cholangitis, PBC). CA 19.9 is non-specific but useful for prognosis and response. ERCP or PTC can relieve obstruction. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Tissue (core biopsy where possible) is mandatory before systemic therapy,** for histology and NGS. A biopsy is not always needed before curative resection. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do comprehensive NGS early, especially in advanced disease,** for IDH1/2, FGFR2 fusions, KRAS, BRAF V600E and NTRK, plus HER2 immunohistochemistry and MSI. Around 45% have a potentially targetable alteration; FGFR2 fusions and IDH1 mutations cluster in intrahepatic tumours. [S1]
- **Profile before or during first-line therapy** for locally advanced or metastatic disease (ESMO I, A). The panel should cover hotspots in FGFR2, IDH1, HER2, BRAF, RET, NTRK, PALB2 and BRCA1/2, and may add genes such as c-MET. [S2]
- **HER2 alterations occur in about 5%-10% of biliary cancers, more often extrahepatic and gallbladder tumours.** Test protein by IHC and amplification by FISH (or by NGS); with no BTC-specific scoring standard, score IHC with the gastric cancer criteria. [S2]

## Resectable and locally advanced disease

- **Resection with clear margins is the only cure, but only 20–30% are candidates;** a specialised multidisciplinary team should decide on surgery and all treatment. [S1]
- ⚠️ **After resection, offer eight cycles of adjuvant capecitabine** (BILCAP). Radiotherapy afterwards is an exception for R1-resected gallbladder or extrahepatic tumours. Neoadjuvant treatment remains experimental. [S1]
- **Locally advanced disease: systemic therapy first,** with locoregional options (ablation, radiotherapy, intra-arterial therapy) for selected patients; reassess for surgery after a response. Node-negative unresectable hilar tumours may suit liver transplantation in a specialised centre. [S1]
- **Before resection (ESMO algorithm):** consider whether preoperative biliary drainage is needed, avoid percutaneous biopsy of a resectable distal or perihilar cholangiocarcinoma, assess the future liver remnant, and complete the surgery for an incidental gallbladder cancer of stage T1b or above. Reconsider surgery or local therapy when initially inoperable disease responds. [S2]

## Advanced disease: first line

- ⚠️ **Standard first line for unresectable or metastatic disease is cisplatin–gemcitabine plus a checkpoint inhibitor** (durvalumab or pembrolizumab), regardless of PD-L1 or targetable alterations. If immunotherapy is contraindicated, cisplatin–gemcitabine alone; if cisplatin is contraindicated, gemcitabine alone or with oxaliplatin. [S1]
- **Durvalumab added to cisplatin–gemcitabine raised 36-month survival from 6.9% to 14.6%,** and was the only one of the two immunotherapy trials to also improve progression-free survival and response. [S1]
- **ESMO also gives both first-line chemo-immunotherapy regimens equal standing (I, A).** In KEYNOTE-966 (1069 patients), adding pembrolizumab to cisplatin–gemcitabine lengthened median survival from 10.9 to 12.7 months (HR 0.83), and ESMO sees no biomarker, efficacy or toxicity reason to prefer one checkpoint inhibitor. [S2]
- **Triplet chemotherapy is not standard:** mFOLFIRINOX and cisplatin–gemcitabine–nab-paclitaxel did not beat cisplatin–gemcitabine and every triplet added toxicity; only a Japanese trial of cisplatin–gemcitabine–S1 showed gains. Gemcitabine alone suits patients too frail for platinum. [S2]

## Advanced disease: later lines

- **Second line without a target: FOLFOX** (about 45% are fit for it). Single fluoropyrimidine or irinotecan suits those who cannot take a doublet; FOLFIRI only in highly selected cases. Liposomal irinotecan is not recommended outside trials. [S1]
- ⚠️ **IDH1-mutant cholangiocarcinoma after at least 1 prior line: ivosidenib** (ClarIDHy: median PFS 2.7 vs 1.4 months). IDH1 mutations occur in 10–20% of intrahepatic tumours. [S1]
- ⚠️ **FGFR2 fusion after at least 1 prior line: an FGFR inhibitor (futibatinib or pemigatinib).** Monitor for hyperphosphataemia, nail changes and retinal toxicity. [S1]
- **Other targets after progression:** dabrafenib–trametinib for BRAF V600E; pembrolizumab for MSI-high/dMMR or high TMB; NTRK inhibitors; HER2-directed drugs for HER2-positive disease; selpercatinib for RET fusions. Re-check the NGS panel over time. [S1]
- **FGFR inhibitor results (phase II):** futibatinib response 42%, median PFS 9.0 and OS 21.7 months; pemigatinib response 37.0%, PFS 7.0 and OS 17.5 months. ESMO recommends both after one or more earlier lines (III, A). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Previously treated HER2-positive disease: zanidatamab is recommended and trastuzumab deruxtecan should be considered** (both III, A; FDA- but not EMA-approved at the time). Phase II response rates were 41.3% for zanidatamab and 56.3% for trastuzumab deruxtecan in IHC 3+ tumours; responses look lower when HER2 is mutated rather than amplified. [S2]
- **NTRK fusions (under 0.1% of cases): entrectinib, larotrectinib or repotrectinib; RET fusions: consider selpercatinib,** each after progression on or intolerance of earlier treatment (III, A). [S2]
- **MSI-high or dMMR tumours: anti-PD-1 therapy if immunotherapy was not already given first line.** [S2]

## Follow-up

- **After curative treatment, review every three to six months for two years** (examination, bloods, CEA and CA 19–9, CT chest, abdomen and pelvis), then yearly to five years; on systemic therapy, CT every three months. [S1]
- **After surgery, watch for malabsorption and diarrhoea from pancreatic insufficiency,** which may need chronic treatment. Biliary obstruction needs specialist multidisciplinary decisions on drainage. [S1]
