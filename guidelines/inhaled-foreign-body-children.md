# Inhaled foreign body in children

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, imaging, emergency and subacute management, referral and prevention for inhaled (airway) foreign bodies in children. Related: `acute-upper-airway-obstruction-children` (differential of stridor), `foreign-body-ingestion-children` (swallowed objects, including button batteries).

> ⚠️ **An inhaled foreign body is not ruled out by a normal chest X-ray or a normal examination.** Ask specifically about choking — it may have been days or weeks earlier.

---

## Recognise it

- ⚠️ **FB in the larynx or trachea is potentially life-threatening and needs immediate emergency airway management.** [S1]
- **Often unwitnessed — keep a high index of suspicion** in young children with respiratory symptoms, and **ask specifically about choking**, which may have been days or weeks before. [S1]
- **Most events are in children under 4 years**, commonly nuts, seeds, popcorn, raw apple or carrot, coins, toy pieces and balloons. Older children with developmental impairment remain at risk. [S1]
- **Most inhaled FB lodge in the bronchi**, not the larynx or trachea. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Larynx/trachea:** a sudden, catastrophic event — choking, coughing ± vomiting, stridor, severe respiratory distress, cyanosis, drooling and voice change, altered mental state. **Total obstruction rapidly progresses to unconsciousness and arrest.** [S1]
- **Think of an FB when a child in cardiorespiratory arrest cannot be ventilated.** [S1]
- **Main bronchus:** witnessed choking, coughing or wheeze while eating or playing (many unwitnessed), tachypnoea and distress, cyanosis, **persistent wheeze (possibly focal; bronchodilators may partly help)**, persistent cough, fever, haemoptysis, recurrent or persistent consolidation. May be asymptomatic until complications (pneumonia, abscess, bronchiectasis). [S1]
- **Examination may be normal.** Look for tracheal deviation, asymmetrical chest movement, reduced breath sounds or a focal wheeze; **observe and maintain the child's preferred position** (e.g. sniffing or tripod). [S1]
- **Check ears, nose and throat for other FB**, only if the child is stable. [S1]

## Investigate

- ⚠️ **Resuscitate first if needed. Observe every child closely until the FB is removed** — any movement of the FB can cause sudden airway compromise. [S1]
- **A normal CXR does not exclude it** — there may be no air trapping and most objects are radiolucent. Consider CXR ± inspiratory/expiratory films (if the child can cooperate) or lateral decubitus views. [S1]
- **Look for:** radio-opaque FB, segmental or lobar collapse, distal hyperinflation from air trapping, mediastinal shift, consolidation, pneumothorax. **Abscess and bronchiectasis signal a chronic impacted FB.** [S1]
- **CT is not recommended** — bronchoscopy is likely to be needed; seek expert advice. [S1]

## Treat

- **Removal attempts and advanced airway management** (laryngoscopy, Magill forceps, cricothyroidotomy) should ideally be done by a clinician familiar with them — but **a less experienced clinician may reasonably proceed if the child is deteriorating.** [S1]
- **If attempting these is not safe, continue basic resuscitation while awaiting senior help.** [S1]
- **Stable airway with ongoing suspicion: refer to ENT or respiratory for consideration of bronchoscopy.** Keep a low threshold for consultation. [S1]

## Refer, transfer, discharge

- **Consult ENT or respiratory for any suspicion of an inhaled FB.** Transfer if local services can't perform the procedures or investigations. [S1]
- **Discharge when the FB is removed and/or the child is stable after a period of observation.** [S1]

## Prevention advice for parents

- **Children under 5 are at greatest risk, especially from food.** Don't offer hard round foods — peanuts or other nuts, uncut grapes, popcorn, hard lollies, raw apples or carrot. [S1]
- **Sit calmly while eating, offering one piece of food at a time; under age 3, no toys with small parts.** [S1]
