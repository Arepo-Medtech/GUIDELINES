# Zollinger–Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma) and gastric acid hypersecretion

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with suspected or confirmed Zollinger–Ellison syndrome (ZES), sporadic or with MEN1: diagnosis of gastrin excess, long-term acid control with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), PPI adverse effects and referral. Tumour localisation, surgery and anti-tumour therapy are summarised only; see `neuroendocrine-neoplasms`. Ordinary peptic ulcer and reflux: see `peptic-ulcer-disease` and `gord-and-dyspepsia`.

## Recognise

- ZES is gastric acid hypersecretion from ectopic gastrin secretion by a neuroendocrine tumour (gastrinoma), causing severe peptic ulcer disease or reflux that is often refractory to standard treatment. [S1]
- Gastrinomas are not curable by surgery in >50% of cases, mainly because of advanced or multifocal (MEN1) disease, so most patients need lifelong acid antisecretory drugs. [S1]
- MEN1 is present in 20–25% of all ZES patients; MEN1/ZES patients usually have multiple, often small duodenal gastrinomas and a very low surgical cure rate. [S1]
- ⚠️ Hyperparathyroidism is present in >95% of MEN1/ZES patients and can increase gastrin release and acid output and make acid harder to suppress medically. [S1]

## Diagnose

- The first test is usually a fasting serum gastrin (FSG); it is raised in >99% of ZES but has low specificity, because atrophic gastritis/pernicious anaemia, PPI use and H. pylori also raise gastrin. [S1]
- ⚠️ PPIs cause hypergastrinaemia in 80–100% of users, starting within 5 days, and control symptoms of most ZES patients at ordinary doses, so PPI use both mimics and masks ZES. [S1]
- ENETS guidance (as summarised): diagnose gastrinoma with FSG > 10× ULN plus gastric fluid pH ≤ 2 off PPIs; if FSG is 1–10× raised, do upper endoscopy with endoscopic ultrasound, CT or MRI, and somatostatin receptor imaging. [S1]
- A secretin test increase in serum gastrin of ≥ 120 pg/mL had 94% sensitivity and 100% specificity for ZES, but secretin is not readily available in many centres. [S1]
- ⚠️ Stopping PPIs abruptly for testing is risky: severe peptic and reflux complications have been reported in ZES patients. [S1]
- A raised gastrin with a positive somatostatin receptor scan is not specific for ZES, especially in MEN1 patients taking PPIs; serum chromogranin A has low specificity too. [S1]
- Refer suspected patients to a centre with ZES expertise so the diagnosis is firmly established by the classical criteria. [S1]

## Control acid secretion

- PPIs are the antisecretory drugs of choice, largely replacing H2-receptor antagonists because of greater potency and longer action. [S1]
- ⚠️ Uncontrolled acid hypersecretion carries very high morbidity and mortality from acid-peptic complications, so control it rapidly. [S1]
- In the NIH series of 303 ZES patients treated for a mean of 13.7 yrs, medical control of hypersecretion succeeded in all patients, with each dose titrated individually; the PPI daily dose could be reduced over time in 47%. [S1]
- Because acid secretory testing is rarely available, the authors recommend care in a specialty centre that can assess acid control, including symptom review and regular upper GI endoscopy on treatment. [S1]

## Monitor long-term PPI therapy

- Long-term PPI treatment in ZES has been linked to hypomagnesaemia and low vitamin B12 stores; one patient's refractory hypomagnesaemia required total gastrectomy. [S1]
- Hypomagnesaemia appears to be a PPI class effect: it resolves when PPIs stop, may recur with another PPI, but not with H2-receptor antagonists. In NIH ZES patients the rate was 0.4%. [S1]
- ⚠️ Vitamin B12 deficiency developed in 21% of 175 NIH ZES patients over a mean 10.2 years, linked to PPI acid suppression; no patient on H2-receptor antagonists alone developed it. [S1]
- Oral multivitamins containing crystalline vitamin B12 raised serum B12 by a mean of 34% without changing the PPI dose, suggesting deficiency can be easily avoided. [S1]
