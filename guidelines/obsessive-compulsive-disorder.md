# Obsessive-compulsive disorder

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising and treating obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) in adults, children and young people: stepped psychological treatment with exposure and response prevention (ERP), SSRI and clomipramine use, poor response and specialist referral, and follow-up after remission. Body dysmorphic disorder, which the source also covers, is outside this guideline. Anxiety in children: see `anxiety-children`; comorbid depression: see `major-depressive-disorder`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ✅ **COMPILED FROM 2 SOURCES.** 32 claims; **51 fragments or anchors re-checkable by machine; 3 doses.** **S1** National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (published 29 November 2005, last reviewed 11 July 2024 (update in progress); international): 22 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored · **S2** Brakoulias V (published 1 August 2015; AU): 10 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored

> ⚠️ International guideline (NICE CG31, 2005). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **Old evidence, update pending.** The recommendations date from 2005. NICE's 2024 review only simplified the text, and an update has been in progress since a 2019 surveillance decision. No current Australian OCD guideline was found, and the WFSBP 2022 guideline is paywalled.

> ℹ️ **Drug choice largely matches the PBS.** The PBS lists fluvoxamine, fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine and clomipramine for OCD. NICE also names citalopram (off-label in the UK), which has no PBS OCD listing.

> 🇦🇺 **S2 is Australian but is a review, not a guideline** (Australian Prescriber, 2015). It adds the trial dose ranges, the pace of SSRI titration, drug-safety points and Australian service notes. Where it differs from NICE (S1), both are shown.

## Recognise

- **Shame makes OCD hard to disclose.** Explain that the symptoms are involuntary, and tell people troubled by obsessional thoughts that nearly everyone has such thoughts now and then; it is their frequency and distress that mark OCD. [S1]
- **Ask directly in higher-risk groups** (depression, anxiety, alcohol or drug misuse, BDD, eating disorders, dermatology patients): about excessive washing or checking, unwanted thoughts that will not go, daily tasks that take too long, a need for order or distress at mess, and whether these cause trouble. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Assess self-harm and suicide risk, particularly with comorbid depression.** Intrusive sexual, aggressive or death-related thoughts are common in OCD and often wrongly read as dangerous; seek OCD expertise if unsure. [S1]
- **Where family or carers have been drawn into compulsions, avoidance or reassurance-giving,** the plan should help them step back, gently and supportively. [S1]
- **About 2% of people have OCD, and 6–8% have subclinical symptoms.** Many see their GP only after years of silent suffering, embarrassed or unaware that treatment works. [S2]
- **Do not confuse OCD with obsessive compulsive (anankastic) personality disorder,** where rigidity, perfectionism and a love of order feel natural to the person rather than distressing; the two often occur apart. [S2]

## Initial treatment in adults

- **Mild impairment, or a preference for it: low-intensity CBT with ERP** (up to 10 therapist hours), by structured self-help materials, by telephone, or in a group. [S1]
- **Moderate impairment, or mild impairment where low-intensity work failed or was not possible:** let the person choose between an SSRI and more intensive CBT with ERP (over 10 therapist hours); the two work about equally well. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Severe impairment: combine an SSRI with CBT including ERP.** [S1]
- **Internet-delivered CBT programs work, whether therapist-guided or self-guided,** and suit people in rural and remote Australia (examples named: STOP Therapy for OCD, This Way Up OCD Course). [S2]
- **Reach for medication when** the person cannot face the anxiety that ERP provokes, symptoms are severe, or no psychologist trained in OCD is available (waiting lists and cost are common barriers). [S2]

## SSRIs in adults

- **First drug: an SSRI,** namely fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine, sertraline or citalopram. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Dose ranges used in OCD trials (per day):** citalopram 20–80 mg (but recommended only up to 40 mg), escitalopram 10–40 mg, fluoxetine 40–80 mg, fluvoxamine 50–300 mg, paroxetine 20–60 mg, sertraline 50–200 mg, clomipramine 75–300 mg. The SSRIs respond at similar rates, so choose by familiarity and interaction risk. [S2]
- **Most people need more than a standard dose.** Raise the SSRI every two weeks as response and tolerance allow; give it at least 12 weeks at its maximum dose before calling it a failure. Higher doses call for careful monitoring with a psychiatrist. [S2]
- **Counsel before starting:** no craving or tolerance; withdrawal symptoms with missed, reduced or stopped doses; side effects that can include more anxiety, suicidal thoughts and self-harm, especially early; a lag of up to 12 weeks before benefit; and medication is not a weakness. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Watch younger adults closely.** Anyone under 30, with comorbid depression or at raised suicide risk needs frequent early monitoring; prescribe small quantities where suicide risk is high, and look actively for akathisia, agitation and new suicidal thinking. [S1]
- **If a standard dose is not working after 4 to 6 weeks and side effects allow, raise the dose gradually** per the product information, after first checking adherence and alcohol or drug use. [S1]
- **If the SSRI works, keep it going for 12 months or longer** to prevent relapse, then review; when stopping, taper over several weeks. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Stopping a successful SSRI carries a high relapse risk** (over 50% within 12 weeks in most studies). Weigh the person's circumstances, then withdraw slowly over several months, with a psychiatrist's guidance if the illness was severe. [S2]
- **Avoid in OCD without comorbidity:** tricyclics other than clomipramine, tricyclic-related drugs, SNRIs (including venlafaxine), MAOIs, and anxiolytics except briefly to cover early SSRI activation. Do not use an antipsychotic on its own. [S1]

## Poor response in adults

- **Not enough benefit from 12 weeks of an SSRI on its own, or from CBT on its own (over 10 therapist hours):** hold a multidisciplinary review, then offer the two combined. [S1]
- **Still not responding after 12 weeks of combined treatment:** offer a different SSRI or clomipramine. Clomipramine is for after at least 1 SSRI has failed or not been tolerated, or when the person prefers it or did well on it before. [S1]
- **S2's order: clomipramine usually follows two failed SSRI trials,** because it is harder to tolerate and trials have not clearly shown it is better. Benzodiazepines generally do not help OCD, unlike other anxiety disorders. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Clomipramine safety:** it is toxic in overdose, so supply small amounts when suicide risk is significant; check an ECG and blood pressure first if cardiovascular risk is significant. [S1]
- ⚠️ **High-dose SSRIs and clomipramine can prolong the QT interval,** so repeat the ECG after dose increases. Watch for serotonin syndrome and tell patients to avoid St John's wort and tramadol. Fluoxetine and paroxetine strongly inhibit CYP2D6, and clomipramine inhibits several CYP450 enzymes. [S2]
- **Refractory OCD** (an SSRI, combined treatment and clomipramine have all failed): refer to a specialist OCD team. Options, in no proven order, are further CBT or cognitive therapy, antipsychotic augmentation of an SSRI or clomipramine, or clomipramine with citalopram. Primary care should not routinely start combinations or augmentation. [S1]
- **Neurosurgery is not recommended for OCD.** [S1]
- **Australian position (S2):** ERP plus an SSRI beats either alone, so add ERP when drugs alone fail and refer treatment-resistant patients to a psychiatrist. Deep brain stimulation is a last resort in Australia, and NSW mental health law (2007) bans it. [S2]

## Children and young people

- **Psychological treatment first.** Mild impairment: guided self-help with family support may be tried. Moderate to severe impairment, or when self-help has failed: CBT with ERP, involving the family and adapted to the child's developmental age. [S1]
- **Adding an SSRI to CBT after multidisciplinary review** (moderate to severe impairment, inadequate CBT response): consider it at ages 8 to 11, offer it at 12 to 18. A child and adolescent psychiatrist should assess first. Use sertraline or fluvoxamine, or fluoxetine if there is significant comorbid depression. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Prescribing for children:** start low (a half or quarter of the usual starting dose may be used for the first week); warn families about suicidal behaviour, self-harm or hostility; continue a successful SSRI for 6 months or more after remission; and do an ECG before clomipramine. [S1]

## After remission

- **Follow up for a year after remission**, then discharge to primary care if recovery holds. Relapse is common, so see re-referred people promptly rather than putting them on a routine waiting list. [S1]
