# Preventing preterm birth with progesterone (short cervix or previous preterm birth)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** vaginal progesterone to lower the risk of spontaneous preterm birth in asymptomatic women with a short cervix at the mid-trimester scan and in women with a previous spontaneous preterm singleton birth; evidence in multiple pregnancy and other risk groups; route, dose and timing. Acute preterm labour (tocolysis, antenatal corticosteroids, magnesium sulfate): see `preterm-labour`. Cervical cerclage and pessary are not covered. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> 🌐 **S2 is international (FIGO, 2021).** It adds the 2021 EPPPIC individual-participant meta-analysis that S1 was awaiting. Australian practice follows the PBS listing below and may differ; the weekly intramuscular 17-OHPC that FIGO offers as an alternative is not available in Australia (S1).

## Why it matters

- **Preterm birth is the main cause of newborn deaths, so preventing it is a priority;** about two thirds of preterm births are spontaneous and the rest are medically indicated. [S1]
- **A short cervix measured by transvaginal ultrasound in mid-pregnancy strongly predicts spontaneous preterm birth.** [S1]

## Short cervix (singleton)

- ⚠️ **Recommend vaginal progesterone to asymptomatic women whose cervix measures under 25 mm on mid-trimester transvaginal ultrasound.** (Consensus-based recommendation) [S1]
- **In a cervix of 25 mm or less, vaginal progesterone lowered the rate of birth before 34 weeks to 18.1%, against 27.5% without it (RR 0.66)** in an updated meta-analysis. [S1]
- **It also significantly lowered respiratory distress, composite neonatal illness or death, birth weight under 1500 g and NICU admission,** although the fall in perinatal deaths was not significant. [S1]

## Previous spontaneous preterm birth

- **Consider progesterone for a woman with a singleton pregnancy who has previously had a spontaneous preterm singleton birth.** (Consensus-based recommendation) [S1]
- **The evidence here is mixed:** a meta-analysis of five trials favoured progesterone, but the OPPTIMUM trial (2016), which that analysis left out, found no reduction in preterm birth. [S1]
- **Cervical-length surveillance may pick out the women with a previous preterm birth who gain most,** and because most of these trials used intramuscular progesterone, the place of vaginal progesterone in this group is less certain. [S1]

## Other groups

- **Twins and other multiple pregnancies: routine progesterone from 24 weeks has not lowered preterm birth,** and it has not been shown to help significantly when the cervix is short, though some trials were small. [S1]
- **An updated individual-patient meta-analysis in twins with a cervix of 25 mm or less found fewer births before 33 weeks with vaginal progesterone (38.5% vs 55.8%),** but RANZCOG still calls for more research to find which multiple pregnancies benefit. [S1]
- **Uterine malformation or 'cervical incompetence' alone: the studies are too varied and small to say whether progesterone works.** Evidence is also limited for progesterone as ongoing tocolysis after threatened preterm labour before 34 weeks. [S1]

## International recommendations (FIGO 2021)

- ⚠️ **FIGO: offer progesterone to every woman with a singleton pregnancy at high risk,** meaning a previous spontaneous preterm birth, a short cervix on ultrasound, or both, as daily vaginal progesterone or weekly 17-OHPC. Whether it helps after a previous preterm birth when the mid-trimester cervix is longer than 30 mm is not known. [S2]
- **The evidence behind it is the EPPPIC individual-participant meta-analysis (31 trials, 11 644 women):** both vaginal progesterone and 17-OHPC reduced birth before 34 weeks in high-risk singletons. Among women who qualified only through a short cervix or a previous preterm birth, the relative risk was 0.78 with vaginal progesterone (9 trials) and 0.83 with 17-OHPC (5 trials). [S2]
- **No risk factor, no progesterone:** FIGO does not recommend it for women without a previous preterm birth, a mid-trimester loss or a short cervix, because so few of them were in the trials that any benefit is unknown. [S2]
- **Unselected twins or higher-order multiples: not recommended.** In EPPPIC (16 trials) neither vaginal progesterone (RR 1.01) nor 17-OHPC (RR 1.04) lowered birth before 34 weeks; for a multiple pregnancy with another risk factor, such as an earlier preterm birth, the effect is unknown. [S2]
- **Either progestogen can be used, and the woman chooses with her clinician:** the only two direct comparisons found no difference between vaginal progesterone and 17-OHPC for birth before 34 weeks (RR 1.18). [S2]
- **No neurological or developmental benefit or harm has been shown in children exposed before birth:** a systematic review of 5 trials (4222 measurements in children aged 6 months to 8 years) found neither. [S2]

## Route, dose and timing

- **The weekly intramuscular 17-alpha-hydroxyprogesterone caproate used in the US trials is not available in Australia.** [S1]
- **Vaginal pessaries give high uterine levels with few systemic effects,** though vaginal irritation can be a problem. Trials used 90 mg to 400 mg and the best dose is not settled; for a short cervix, 90-100 mg and 200 mg pessaries worked equally well. [S1]
- **Trials began treatment as early as 16 weeks (after a previous spontaneous preterm birth) and some continued it to 37 weeks;** stopping 17-alpha-hydroxyprogesterone caproate early was linked to more recurrent preterm birth. [S1]
- **Starting progesterone at 16-24 weeks appears safe for mother and baby,** with no teratogenic effects seen, and children followed to four years in the NICHD trial showed no harm. [S1]
- **Long-term benefit, and the best timing, dose and route, are still unknown,** so enrolment in relevant clinical trials is encouraged. [S1]
