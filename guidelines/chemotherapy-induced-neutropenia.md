# Chemotherapy-induced neutropenia — G-CSF prophylaxis in adults

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** defining febrile neutropenia, assessing its risk, and choosing, timing and managing short- and long-acting G-CSF (filgrastim, pegfilgrastim, biosimilars) for primary and secondary prophylaxis in adults on myelosuppressive chemotherapy. Not covered: treating febrile neutropenia once it occurs (see `anticancer-drugs-general-principles`; children: `febrile-neutropenia-children`), and chronic or congenital neutropenia (`chronic-and-congenital-neutropenia`). PBS access (filgrastim, pegfilgrastim, lipegfilgrastim) is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- ⚠️ **Febrile neutropenia is an oncological emergency:** fever above 38.3 °C (or above 38.0 °C twice over 2 h), or signs of sepsis, with a neutrophil count below 0.5, or below 1.0 and expected to fall. A lower reading of 38 °C lasting 1 h can also count. [S1]
- **FN incidence ranges from 2 to 50%,** set by patient factors (age, comorbidity, organ dysfunction, performance status, marrow reserve), cancer type, regimen and genetics. Serious complications reach 25–30% and mortality 9–12% in high-risk groups. [S1]
- **Neutropenia severity follows chemotherapy intensity,** and the nadir usually falls between days 7 and 14 after the first dose. [S1]

## Assess the risk

- **Assess FN risk at the start of every chemotherapy cycle,** and fit the regimen to the patient's characteristics and treatment goals. [S1]
- **Patient factors S2 lists as raising FN risk** (taken from the German G-CSF guideline group; the risk is greatest when several combine): being older than 65; poor performance status on the Karnofsky or ECOG scale; comorbid COPD, NYHA III–IV heart failure, HIV, autoimmune disease or marked kidney impairment; advanced symptomatic cancer; previous chemotherapy; and abnormal blood tests such as anaemia, lymphocytes under 700 cells/μl or high bilirubin (plus an albumin abnormality the table prints as hyperalbuminuria). [S2]
- **Adding immunotherapy to chemotherapy does not raise FN risk,** so use the same criteria as for chemotherapy alone. [S1]
- **Check nutritional status before chemotherapy:** poor nutrition raises marrow toxicity. Neutropenic diets do not reduce FN. [S1]

## When to give G-CSF

- ⚠️ **Primary prophylaxis when FN risk is 20% or more;** consider it at 10–20% depending on the patient. [S1]
- **Factors that push intermediate (10–20%) risk towards prophylaxis:** age over 65, advanced disease, poor performance status, liver or kidney dysfunction, recent extensive surgery, persistent neutropenia, prior FN, poor nutrition, bone marrow involvement, multimorbidity or frailty, or when an episode would be especially dangerous. [S1]
- **Not recommended as primary prophylaxis below 10% risk.** [S1]
- **Long-acting G-CSF suits primary and secondary prophylaxis** when the regimen can drop neutrophils to 500 cells/μL or below and keeping dose intensity matters. [S1]
- **Using G-CSF instead of cutting chemotherapy dose is an accepted alternative;** primary prophylaxis reduced all-cause mortality in a meta-analysis, especially with dose-dense chemotherapy. [S1]
- **When treatment is curative, protecting planned dose intensity with G-CSF is the standard of care** (S2 consensus, evidence level I). [S2]
- ⚠️ **S2 goes further on low risk:** whatever the regimen's FN risk (10–20% or even below 10%), give G-CSF when patient, disease or treatment factors bring the overall risk of neutropenic complications to 20% or more, and do not refuse G-CSF on regimen risk alone. Decide it together with the patient. [S2]
- **G-CSF is recommended for secondary as well as primary prophylaxis, and once started it continues through every remaining cycle:** stopping pegfilgrastim raises FN risk in the next cycle. [S2]

## Which G-CSF and how

- **Biosimilar G-CSFs are appropriate for routine use;** their activity and toxicity match the originators. [S1]
- **Filgrastim 5 μg/kg daily until post-nadir recovery (up to 14 days), or pegfilgrastim 6 mg once per cycle.** Long-acting forms help patients with poor adherence and reduce severe neutropenia and FN. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give long-acting G-CSF 24 to 72 h after chemotherapy,** not on the same day: same-day pegfilgrastim had higher FN rates in more than 45,000 adults. [S1]
- **Too-short courses of short-acting G-CSF raise FN risk** and worsen neutropenic outcomes. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Daily filgrastim matches pegfilgrastim only over about 11 days; in practice courses are often under 7 days.** If filgrastim will not be given for 11 days, use pegfilgrastim rather than a shortened filgrastim course; if filgrastim is used, continue it until neutrophils recover (up to 14 days). Pegfilgrastim may be preferred for convenience, particularly in frail or elderly patients. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Do not use pegfilgrastim with weekly chemotherapy** (use short-acting G-CSF until there is evidence); it works with fortnightly regimens such as R-CHOP-14. With split-dose regimens, give it at least 24 h after the last dose and at least 14 days before the next. [S2]
- **Palliative chemotherapy:** if G-CSF is needed, pegfilgrastim suits for convenience, but first consider switching regimen, lowering the dose or lengthening the gap between cycles. [S2]
- **Renal impairment: no pegfilgrastim dose change is needed** (panel opinion, no formal consensus), because it is cleared by neutrophils, not the kidneys. [S2]
- **Targeted drugs and immunotherapy** (for example lenalidomide, bortezomib, obinutuzumab, ibrutinib) can cause neutropenia; with little specific evidence, apply the chemotherapy rules. [S2]

## Haematological cancers

- **Myeloma: filgrastim often fits better,** because weekly and fortnightly regimens are common and it can run alongside immunomodulatory drugs and proteasome inhibitors; pegfilgrastim can be used in some cases. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Acute myeloid leukaemia: filgrastim may be considered during induction, where pegfilgrastim cannot be recommended; pegfilgrastim may be preferred for curative consolidation.** Pegfilgrastim is contraindicated in chronic myeloid leukaemia. [S2]
- **Lymphoma, CLL or myeloma on targeted treatment:** consider pegfilgrastim or short-acting G-CSF if needed to keep treatment going, particularly once neutropenia reaches grade 3 or above. [S2]

## Adverse effects

- **Bone pain affects 25 to 38%.** Treat first with paracetamol or an NSAID (e.g. naproxen); antihistamines (e.g. loratadine), opioids or G-CSF dose reduction are second line. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Rare large-vessel vasculitis (about 0.5%)** can occur, sometimes with aortic dissection or aneurysm. [S1]
- **FN carries real mortality:** 6.8 to 20% among patients admitted with FN complications, higher with comorbidity, sepsis or shock. [S1]
- **For bone pain or other G-CSF side effects, S2 suggests switching between filgrastim and pegfilgrastim; it does not recommend lowering the pegfilgrastim dose,** which is off-label and of disputed effect. [S2]
