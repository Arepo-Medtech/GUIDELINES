# Hypereosinophilia, hypereosinophilic syndromes and PDGFR-rearranged eosinophilic neoplasms

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation

## Recognise

- **Definitions:** eosinophilia is 0.5-1.5 per nL; hypereosinophilia (HE) is above 1.5 per nL and/or tissue eosinophilia. HES is diagnosed as soon as HE is judged to cause organ dysfunction, whatever the cause. [S1]
- **The eosinophil count does not track severity**; organ damage depends on tissue infiltration and eosinophil activation. With urgent, life-threatening disease, a 1-month wait before diagnosing HES is no longer required. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Emergencies:** eosinophilic myocarditis, intracardiac thrombus with embolism, severe asthma or hypoxic lung disease, strokes and arterial or venous thrombosis. A very high count alone (for example above 10 per nL) without clinical involvement is not an emergency. [S1]
- **S2 definitions:** eosinophilia is a count above 0.5 per nL; hypereosinophilia needs a count above 1.5 per nL on 2 tests more than 1 month apart, and/or marrow eosinophils of 20% or more, and/or proven extensive tissue infiltration or marked eosinophil granule-protein deposition. [S2]
- **S2 criteria for idiopathic HES:** exclude reactive eosinophilia, lymphocytic-variant disease, CEL-NOS, WHO-defined myeloid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine-kinase fusion neoplasms; the count must stay above 1.5 per nL for at least six months with tissue damage. Without tissue damage the diagnosis is idiopathic hypereosinophilia. [S2]

## Assess

- **The GP starts the work-up** (history, examination, first-line tests for drug and parasitic causes), but must refer to an organ specialist without delay for unusual symptoms or suspected cardiac, respiratory, thrombotic or neurological disease. [S1]
- **For recent-onset HE above 1.5 per nL, look first for drugs and parasites.** Drug-induced HE usually appears 2-8 weeks after starting the drug; common culprits include NSAIDs, antiepileptics, antibiotics, sulfonamides and allopurinol. Stop the suspect drug where possible. [S1]
- **Suspect a clonal (myeloid) cause** with hepato- or splenomegaly, cytopenia, thrombocytosis, polycythaemia, monocytosis, basophilia, raised vitamin B12 or tryptase, or eosinophils that do not normalise on steroids. Endomyocardial fibrosis, border-zone strokes and mucosal ulcers point strongly to FIP1L1::PDGFRA disease, which is mainly seen in men. [S1]
- **First test for clonal HE: FIP1L1::PDGFRA by RT-PCR or RQ-PCR on peripheral blood.** If blood molecular tests are negative, do a bone marrow aspirate with karyotype and FISH for ABL1, PDGFRB, PDGFRA and FGFR1 rearrangements. [S1]
- **Screen every patient for cardiac involvement promptly**, because it can be silent early and late diagnosis carries a poor prognosis. In FIP1L1::PDGFRA disease, cardiac and brain MRI are suggested first-line even when troponin, BNP and echo are normal. [S1]
- **S2 baseline tests in every patient with eosinophilia:** blood count with film differential; liver and kidney function, electrolytes and LDH; ESR and/or CRP; and serum vitamin B12. With no symptoms and only mild to moderate eosinophilia (0.5-1.5 per nL), further tests can wait. [S2]
- **S2 clonal work-up when no secondary cause is found and the count is above 1.5 per nL:** marrow aspirate and trephine; FISH or RT-PCR for FIP1L1::PDGFRA; karyotype; serum tryptase; T-cell immunophenotyping with or without TCR gene rearrangement; and RT-PCR or RNA sequencing to identify the fusion if the karyotype shows a translocation involving the PDGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, JAK2, ABL1 or FLT3 locus or another tyrosine-kinase gene. [S2]
- **S2 cardiac assessment:** ECG, troponin and/or NT-proBNP and echocardiography, with cardiac MRI when troponin is raised or there are clinical signs of heart injury. Record any unexplained thrombosis as eosinophil-related tissue damage. [S2]

## Emergency treatment

- ⚠️ **Do not let the work-up delay treatment of life- or organ-threatening HES:** prednisone 1 mg/kg/day, preceded by IV methylprednisolone 15 mg/kg for 1-3 days depending on the picture, together with antiparasitic treatment. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give ivermectin 200 µg/kg before steroids** (unless contraindicated) to prevent severe strongyloidiasis. [S1]
- **Steroid-resistant HES** means no clinical or laboratory response after 3 days of corticosteroids and should prompt a search for other causes, including clonal disease. [S1]
- ⚠️ **S2 emergency treatment for severe or life-threatening involvement (especially heart or lung):** IV methylprednisolone 1 mg/kg/day, or oral prednisone one-half to 1.0 mg/kg/day, with allopurinol if eosinophilia is extreme; after 1-2 weeks taper slowly to the lowest maintenance dose by 2-3 months (see Unresolved for the different S1 dose). [S2]

## FIP1L1::PDGFRA-positive chronic eosinophilic leukaemia

- ⚠️ **Treat every patient, with or without symptoms, and start promptly**: persistent HE risks irreversible (especially cardiac) damage and leukaemic transformation, and delay raises relapse risk after later stopping. [S1]
- ⚠️ **First line is imatinib 100 mg daily**, which gave molecular remission in 100% of the authors' patients. In a severe, suggestive presentation it can be started before the PCR result. Male and female contraception is required. [S1]
- **With cardiac involvement, check troponin 48 h after starting imatinib** (in hospital if heart disease is severe), and give a short steroid course (for example 1 mg/kg/day for 5-7 days) if troponin rises. [S1]
- **If imatinib seems to fail, check adherence and absorption first, then reconsider the diagnosis.** Rare resistance mutations (T674I, D842V) may respond to dasatinib or nilotinib. [S1]
- **Duration:** imatinib can continue long term; a stop can be tried case by case after at least 5 years in prolonged molecular remission (about 40% stay in remission). Molecular monitoring is mandatory: every 3 months in year one, then every 6 months. [S1]
- **S2 for PDGFRA or PDGFRB rearrangement in chronic phase:** imatinib from 100 mg/day, raised to 400 mg/day if the response is poor, until complete clinical, haematological and molecular remission; assess cytogenetic and molecular response after three months. Maintenance has no consensus: keep the dose or give it every other day or weekly. In blast phase, imatinib 100-400 mg/day with the tumour's own regimen bridges to allogeneic transplant as soon as feasible. Resistance mutations (PDGFRA T674I, D842V; PDGFRB C843G) are reported. [S2]

## Other clonal (PDGFR-rearranged and related) disease

- **Other PDGFRA fusions** (partners such as BCR, ETV6, KIF5B) respond, like FIP1L1::PDGFRA, to low-dose imatinib 100 mg daily. [S1]
- **PDGFRB rearrangements** (for example ETV6::PDGFRB, which can look like CMML) usually respond to imatinib but at a higher starting dose of 400 mg daily, which may be reduced carefully to 100 mg once molecular remission is reached. [S1]
- **FGFR1, JAK2 and FLT3 rearrangements are aggressive and imatinib-resistant;** they need specialist or trial-based targeted therapy and consideration of allogeneic transplant. [S1]
- **S2 for other fusions (clinical trial first):** FGFR1, pemigatinib, midostaurin, ponatinib or olverembatinib; ABL1, dasatinib, nilotinib, asciminib, bosutinib, imatinib or ponatinib; FLT3, gilteritinib, midostaurin, sorafenib or sunitinib; JAK2, ruxolitinib or fedratinib. In blast phase these are combined with AML- or ALL-type chemotherapy as a bridge to transplant, done as soon as feasible. [S2]

## Idiopathic HES

- **Corticosteroids are first line** and usually work quickly. Prefer local steroids where possible; when systemic treatment is needed use prednisone 0.5-1 mg/kg/day, then taper to the lowest effective dose. In chronic disease about 12 months of treatment is advised before trying to stop. [S1]
- **Mepolizumab 300 mg subcutaneously monthly is the recommended first steroid-sparing drug** for chronic steroid-dependent idiopathic HES, or relapsing HES with at least 3 steroid-requiring flares a year. It has no role as first-line therapy. [S1]
- **After mepolizumab failure:** pegylated interferon alfa-2a (start 45 µg weekly) or hydroxycarbamide (start 1 g/day, up to 2 g/day). Empirical imatinib 400 mg/day may be tried when myeloid features are present, stopping if there is no response at 1 month. [S1]
- **S2 first line for idiopathic disease: oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day, tapered slowly from 1-2 weeks to the lowest maintenance dose by 2-3 months;** if it flares during the taper, return at least to the previous dose. Complete plus partial response is sixty-five to 85%. Switch to second line if after 1 month the count is still above 1.5 per nL or the lowest maintenance dose exceeds 10 mg/day. [S2]
- **S2 second-line options:** imatinib four hundred mg/day (stop if no effect after 4-6 weeks); interferon (no agreed dose; takes weeks to act); ciclosporin (one hundred and fifty to 500 mg/day reported); azathioprine from 1-3 mg/kg/day (lower in liver or kidney impairment); hydroxycarbamide one-half to 3.0 g/day, alone or with interferon; or monoclonal antibodies such as mepolizumab, reslizumab or alemtuzumab. [S2]
- **S2 supportive care:** leukapheresis for very high white-cell and eosinophil counts; anticoagulant or antiplatelet treatment to prevent thrombosis; and valve replacement for damaged heart valves. [S2]
