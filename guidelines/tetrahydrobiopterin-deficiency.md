# Tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) deficiencies

## Recognise

- **BH4 deficiencies are six rare neurometabolic disorders** of BH4 synthesis or regeneration. They deplete dopamine and serotonin, and cause hyperphenylalaninaemia (HPA) in all types except AD-GTPCHD and SRD. [S1]
- **BH4 deficiencies are around 1-2% of HPA found on newborn screening;** PTPS deficiency is the most frequent (about 54%), then DHPR deficiency (about 33%). [S1]
- **Consider a BH4 deficiency** with unexplained hypotonia or hypertonia, dystonia or oculogyric crises, parkinsonism, autonomic dysfunction, or symptoms that fluctuate through the day. [S1]
- **Consider AD-GTPCHD (dopa-responsive dystonia)** when dystonia, especially of the legs, starts in the first or second decade with diurnal fluctuation and normal development. [S1]
- **The six disorders, their genes and inheritance (Table 1):** AD-GTPCHD (Segawa disease or dopa-responsive dystonia, DYT5a; GCH1; autosomal dominant); and, all autosomal recessive, AR-GTPCHD (GCH1), PTPSD (PTS), SRD (SPR), DHPR deficiency (QDPR) and PCDD (primapterinuria; PCBD1). [S1]

## Diagnose

- **Newborn screening for PKU may give the first clue** (HPA) to AR-GTPCHD, PTPSD, DHPRD or PCDD, but it cannot detect AD-GTPCHD or SRD. [S1]
- **Refer HPA found on screening to a specialised metabolic centre** for work-up and prompt treatment. [S1]
- **In every screen-detected HPA, analyse pterins (urine or dried blood spot) and measure DHPR enzyme activity in a dried blood spot;** pterins alone cannot rule out DHPR deficiency. [S1]
- **CSF HVA, 5-HIAA, pterins and 5-MTHF differentiate the BH4 deficiencies;** confirm any biochemical diagnosis with molecular genetic testing. [S1]

## Treat: control phenylalanine

- ⚠️ **Do not delay treatment:** long-term neurodevelopmental outcome depends strongly on starting effective therapy early. [S1]
- **Control Phe in the BH4 deficiencies with HPA** (AR-GTPCHD, PTPSD, DHPRD, PCDD), by Phe-reduced diet or sapropterin, with Phe targets as for PKU. **Do not** use a Phe-reduced diet in AD-GTPCHD or SRD, nor as the only treatment for neurological symptoms. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Sapropterin is the treatment of choice in AR-GTPCHD, PTPSD and PCDD:** once daily, titrated to Phe levels. In PCDD it can be tried off after the first year of life with careful Phe monitoring. [S1]
- **In DHPR deficiency, a Phe-reduced diet is the preferred method;** sapropterin can still be considered, because evidence of harm is scarce. [S1]

## Treat: neurotransmitter replacement

- ⚠️ **L-Dopa, always with a decarboxylase inhibitor (4:1), is first-line** in AD-GTPCHD, AR-GTPCHD, DHPRD, PTPSD and SRD. [S1]
- **Start L-Dopa low, in several daily doses, and titrate slowly;** the target in infants under 40 kg (except AD-GTPCHD) is 10 mg/kg/day if tolerated. AD-GTPCHD usually responds to lower doses. [S1]
- **5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) is recommended in DHPRD, PTPSD and SRD** (conditionally in AR-GTPCHD). Start it after L-Dopa, at a lower dose, and do not change both drugs at once. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give folinic acid in DHPR deficiency, not folic acid,** which can worsen cerebral folate deficiency. Consider folinic acid in any BH4 deficiency with low CSF 5-MTHF. [S1]
- **Second line, for residual symptoms or L-Dopa side effects:** a dopamine agonist, preferably non-ergot (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine) or bromocriptine with cardiac screening; or a MAO inhibitor. [S1]
- ⚠️ **5-HTP combined with an SSRI, especially at very high doses, can cause serotonin syndrome.** [S1]

## Follow up

- **See patients at least yearly with a neurologist experienced in movement or neurometabolic disorders;** during titration see infants every 3 months and older children at least every 6 months. [S1]
- **Screen PCDD patients for low magnesium and for HNF1A-like (MODY3) diabetes in puberty;** follow all patients for psychiatric, behavioural and sleep problems. [S1]
- **Plan transition to adult specialist care early;** offer genetic counselling to every family, and provide multidisciplinary supervision during and after pregnancy. [S1]
