# Short bowel syndrome and chronic intestinal failure in adults

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with short bowel syndrome (SBS), including SBS with chronic (type III) intestinal failure: classification, referral, diet, oral fluids and rehydration solutions, antisecretory and antidiarrhoeal drugs, GLP-2 analogue (teduglutide) treatment and non-transplant surgery. Home parenteral nutrition technique, catheter care and intestinal transplantation are summarised only. Children are out of scope (see `slow-weight-gain-children`, `cows-milk-allergy-formulas`). Non-specific diarrhoea: see `diarrhoea`.

> ⚠️ International guideline (ESPEN, 2023). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> 🇦🇺 **S2 is Australian but is not a guideline:** a 2026 report from the Australian Intestinal Transplant Service (a retrospective series). It is used only for where and when to refer in Australia; all management recommendations rest on ESPEN (S1).

> ⚠️ **Two different 'type 3's.** The PBS row *Type III short bowel syndrome with intestinal failure* uses the **functional** classification (type III = chronic intestinal failure needing IV support for months or years). ESPEN's **anatomical** SBS type 3 means a jejuno-ileal anastomosis with an intact colon and ileocaecal valve. Do not confuse them.

---

## Recognise and classify

- **In adults, SBS means less than 200 cm of small bowel left in continuity.** Symptoms of SBS despite a longer remnant are called 'functional SBS'. [S1]
- **Classify SBS by the anatomy that remains:** type 1 is an end small-bowel stoma; type 2 is a jejuno-colic anastomosis; type 3 is a jejuno-ileal anastomosis with the whole colon and ileocaecal valve kept. [S1]
- **Type III (chronic) intestinal failure** is a long-term state in a metabolically stable patient who needs intravenous support for months or years; it may or may not reverse. [S1]

## Refer and organise care

- **Home parenteral nutrition is the primary, life-saving treatment** for chronic intestinal failure, whether reversible or permanent. (Grade B) [S1]
- **Refer early to an intestinal failure / rehabilitation centre** with both medical and surgical expertise. This gives the best chance of coming off parenteral nutrition, avoids its failure and allows timely transplant assessment. (GPP) [S1]
- **Manage SBS through a multidisciplinary team** to get the most from intestinal rehabilitation. (GPP) [S1]
- **Australian authors' recommendation:** refer every patient with intestinal failure to an expert tertiary centre for multidisciplinary rehabilitation aimed at getting off parenteral nutrition, and on to the Australian Intestinal Transplant Service if life-threatening complications develop. [S2]

## Intestinal transplantation in Australia

- **Australia has a single intestinal transplant service** for adults and children: a joint Royal Children's Hospital and Austin Health program in Melbourne, running since 2010 and now also accepting New Zealand patients. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Refer early for transplant assessment when the risk of complications is highest:** radiation enteritis, microvillous inclusion disease, several enterocutaneous fistulae, an ultrashort bowel such as an end duodenostomy, intestinal failure following bariatric surgery, and loss of two of the four central veins above the diaphragm. Late referral, with long waits, harms outcomes. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Waiting-list deaths are high:** about 30% for people awaiting a combined liver-intestine graft and about 10% for an isolated intestine. Assessing patients before liver failure sets in makes an isolated intestinal graft, with a shorter wait, more likely. [S2]
- **Candidates needing a liver-containing graft because of IF-associated liver disease** go on the national liver transplant list in category 2C. [S2]
- **Most people on home parenteral nutrition are not transplant candidates:** long-term PN rarely leads to life-threatening complications, and a large European study found suitable candidates among just 16% of adults (35% in children) on HPN. [S2]
- **Likely under-referral:** the new national HPN registry counted 328 people on HPN in 2024 (244 adults, 84 children); by European rates about 39 adults and 29 children could have an indication, more than are currently referred. [S2]
- **Australian results:** nineteen patients had 20 transplants between July 2010 and April 2026, half of them for short bowel syndrome. Graft and patient survival were 85% and 89.5% at median follow-up; conditional 5-year figures were 67% and 75%. Every recipient came off parenteral nutrition before discharge, a median of 21 days after transplant. [S2]

## Diet

- **Diet should be guided by an expert dietitian**, drawing on the patient's own experience and, ideally, metabolic balance studies, so that the patient can stick to it. (GPP) [S1]
- **If the colon is still in continuity**, the diet can be rich in complex carbohydrate and low in simple sugars and fat (weakest grade), and should contain plenty of **medium-chain triglyceride (MCT)**, which gives a small gain in energy absorbed over ordinary long-chain fat (Grade B). [S1]
- **Without a colon in continuity**, any ratio of fat to carbohydrate is acceptable, but keep simple sugars low. (weakest grade) [S1]
- **On a low-fat or MCT-substituted diet, watch for essential fatty acid and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency.** (GPP) [S1]
- **Do not routinely remove lactose**; restrict it only when intolerance is clinically clear, such as more diarrhoea or stoma output after lactose. (weakest grade) [S1]
- **Glutamine, probiotics and other add-on nutrients are not recommended** to promote intestinal rehabilitation. (weakest grade) [S1]

## Fluids and oral rehydration

- **With an end jejunostomy (type 1 SBS)**, patients can salt food freely and should separate drinks from meals. (GPP) [S1]
- **For borderline dehydration or sodium depletion, an isotonic, high-sodium oral rehydration solution** can replace sodium lost from the stoma. (weakest grade) [S1]
- ⚠️ **A secretory, high-output jejunostomy needs low-sodium drinks cut back**: both hypotonic fluids (water, tea, coffee, alcohol) and hypertonic ones (fruit juice, cola) increase stoma output. (GPP) [S1]

## Drug treatment

- **An H2-receptor antagonist or PPI may reduce faecal fluid and sodium losses**, especially in the first six months after surgery and when output is over 2 L/d (weakest grade); it can also be continued long term in individual patients (GPP). [S1]
- **Oral loperamide is the antidiarrhoeal of choice** to cut stoma fluid and sodium losses (Grade A), preferred over codeine or opium because it is neither addictive nor sedating (Grade A). [S1]
- **Octreotide can be tried for a high-output jejunostomy**, mainly soon after resection, when fluid and electrolytes stay hard to control despite standard measures. Monitor for fluid retention and possible harm to intestinal adaptation. (GPP) [S1]
- **Antibiotics are not routine when the colon is preserved**, because colonic fermentation salvages energy; occasional courses can help suspected bacterial overgrowth with dysmotility or blind loops. (GPP) [S1]
- **Oral absorption of any drug is uncertain in SBS.** Prescribe individually, titrate to effect or levels, and consider parenteral, transdermal or rectal routes when absorption is limited. (GPP) [S1]

## GLP-2 analogue (teduglutide)

- **Consider an intestinal growth factor when a patient still needs parenteral nutrition after adaptation**, usually twelve to 24 months after the last resection, if there is no contraindication. (Grade B) [S1]
- **Teduglutide is the first-choice growth factor** for carefully selected candidates. (Grade A) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Before starting, exclude polyps and cancer:** colonoscopy (if any colon or rectum remains), gastroscopy and abdominal ultrasound for everyone, which also clarify strictures, blind loops or active Crohn's disease. (GPP) [S1]
- **Only experienced SBS clinicians should prescribe it**, with the means to measure benefit objectively (fluid, electrolyte and energy balance by standard protocol) against adverse effects, risk and cost. Explain the chance of weaning, the duration, what happens on stopping and the monitoring required. (GPP) [S1]

## Surgery

- **At resection, keep as much bowel as possible** to avoid parenteral nutrition dependence, and **restore intestinal continuity whenever possible** (both Grade B). Bowel-lengthening procedures may suit selected patients (weakest grade). [S1]
