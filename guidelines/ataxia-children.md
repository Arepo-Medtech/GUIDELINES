## Recognise it

- **Assessment hunts for serious, treatable causes:** trauma, stroke, mass lesions, toxin ingestion, and CNS infection or inflammation. [S1]
- **The commonest cause is post-infectious acute cerebellar ataxia**, which settles on its own and is **a diagnosis of exclusion.** [S1]
- **Mimics:** weakness or pain can cause an unsteady gait, and a frightened or very unwell child may appear ataxic. [S1]
- **Ataxia** is unsteadiness, imbalance or clumsiness from uncoordinated muscle movement, affecting gait, speech or motor skills. **Acute = under 72 hours in a previously well child.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Vascular causes may need urgent lysis** — see the Stroke guideline. [S1]

## Common causes of acute ataxia

- **Post-infectious acute cerebellar ataxia** (viral — varicella, EBV, HHV6, enteroviruses — or *Mycoplasma*): most commonly **5–10 days after a prodromal illness**, typically in children **2–7 years**. [S1]
- **Its course:** cerebellar features are **worst at the start**, typically getting better within **48–72 hours**; most recover fully in **10–21 days** (over 90% resolve by 2 months). [S1]
- **Toxins** (antihistamines, antiepileptics, ethanol/ethylene glycol/isopropyl, sedative-hypnotics): variable and dose-related; ask about environmental exposure, medications the child could reach, or a recent medication change; look for **altered GCS or a toxidrome.** [S1]
- **Tumours** (via haemorrhage, oedema or obstructive hydrocephalus): acute or acute-on-chronic; **conscious level is usually normal**, with raised ICP features, constitutional symptoms or focal deficits. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Trauma** (haemorrhage, contusion, diffuse axonal injury, concussion): acute or chronic; look for a history or signs of head injury and **risk factors for non-accidental injury.** [S1]
- **ADEM** (autoimmune CNS demyelination): typically **1–4 weeks after a febrile illness**, with lethargy or irritability and multiple deficits — pyramidal signs, optic neuritis, hemiplegia, ataxia. [S1]

## Assess

- **History:** time course (acute under 72 hours); antecedent viral illness, rash, head trauma or earache; drug, toxin or environmental exposures. [S1]
- **A thorough neurological examination is essential**, including conscious state; **exclude meningoencephalitis** (fever, meningism). [S1]
- **Localise it.** Cerebellar: dysdiadochokinesis, dysmetria, dysarthria, tremor, nystagmus, staggering or wide-based gait. Sensory: positive Romberg, hyporeflexia, lost proprioception, vibration and light touch. Vestibular: nystagmus without other cerebellar signs. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Red flags:** altered conscious state · raised intracranial pressure · meningism · focal neurology · weakness · abnormal deep tendon reflexes · lost vibration sense, proprioception or tactile discrimination. [S1]

## Investigate and treat

- **If features fit post-infectious acute cerebellar ataxia, no investigations may be needed.** Investigate if ataxia **persists beyond 72 hours, gets worse at any point after onset**, or another cause is suspected. [S1]
- **Then consider:** blood glucose, venous blood gas, electrolytes, LFTs, anticonvulsant level and ethanol; CT or MRI; toxicology, metabolic screen, viral studies, vitamin B12, EEG — and **lumbar puncture only after neuroimaging.** [S1]
- **Treatment depends on cause**; post-infectious acute cerebellar ataxia generally needs **no treatment.** [S1]

## Consult, transfer, discharge

- **Consult the local paediatric team** for acute ataxia of unclear cause, or any chronic or recurrent ataxia. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider transfer** for other focal neurological signs, raised ICP, a history atypical for post-infectious ataxia or unclear cause, or care beyond the hospital's comfort level. [S1]
- **Consider discharge** once the cause is clear, follow-up is arranged and the child is clinically well. [S1]
