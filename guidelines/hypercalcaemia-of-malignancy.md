# Hypercalcaemia of malignancy

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, grading, investigation and acute management of hypercalcaemia in adults with cancer: rehydration, IV bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid, pamidronate), denosumab, calcitonin and glucocorticoids. Bone-directed therapy to prevent skeletal events from bone metastases: see `bone-metastases`. The drug-by-drug HCM administration protocols (zoledronic acid, pamidronate) are separate eviQ pages. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **Hypercalcaemia of malignancy means an albumin-corrected calcium above 2.6 mmol/L**, or above the laboratory's upper reference limit, in a person with cancer. [S1]
- **No formal severity scale exists, but a common split is:** mild 2.6 to under 3.1 mmol/L, moderate 3.1 to 3.4 mmol/L, severe above 3.4 mmol/L. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Start treatment for anyone with symptoms or a calcium above 3.1 mmol/L.** Severe hypercalcaemia is a medical emergency and is treated aggressively. [S1]
- **Up to 30 percent of people with cancer develop it, and it carries a poor prognosis.** Breast and renal cancer, myeloma, lymphoma and squamous cell cancers are the usual causes, mostly through a tumour protein that behaves like parathyroid hormone. [S1]
- **Three main mechanisms drive bone calcium release:** lytic bone metastases releasing cytokines (typical of myeloma); tumour PTH-related protein, which also makes the kidney hold calcium (breast, head and neck, kidney and lung squamous cancers); and tumour-made calcitriol, which also boosts gut absorption (some lymphomas). [S1]
- **Primary hyperparathyroidism is common and can coexist with cancer;** a PTH level tells them apart. [S1]
- **Symptoms are often vague and can look like advanced cancer or treatment side effects:** thirst, polyuria, confusion, weakness, fatigue, anorexia and constipation; when severe, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, lethargy and a falling conscious level, which can progress to coma and death. Arrhythmias are uncommon. How fast the calcium rose matters as well as the level. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Grade 3 (above 3.1 to 3.4 mmol/L)** may be tolerated if it rose slowly, but a rapid rise causes marked symptoms and usually needs prompt treatment. **Grade 4 (above 3.4 mmol/L) needs urgent correction regardless of symptoms**, because of the risk of arrhythmia and coma. [S1]

## Investigate

- **Correct calcium for albumin:** corrected calcium (mmol/L) = measured calcium (mmol/L) + 0.02 × (40 − albumin in g/L). [S1]
- **Also check** creatinine, electrolytes with phosphate and magnesium, PTH (for primary hyperparathyroidism), PTHrP (for humoral hypercalcaemia), 1,25- and 25-hydroxy vitamin D, TSH and vitamin A, plus an ECG for a short QT or other conduction changes. [S1]

## Treat

- **Goals: relieve symptoms, return corrected calcium to normal and, where possible, treat the cancer.** Stop or cut back medicines that raise calcium, such as thiazides, calcitriol, calcium supplements, antacids and lithium. [S1]
- **Symptomatic hypercalcaemia generally needs prompt, often inpatient, care with IV fluids and a bisphosphonate.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Rehydration is essential:** these patients are dry, and a low GFR further blocks calcium excretion. Give IV sodium chloride 0.9%, tailored to volume, cardiac and renal status; for acute severe hypercalcaemia in an adult, 4 to 6 litres over 24 hours is a reasonable rate. [S1]
- **Watch for fluid overload in older patients or with renal impairment.** Loop diuretics do not lower calcium and are used only for fluid overload, with potassium and phosphate monitored and replaced. Calcium reaches its lowest point at 2 to 4 days; severe renal failure may need dialysis. [S1]
- **IV bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid or pamidronate) work fast and durably.** Local policy may decide which; zoledronic acid is often preferred as it is more potent and infuses in 15 minutes against 2 hours for pamidronate. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Bisphosphonates are nephrotoxic:** good hydration helps protect the kidneys, and renal impairment may mean a dose change or avoiding them (see the product information). They can cause hypocalcaemia when vitamin D is deficient or PTH is suppressed. [S1]
- **Expect calcium to fall within 3 to 4 days, bottom out at 4 to 7 days, and stay down for 2 to 4 weeks.** Pamidronate can be repeated if calcium has not dropped within 48 hours; zoledronic acid can be repeated no sooner than 7 days for refractory or relapsed hypercalcaemia. [S1]
- **Denosumab (anti-RANKL) is an option if hypercalcaemia resists zoledronic acid, or bisphosphonates are ruled out by severe renal impairment.** It acts in about 4 days with a nadir by day 10. Hypocalcaemia is a real risk, especially with poor kidney function, so monitor calcium regularly. [S1]
- **Calcitonin works within hours, but tolerance sets in within 48 to 72 hours.** It is most useful, with fluids and a bisphosphonate, for symptomatic severe hypercalcaemia (calcium above 3.5 mmol/L): calcitonin gives the quick drop, the bisphosphonate the lasting one. No renal dose change is needed; the Australian product is calcitonin salmon (salcatonin). [S1]
- **Glucocorticoids are the primary treatment when excess gut calcium absorption is the cause** (vitamin D toxicity, granulomatous disease, some lymphomas). They take roughly 2-4 days to work; taper them off to avoid adrenal insufficiency. [S1]
