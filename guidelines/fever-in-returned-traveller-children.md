# Fever in the recently returned traveller (children)

> ⚠️ **Think falciparum malaria first** — the commonest serious infection and cause of death in returned travellers. Consider it in undifferentiated fever **up to two years** after return from an endemic region.

## Key points

- **Most infections are mild or self-limiting**, but their non-specific presentation makes them hard to tell apart from serious infection such as malaria. [S1]
- ⚠️ **In returning travellers, falciparum malaria is the leading serious infection and the leading cause of death.** [S1]
- **Common non-travel diseases are more likely than exotic ones.** A serious cause is more likely in children who travelled to visit friends and relatives. Consider non-infectious causes too (e.g. DVT/PE). [S1]
- **Drug-resistant infection or colonisation may have been acquired**, especially if the child was hospitalised. [S1]

## Isolation and public health

- ⚠️ **Ask whether this could be a disease of public health importance** needing immediate infection control; contact the local health department for advice. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Suspected measles: negative pressure room with airborne precautions.** Possible novel respiratory illness: appropriate precautions. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Haemorrhagic fevers (Ebola, Marburg, Lassa, Crimean-Congo): strict isolation and strict barrier nursing.** [S1]

## History and examination

- **Take a travel history:** location and type (country/region, urban/rural, living conditions, altitude) · timing (dates, seasons, duration) · exposures (animals, insect bites, water and food, activities including sexual contact, drug use, tattoos, sick contacts). [S1]
- **Check immunisation history** — routine and travel-specific; typhoid vaccine is only about 70% effective. Ask about prophylaxis adherence, nets and repellent, and any healthcare or medicines abroad. [S1]
- **Fever is the only finding in most.** ⚠️ **Altered consciousness, haemorrhage or hypotension suggest cerebral malaria or haemorrhagic fever with shock.** [S1]
- **Eschar:** rickettsial infection, borrelia, Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever. **Petechiae:** meningococcal disease, viral haemorrhagic fever, rickettsia. [S1]
- **Altered conscious state, lethargy or meningism:** cerebral malaria, meningitis, African trypanosomiasis. [S1]
- **Fever persisting over 2 weeks:** malaria, EBV, enteric fever, toxoplasmosis, CMV, acute HIV, TB, brucellosis, Q fever, acute schistosomiasis. **Fever starting over 6 weeks after travel:** TB, amoebic liver abscess, acute hepatitis (B, C or E), *P. vivax* or *P. ovale*. [S1]

## Investigations

- **Fever alone** (think malaria, typhoid, dengue, hepatitis A): **FBE with thick and thin film**, BGL, blood culture, LFT if jaundiced, serum to store; discuss specific tests with Infectious Diseases. [S1]
- **Also consider:** measles serology ± throat or nose PCR · urine microscopy and culture · **CSF in severely unwell children with meningism, encephalopathy or seizures**, after considering whether neuroimaging is needed first. [S1]
- **Fever with diarrhoea** (think malaria, typhoid, dengue, hepatitis, cholera, dysentery, travellers' diarrhoea): the fever-alone tests, with stool MCS and OCP added (the latter particularly if prolonged beyond 10 days). [S1]
- **Fever with respiratory symptoms** (think malaria, pneumonia, influenza, TB): as for fever alone, plus CXR and a respiratory virus swab including influenza and COVID; discuss TB investigations with Infectious Diseases. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Undifferentiated fever within two years of return from an endemic region → consider malaria.** [S1]
- **Travellers' diarrhoea** = more than 3 diarrhoeal episodes in 24 hours after travel, plus cramping, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting or fever. [S1]

## Treatment and escalation

- **Most children need no empiric treatment** and can be managed as outpatients with GP follow-up. [S1]
- ⚠️ **A sick child may warrant empiric treatment for sepsis, malaria and/or typhoid** — see the individual guidelines or local antimicrobial guidance, and discuss with Infectious Diseases when needed. [S1]
- **Consult the local paediatric team** for suspected sepsis, malaria or typhoid, or an unclear diagnosis with clinical concern. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider transfer** for severe malaria, a sick child with persistent fever despite basic empiric therapy, or care beyond the local hospital's comfort level. [S1]
