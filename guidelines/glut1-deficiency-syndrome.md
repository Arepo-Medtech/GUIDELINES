# Glucose transporter type 1 deficiency syndrome (Glut1DS)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising, confirming and treating Glut1DS (the PBS 'cerebrospinal fluid glucose transporter defect') in children and adults: clinical clues, diagnostic lumbar puncture and SLC2A1 testing, ketogenic diet therapy, antiseizure drugs and long-term monitoring. General ketogenic-diet management and other epilepsies: see `epilepsy`. Initial work-up of a suspected inborn error: `metabolic-disorders-children`.

> ⚠️ International guideline (International Glut1DS Study Group, 2020). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

It adds CSF glucose ranges, erythrocyte blood tests, how to act on a probable or possible diagnosis, which antiseizure drugs have failed, and an Australian prevalence figure. It also lists acetazolamide as a possible option, where S1 warns against carbonic anhydrase inhibitors on the diet; both are shown.

> The PBS lists **medium-chain triglyceride (MCT)** products for this condition, as a way of delivering a ketogenic diet. The source recommends ketogenic diet therapy but does not discuss the MCT variant specifically.

## Recognise

- **Typical picture:** epilepsy starting in infancy, slowing head growth, delayed development and complex movement disorders. [S1]
- **Think of Glut1DS with:** an unexplained movement disorder (spasticity, dystonia, ataxia); drug-resistant epilepsy that is fully controlled by a ketogenic diet; unexplained paroxysmal events at any age; absence epilepsy starting before age 4; or myoclonic-atonic (Doose) epilepsy. [S1]
- **Paroxysmal eye-head movements in infancy** were judged specific for Glut1DS by nearly all expert centres (12 of 13). [S1]
- **Movement problems are typically worse before meals and better after eating,** and are often brought on by fasting or exercise. [S1]
- **The picture changes with age:** seizures dominate in infants and young children, then tend to fade; movement disorders become the main problem in adolescents and adults. [S1]
- 🇦🇺 **How common:** a retrospective Australian analysis estimated a prevalence of 1 in 90,000 (Denmark 1 in 83,000), probably an underestimate. Seizures affect about 90% and usually start before 2 years, most often at 1 to 6 months. [S2]

## Confirm the diagnosis

- **The hallmark is low CSF glucose with normal blood glucose (hypoglycorrhachia)**, with CSF lactate low-normal or low, which separates it from mitochondrial disease. Exclude other causes first: hypoglycaemia, meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage, or a VP shunt. [S1]
- **Do the lumbar puncture after a four- to six-hour fast,** and measure blood glucose immediately beforehand to avoid a stress-related rise. [S1]
- **CSF glucose cut-off:** classically below 2.2 mmol/L (40 mg/dL); milder cases can sit at 2.2 to 2.9 mmol/L, but are never normal. [S1]
- **SLC2A1 testing:** sequencing finds a variant in 81%–89%, and deletion/duplication analysis a further 11%–14%. A negative result does not exclude Glut1DS. [S1]
- **A definite diagnosis needs all three:** characteristic clinical features, hypoglycorrhachia and a pathogenic SLC2A1 variant. With characteristic features, most experts accept two of the three. [S1]
- **CSF glucose in Glut1DS (S2):** typically 0.9 to 2.9 mmol/L, always below 3.3 mmol/L; most patients are below 2.2 mmol/L with a CSF/blood glucose ratio below 0.4. The absolute CSF glucose is more reliable than the ratio. A low CSF lactate helps exclude mitochondrial disease and hexokinase 1 deficiency, in which lactate is usually raised. [S2]
- **Blood tests of red-cell Glut1 (S2):** red-cell 3-O-methyl-D-glucose uptake is reduced in about 98.6% of genetically confirmed patients but needs a specialised radiotracer laboratory; flow cytometry of red-cell Glut1 (METAglut1) was 80% sensitive and > 99% specific in a prospective multicentre study. [S2]
- **Act on an incomplete picture (S2):** with classic symptoms and low CSF glucose but no SLC2A1 variant, or low CSF glucose and a variant without classic symptoms, Glut1DS is highly probable and the ketogenic diet should be started; a good response or a family history supports the diagnosis. With only one supporting finding, or normal CSF glucose, it is possible and the diet should be tried. [S2]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **Ketogenic diet therapy (KDT) is the treatment of choice; start it as early as possible.** All 13 centres agreed. [S1]
- **Under two years, use a classical 3:1 ketogenic diet.** [S1]
- **The modified Atkins diet is a good alternative** for adolescents, adults and patients who cannot keep to the classical diet. [S1]
- **Do not use the low glycaemic index treatment:** it produces very little ketosis and has no evidence of benefit in Glut1DS. [S1]
- **Monitor ketosis with blood beta-hydroxybutyrate (target 2–5 mmol/L)**, not urine ketones, which are only qualitative. [S1]
- **Do not stop the diet after two years** as in other epilepsies: benefit is thought to continue into adulthood, so continue it for as long as it is tolerated. [S1]
- **Check carnitine levels,** because patients on the diet can become carnitine-deficient over time. [S1]
- **No antiseizure drug can be recommended:** none treats the underlying defect, and some may interact harmfully with the diet. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Drugs to be wary of:** carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide, topiramate, sulthiame) can worsen acidosis; topiramate, acetazolamide and zonisamide raise kidney-stone risk on the diet; barbiturates, diazepam, valproate and others inhibit Glut1 in the laboratory. [S1]
- **Triheptanoin is not an evidence-based option:** its phase 3 trial in Glut1DS missed its primary and secondary endpoints. [S1]
- **Adults:** consider a pregnancy test before starting a ketogenic diet in women of childbearing age (teratogenic risk unknown). The modified Atkins diet is a reasonable choice for poorly controlled seizures or paroxysmal movement disorders. [S1]
- **Keep the diet going long term if tolerated (S2),** usually into adolescence or adulthood, although whether it is needed for life is uncertain. Start and supervise it by the International Ketogenic Diet Study Group recommendations (see `ketogenic-diet-therapy`). [S2]
- **Antiseizure drugs that have failed (S2):** ethosuximide, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine and zonisamide have been ineffective; a minority with generalised epilepsy respond to conventional drugs, and late-onset generalised tonic–clonic seizures may predict a response. In vitro, phenytoin may enhance glucose transport by 30% to 60% (effect in patients unclear). [S2]
- **Other options when the diet fails or cannot be used (S2), all with limited evidence:** acetazolamide cut seizures in 76% in a small study (by more than half in 58%); diazoxide to raise blood glucose; triheptanoin may reduce absence seizures but not overall seizures or paroxysmal movement disorders off the diet. [S2]

## Follow-up

- **Monitor long-term diet effects regularly:** kidney stones, growth, and cardiovascular risk (blood pressure, fasting lipids, and transcranial Doppler from age 10). [S1]
- **Plan the transition to adult care** for both diet therapy and medical follow-up. [S1]
- **Do not stop the ketogenic diet to join a trial of a new treatment:** the group strongly rejected this. [S1]
