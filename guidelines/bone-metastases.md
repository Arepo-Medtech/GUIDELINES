# Bone metastases: denosumab to prevent skeletal-related events

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** denosumab 120 mg every 4 weeks to prevent skeletal-related events in adults with bone metastases, following eviQ's castration-resistant prostate cancer protocol: dosing, calcium and vitamin D, hypocalcaemia, jaw osteonecrosis, monitoring and trial evidence. Zoledronic acid and pamidronate for bone metastases are separate eviQ protocols. Acute hypercalcaemia: see `hypercalcaemia-of-malignancy`. Myeloma bone disease: see the myeloma guidelines. Osteoporosis-dose denosumab (60 mg): see `osteoporosis`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ⚠️ **This guideline follows a single eviQ protocol written for bone metastases from castration-resistant prostate cancer.** The PBS row 'Bone metastases' also covers other solid tumours and three bisphosphonates; the safety points here (calcium, jaw, kidneys) apply broadly, but check the tumour-specific protocol.

## Who it is for

- **Denosumab is given to prevent skeletal-related events in men whose castration-resistant prostate cancer has spread to bone.** [S1]
- **Whether to keep going once a skeletal-related event has already happened is uncertain.** [S1]

## Dose and schedule

- ⚠️ **Denosumab 120 mg subcutaneously every 28 days, continuing until unacceptable toxicity.** It is a PBS authority item. [S1]
- **After one to two years, consider spacing doses to every 12 weeks.** Safety data beyond two years are limited and jaw osteonecrosis may become more likely with longer exposure; this is the eviQ reference committee's expert opinion. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Every patient takes daily calcium (at least 500 mg) and vitamin D (at least 400 IU) for the whole course**, unless contraindicated. [S1]
- **No kidney dose adjustment is needed.** Denosumab has not been studied in hepatic impairment. [S1]
- **Administration:** let the refrigerated drug warm to room temperature (about 30 minutes), inject slowly under the skin, and rotate sites between the upper outer thigh, the abdomen (keeping 5 cm clear of the navel) and the upper arm. [S1]

## Safety and monitoring

- ⚠️ **Severe symptomatic hypocalcaemia (corrected calcium below 1.75 mmol/L) has occurred, sometimes fatally**, with confusion, tetany, seizures or QTc prolongation. Risk is higher with eGFR under 30 mL/min/1.73 m², on dialysis, or after thyroid surgery. [S1]
- **Check calcium, magnesium and phosphate before starting and before every dose;** check vitamin D when clinically indicated. [S1]
- ⚠️ **See a dentist before the first dose and every 6 months during treatment,** and avoid dental procedures once treatment has started, to lower the risk of jaw osteonecrosis. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Never combine denosumab with a bisphosphonate.** Other calcium-lowering drugs (for example calcitonin, carbamazepine, phenytoin) add to the hypocalcaemic effect: use them cautiously, monitor calcium, and keep calcium and vitamin D intake adequate. Immunosuppressants may raise infection risk. [S1]
- **Expected side effects:** headache, fatigue, joint and muscle pain, and low calcium and phosphate; later, jaw osteonecrosis and, very rarely, atypical femoral fractures that may relate to how long treatment lasts. [S1]

## Evidence

- **The supporting phase 3 trial randomised 1904 men with castration-resistant prostate cancer** to denosumab 120 mg or zoledronic acid 4 mg (adjusted for creatinine clearance), each every 4 weeks. [S1]
- **Denosumab delayed the first skeletal-related event:** median 20.7 months against 17.1 months with zoledronic acid (hazard ratio 0.82), meeting non-inferiority and also superiority (p=0.008). [S1]
- **Hypocalcaemia was about twice as frequent with denosumab (13% vs 6%);** jaw osteonecrosis was uncommon in both arms (2% vs 1%, p=0.09). [S1]
