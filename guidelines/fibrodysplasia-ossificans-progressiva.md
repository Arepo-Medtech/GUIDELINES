## Recognise and diagnose

- **FOP is an ultra-rare, fully penetrant autosomal dominant disorder: malformed great toes at birth and progressive heterotopic ossification (HO)** in set anatomical patterns. [S1]
- **Flare-ups, painful inflammatory soft-tissue swellings, usually start in the first decade and mostly turn into bone;** they can be mistaken for tumours. Most patients use a wheelchair by their third decade, and median life expectancy is 56 years, often ending in thoracic insufficiency. [S1]
- **Diagnose clinically (malformed great toes plus progressive HO) and confirm with a pathogenic ACVR1 variant;** about 97% carry ACVR1 R206H. [S1]
- ⚠️ **If FOP is suspected, defer every elective procedure — surgery, biopsy and IM vaccines — until the diagnosis is confirmed.** [S1]

## Prevent injury and flare-ups

- ⚠️ **Avoid the triggers:** contact sport, soft-tissue and blunt muscle injury, fatigue and stretching, IM injections, biopsies, removing heterotopic bone, non-urgent surgery, and viral illness. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Never do passive range-of-motion exercises;** activity is otherwise encouraged, especially singing, water exercise and respiratory activities. [S1]
- **Surgery only in emergencies, and never to remove heterotopic bone.** [S1]
- **Vaccinate subcutaneously; never IM or with live intranasal influenza vaccine.** Not during a flare-up, and wait 8 weeks after one resolves. Immunise household contacts against influenza, pertussis and COVID-19. [S1]
- **Venepuncture and superficial IV access are fine if gentle, with brief tourniquet time;** avoid central lines, PICC lines and arterial punctures unless essential. [S1]

## Treat flare-ups

- ⚠️ **Limb, jaw or submandibular flare-up: prednisone 2 mg/kg once daily in the morning (maximum 100 mg) for 4 days, started as early as possible;** keep a supply at home. [S1]
- **Best within 24 h of onset; after 2 days prednisone works less well.** If a flare-up recurs on stopping, repeat the 4-day course then taper about 10% a day over 10 days. Long-term steroids help nothing and do harm. [S1]
- **Back or chest flare-ups:** NSAIDs or COX-2 inhibitors with careful gut protection, analgesics, muscle relaxants and ice; steroids work less well here. Avoid narcotic analgesia. [S1]
- **After severe soft-tissue injury, prednisone 1-2 mg/kg daily (maximum 100 mg) for 3-4 days may prevent a flare-up;** not for minor bumps. Also give it around dental or surgical procedures. [S1]
- **Palovarotene (an RAR-gamma agonist) is approved in Australia, the US, Canada, UAE and Russia, but not in Europe;** it is the only regulator-approved medicine, for chronic and episodic use. [S1]
- **No proven benefit on flare-ups from chronic NSAIDs/COX-2 inhibitors, bisphosphonates or off-label drugs (imatinib, tofacitinib, anakinra and others);** chemotherapy and radiotherapy are contraindicated. [S1]

## Emergencies

- ⚠️ **A submandibular flare-up is a medical emergency** (airway and swallowing): give IV methylprednisolone 80 mg or dexamethasone 15 mg at once, and monitor the airway. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Airway:** intubate only awake, nasotracheal and fibre-optic, by an experienced anaesthetist. Do not manipulate the neck, brace it after head injury, and avoid unmonitored oxygen. [S1]
- **Any head injury needs a CT, even without loss of consciousness:** stiff arms cannot break a fall, and epidural haematoma is common. [S1]
- **Suspected compartment syndrome: do not do a fasciotomy** (it worsens the flare-up); consider mannitol. [S1]

## Monitor

- **Lungs:** baseline lung function tests, echocardiogram, chest X-ray and oximetry after age 4; respiratory specialist review by the end of the second decade. [S1]
- **Screen hearing in childhood; prevent kidney stones (3-fold risk) with 1.5-2 L/day of fluid.** Pregnancy carries major risk and needs a high-risk centre. [S1]
