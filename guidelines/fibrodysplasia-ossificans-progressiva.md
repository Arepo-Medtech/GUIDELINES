# Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP)

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosis, injury and flare-up prevention, corticosteroid flare-up treatment, immunisation, anaesthesia and procedures, emergencies, and monitoring for fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP) in children and adults. ## Recognise and diagnose

- **FOP is an ultra-rare, fully penetrant autosomal dominant disorder: malformed great toes at birth and progressive heterotopic ossification (HO)** in set anatomical patterns. [S1]
- **Flare-ups, painful inflammatory soft-tissue swellings, usually start in the first decade and mostly turn into bone;** they can be mistaken for tumours. Most patients use a wheelchair by their third decade, and median life expectancy is 56 years, often ending in thoracic insufficiency. [S1]
- **Diagnose clinically (malformed great toes plus progressive HO) and confirm with a pathogenic ACVR1 variant;** about 97% carry ACVR1 R206H. [S1]
- ⚠️ **If FOP is suspected, defer every elective procedure — surgery, biopsy and IM vaccines — until the diagnosis is confirmed.** [S1]
- **Early diagnosis matters most for the course of the disease.** The curved or shortened great toes are visible at birth, so routine infant checks can raise the suspicion. [S2]
- **About 50% of patients are first given another diagnosis.** Common first suspicions are a cancer, juvenile fibromatosis, non-hereditary myositis ossificans, Klippel-Feil syndrome and hallux valgus. [S2]

## Organise care and follow up

- **Care needs a multidisciplinary team experienced in FOP:** paediatrics, internal medicine, cardiology, neurology, surgery, dentistry, anaesthesia, ENT, physiotherapy, gynaecology, dermatology and psychology. Patients can have important cardiopulmonary and neurological problems, including thoracic insufficiency syndrome, pulmonary hypertension and neuropathic pain. [S2]
- **First visit:** record the history, earlier medical and surgical care, the course so far and flare-ups; examine, and consider vital signs and lung function testing (from age 6). Note the aids and adaptations in use. [S2]
- **Track function over time** with the FOP Physical Function Questionnaire (FOP-PFQ, 36 items, a patient-reported measure) and regular joint status, for example with the FOP-specific CAJIS scale. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Limit imaging because of radiation:** outside studies, image only for acute symptoms with a clinical indication, localised where possible. Avoid whole-body imaging in routine care. [S2]
- **Review yearly;** closer checks at an experienced FOP centre may be needed when the disease is very active. [S2]
- **Local doctors can give glucocorticoids in an acute flare-up,** with telehealth consultation to link them with FOP specialists at the centres. [S2]
- **Starting a disease-modifying drug: monitor closely at a centre, especially at first,** at a frequency set by the disease course, the drug and its side effects. Once stable, repeat prescriptions can come from the centre or from local doctors on the centre's advice. [S2]

## Prevent injury and flare-ups

- ⚠️ **Avoid the triggers:** contact sport, soft-tissue and blunt muscle injury, fatigue and stretching, IM injections, biopsies, removing heterotopic bone, non-urgent surgery, and viral illness. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Never do passive range-of-motion exercises;** activity is otherwise encouraged, especially singing, water exercise and respiratory activities. [S1]
- **Surgery only in emergencies, and never to remove heterotopic bone.** [S1]
- **Vaccinate subcutaneously; never IM or with live intranasal influenza vaccine.** Not during a flare-up, and wait 8 weeks after one resolves. Immunise household contacts against influenza, pertussis and COVID-19. [S1]
- **Venepuncture and superficial IV access are fine if gentle, with brief tourniquet time;** avoid central lines, PICC lines and arterial punctures unless essential. [S1]

## Treat flare-ups

- ⚠️ **Limb, jaw or submandibular flare-up: prednisone 2 mg/kg once daily in the morning (maximum 100 mg) for 4 days, started as early as possible;** keep a supply at home. [S1]
- **Best within 24 h of onset; after 2 days prednisone works less well.** If a flare-up recurs on stopping, repeat the 4-day course then taper about 10% a day over 10 days. Long-term steroids help nothing and do harm. [S1]
- **Back or chest flare-ups:** NSAIDs or COX-2 inhibitors with careful gut protection, analgesics, muscle relaxants and ice; steroids work less well here. Avoid narcotic analgesia. [S1]
- **After severe soft-tissue injury, prednisone 1-2 mg/kg daily (maximum 100 mg) for 3-4 days may prevent a flare-up;** not for minor bumps. Also give it around dental or surgical procedures. [S1]
- **Palovarotene (an RAR-gamma agonist) is approved in Australia, the US, Canada, UAE and Russia, but not in Europe;** it is the only regulator-approved medicine, for chronic and episodic use. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Palovarotene dosing (S1 Table 1, from the US label), age 14 and over:** 5 mg once daily; for a flare-up, stop the daily dose and give 20 mg daily for 4 weeks then 10 mg daily for 8 weeks (12 weeks in all, even if symptoms settle sooner), then go back to 5 mg. Restart the 12-week course at 20 mg if the flare-up clearly worsens or a new one appears during it; if symptoms remain at 12 weeks, extend the 10 mg dose in 4-week blocks until they resolve. Under 14 (girls 8–13, boys 10–13) dosing is by weight. In the US, prescribers must first complete the Sohonos educational programme. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Palovarotene safety (S1 Table 1):** boxed warnings for teratogenicity and premature epiphyseal closure. In growing children, assess growth and skeletal maturity before starting, then monitor clinically and radiographically every 6-12 months until skeletal maturity or final height. Mucocutaneous effects (dry skin and lips, itch, rash, hair loss, redness, peeling, dry eye) are handled with emollients, sunscreen and artificial tears, sometimes with a dose cut. Also watch for reduced vertebral bone density (check periodically for vertebral fracture on imaging), depression, anxiety, mood change or suicidal thinking, and night blindness that makes night driving unsafe. [S1]
- **Class I (generally accepted) medicines (S1 Table 2):** prednisone or liquid prednisolone, ibuprofen, indomethacin and celecoxib. Start the steroid within 24 h of flare-up onset, but not for minor bumps and not for chest or back flare-ups; a longer tapering course may be used for submandibular flare-ups affecting breathing or swallowing. After severe soft-tissue injury the 3-4 day course may be repeated for 4 days and tapered over up to 2 weeks. Consider a bisphosphonate if frequent steroid courses threaten osteoporosis, and anti-parasite precautions in endemic regions. [S1]
- ⚠️ **IV corticosteroid protocol (S1 Table 2), inpatient only because of the risk of hypertension:** methylprednisolone 7-15 mg/kg or prednisolone sodium 20-30 mg/kg IV once daily on 3 consecutive days (some give it on alternate days for tolerability), with a total daily dose of no more than 1000 mg. [S1]
- **Class II (off-label) medicines (S1 Table 3):** montelukast, cromolyn, pamidronate, zoledronate, imatinib, tofacitinib and canakinumab. Pamidronate: 0.75 mg/kg/day for 3 days at 2-3 years, 1.0 mg/kg/day for 3 days above 3 years and in adults, each infusion given slowly over 4-5 h, half the dose on day 1 of the first cycle, no more than 4 cycles a year; check blood count, urea, creatinine, calcium, phosphate, albumin, alkaline phosphatase and 25-hydroxyvitamin D first, and give calcium and vitamin D during and indefinitely after. Tofacitinib 5 mg twice daily helped in one case series; canakinumab follows FMF dosing (under 40 kg, 2 mg/kg every 4 weeks, up to 4 mg/kg; over 40 kg, 150 mg every 4 weeks, maximum 300 mg) and cut flare activity by 61%-89% in a 4-patient uncontrolled series. [S1]
- **Class III (investigational only; S1 Table 4):** ACVR1/ALK2 signal-transduction inhibitors (saracatinib, fidrisertib, zilurgisertib; phase II), mTOR inhibitors such as sirolimus (phase II), garetosmab, an activin A antibody (phase III), and andecaliximab, an MMP-9 antibody (phase II/III). None has a dose outside trials. [S1]
- **No proven benefit on flare-ups from chronic NSAIDs/COX-2 inhibitors, bisphosphonates or off-label drugs (imatinib, tofacitinib, anakinra and others);** chemotherapy and radiotherapy are contraindicated. [S1]
- **S2 (German experts, following the ICC):** trunk and back flare-ups get an NSAID, and a topical NSAID can be added; neck, jaw and large-joint (e.g. hip) flare-ups get prednisone 2 mg/kg/day orally, up to 100 mg, for 4 days, started as early as possible, with a 4-day supply kept at home. [S2]
- **S2 also allows an IV glucocorticoid pulse (for example prednisolone 20–30 mg/kg/day) for 3 days, as an inpatient,** and advises against long-term glucocorticoids. [S2]
- **Physiotherapy need not always stop during a flare-up;** gentle anti-inflammatory measures such as cold therapy are possible. [S2]

## Emergencies

- ⚠️ **A submandibular flare-up is a medical emergency** (airway and swallowing): give IV methylprednisolone 80 mg or dexamethasone 15 mg at once, and monitor the airway. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Airway:** intubate only awake, nasotracheal and fibre-optic, by an experienced anaesthetist. Do not manipulate the neck, brace it after head injury, and avoid unmonitored oxygen. [S1]
- **Any head injury needs a CT, even without loss of consciousness:** stiff arms cannot break a fall, and epidural haematoma is common. [S1]
- **Suspected compartment syndrome: do not do a fasciotomy** (it worsens the flare-up); consider mannitol. [S1]

## Monitor

- **Lungs:** baseline lung function tests, echocardiogram, chest X-ray and oximetry after age 4; respiratory specialist review by the end of the second decade. [S1]
- **Screen hearing in childhood; prevent kidney stones (3-fold risk) with 1.5-2 L/day of fluid.** Pregnancy carries major risk and needs a high-risk centre. [S1]
