# Tuberous sclerosis complex — diagnosis, kidney surveillance and mTOR inhibitor therapy

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosis (genetic and clinical criteria), kidney involvement (angiomyolipoma, cysts, renal cell carcinoma, CKD, hypertension), surveillance imaging and mTOR inhibitor (everolimus) treatment for enlarging renal angiomyolipoma in tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC). Epilepsy is covered from S2 (edition 2.0); brain (SEGA), skin, lung and neuropsychiatric management are not covered. Related: `infantile-spasms` (vigabatrin first line in TSC), `epilepsy`, `autosomal-dominant-polycystic-kidney-disease` (TSC2/PKD1 contiguous gene syndrome), `renal-cell-carcinoma`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **TSC is a rare autosomal dominant disorder (1:6000–1:10,000 live births) from loss-of-function variants in TSC1 or TSC2,** causing benign tumours in the kidney, brain, skin, heart and lung. [S1]
- **Suspect TSC with prenatal cardiac rhabdomyoma, infantile spasms or epilepsy, and hypomelanotic skin macules in childhood;** each is non-specific alone. In infancy and childhood TSC most often presents with seizures. [S1]
- **Multifocal angiomyolipomas, especially with kidney cysts, should prompt a search for other TSC criteria.** [S1]

## Diagnose

- **A pathogenic TSC1 or TSC2 variant (ACMG standards) is enough for diagnosis.** Clinically, the 2021 criteria have 11 major and seven minor features; diagnosis needs two major, or one major plus two minor. [S1]
- **Offer genetic testing to people who meet the criteria** (for prognosis and family), though it is not required for diagnosis. The review's authors also test all first-degree relatives, which differs from one expert consensus. [S1]
- **Sequence blood first; if negative, test affected tissue,** because mosaicism is common. Next-generation sequencing finds a variant in >90%. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not order TSC gene tests without clinical suspicion:** thousands of rare variants occur in adults without TSC, so pathogenicity can be hard to call. [S1]

## Kidney surveillance

- ⚠️ **Kidney disease is the most common cause of death in adults with TSC.** [S1]
- **Abdominal MRI at diagnosis and every year,** dropping to every 2–3 years for small lesions (<1 cm) stable for ≥3 years. MRI is preferred over CT (radiation) and ultrasound (misses fat-poor lesions). Keep surveilling even with no lesions yet. [S1]
- **Check kidney function annually from adulthood;** children rarely develop CKD. [S1]
- **Blood pressure yearly;** 24-hour ambulatory monitoring for adolescents and adults with office BP ≥120/70. An ACE inhibitor or ARB is first line. [S1]

## Treat angiomyolipoma

- ⚠️ **Treat enlarging angiomyolipomas ≥3 cm pre-emptively,** because lesions ≥4 cm may form aneurysms that rupture. [S1]
- **Everolimus is advised for enlarging angiomyolipomas >3 cm at any age; sirolimus is a reasonable alternative.** Response is usually seen by 3 months, with maximal shrinkage by 6–12 months; safety and efficacy are shown for up to 4 years. [S1]
- **Evidence:** in EXIST-2 (118 adults), everolimus shrank angiomyolipomas ≥3 cm by ≥50% in 42% at 3 months and 55% at 6 months, versus 0% on placebo. In EXIST-1 it reduced SEGA volume by ≥50% in 35% versus 0%. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Acute haemorrhage (such as Wunderlich syndrome): mTOR inhibitors have no role; renal artery embolisation is preferred over nephrectomy.** [S1]
- **Monitor eGFR and proteinuria before starting mTOR inhibitors and every 3–12 months;** they can cause or worsen proteinuria. [S1]
- **Consider biopsy for angiomyolipomas growing >0.5 cm a year,** especially if they do not respond to mTOR inhibition, since fat-poor lesions can mimic renal cell carcinoma. [S1]

## Epilepsy: recognise and monitor

- **Epilepsy is the commonest neurological feature, in 80–90% of people with TSC;** most have seizures in the first year of life (epileptic spasms, focal, tonic, atonic or tonic-clonic, sometimes evolving towards Lennox–Gastaut syndrome), and about 5% start as newborns. Parents may miss subtle focal seizures in infants. [S2]
- **Drug resistance is common. Predictors:** focal seizures (with or without spasms) rather than spasms alone, early focal-onset seizures, infantile spasms that resist treatment, a TSC2 variant (with its higher tuber load), and focal or multifocal interictal discharges on serial EEG before any seizure. [S2]
- ⚠️ **EEG surveillance for every infant with a definite TSC diagnosis:** every 4 weeks for the first 6–8 months, every 4–8 weeks until 12 months, then 12-weekly from 12 to 24 months, to catch interictal discharges and background change early. Teach parents to spot subtle focal seizures, and assess development on standard scales at ages 6, 12, 18 and 24 months. [S2]

## Epilepsy: pre-symptomatic vigabatrin

- ⚠️ **Pre-symptomatic vigabatrin is now an established approach:** start it as soon as focal spikes appear, and use it in all infants once multifocal spikes, (pre-)hypsarrhythmia or subclinical seizures show on EEG. In EPISTOP (vigabatrin at least 100 mg/kg/day), preventive treatment delayed clinical seizures, lowered the risk of drug-resistant epilepsy and avoided infantile spasms, compared with starting after seizure onset. [S2]
- **Caveat from the published corrigendum:** in the PREVeNT trial (eighty-four infants randomised to vigabatrin or placebo once epileptiform discharges appeared), vigabatrin lowered and delayed epileptic spasms but did not change cognition at 24 months or drug-resistant epilepsy. The panel says PREVeNT and EPISTOP cannot be compared directly (different design, and far more TSC2 variants in the PREVeNT vigabatrin arm). [S2]
- **Vigabatrin and vision:** the US registry (more than 9000 patients, followed up to 16 years) found no clinical vision loss, and the 2% with new vision findings were not attributed to vigabatrin, so the retinal risk looks lower than once thought. [S2]

## Epilepsy: drug-resistant seizures

- ⚠️ **Add everolimus for drug-resistant TSC seizures:** in EXIST-3 it cut seizure frequency meaningfully with a favourable risk-benefit balance; response tends to grow with time, a post-authorisation study confirmed long-term safety and found better response under 6 years, and the recommended target concentration is 5–9 ng/ml. Consider it early when a growing SEGA or angiomyolipoma coexists with drug-resistant epilepsy. [S2]
- **Sirolimus:** uncontrolled data are favourable (at least 50% fewer seizures in 56% of 32 children with drug-resistant epilepsy), but only everolimus has long-term controlled data in TSC. [S2]
- **Consider presurgical evaluation for every patient with drug-resistant epilepsy whose seizure semiology is consistent and concordant with EEG and MRI,** at a centre expert in both TSC and epilepsy surgery. Surgery is underused: 50–60% become seizure-free and another 15% have over 50% fewer seizures, even with multiple tubers, though multiple seizure types, tubers and EEG abnormalities make seizure freedom unlikely. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Cannabidiol at 25–50 mg/kg/day reduced TSC seizures against placebo (224 patients),** with no efficacy difference between the doses and more diarrhoea, somnolence and raised transaminases at the higher one. **It raises mTOR inhibitor levels:** with everolimus, cut the everolimus dose by about half and check trough levels regularly. Ketogenic diet and vagus nerve stimulation can help refractory patients unsuitable for surgery; the panel does not recommend metformin on current evidence. [S2]
