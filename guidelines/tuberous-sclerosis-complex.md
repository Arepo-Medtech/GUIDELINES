# Tuberous sclerosis complex — diagnosis, kidney surveillance and mTOR inhibitor therapy

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosis (genetic and clinical criteria), kidney involvement (angiomyolipoma, cysts, renal cell carcinoma, CKD, hypertension), surveillance imaging and mTOR inhibitor (everolimus) treatment for enlarging renal angiomyolipoma in tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC). Brain (SEGA), epilepsy, skin, lung and neuropsychiatric management are not covered by the source. ## Recognise

- **TSC is a rare autosomal dominant disorder (1:6000–1:10,000 live births) from loss-of-function variants in TSC1 or TSC2,** causing benign tumours in the kidney, brain, skin, heart and lung. [S1]
- **Suspect TSC with prenatal cardiac rhabdomyoma, infantile spasms or epilepsy, and hypomelanotic skin macules in childhood;** each is non-specific alone. In infancy and childhood TSC most often presents with seizures. [S1]
- **Multifocal angiomyolipomas, especially with kidney cysts, should prompt a search for other TSC criteria.** [S1]

## Diagnose

- **A pathogenic TSC1 or TSC2 variant (ACMG standards) is enough for diagnosis.** Clinically, the 2021 criteria have 11 major and seven minor features; diagnosis needs two major, or one major plus two minor. [S1]
- **Offer genetic testing to people who meet the criteria** (for prognosis and family), though it is not required for diagnosis. The review's authors also test all first-degree relatives, which differs from one expert consensus. [S1]
- **Sequence blood first; if negative, test affected tissue,** because mosaicism is common. Next-generation sequencing finds a variant in >90%. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not order TSC gene tests without clinical suspicion:** thousands of rare variants occur in adults without TSC, so pathogenicity can be hard to call. [S1]

## Kidney surveillance

- ⚠️ **Kidney disease is the most common cause of death in adults with TSC.** [S1]
- **Abdominal MRI at diagnosis and every year,** dropping to every 2–3 years for small lesions (<1 cm) stable for ≥3 years. MRI is preferred over CT (radiation) and ultrasound (misses fat-poor lesions). Keep surveilling even with no lesions yet. [S1]
- **Check kidney function annually from adulthood;** children rarely develop CKD. [S1]
- **Blood pressure yearly;** 24-hour ambulatory monitoring for adolescents and adults with office BP ≥120/70. An ACE inhibitor or ARB is first line. [S1]

## Treat angiomyolipoma

- ⚠️ **Treat enlarging angiomyolipomas ≥3 cm pre-emptively,** because lesions ≥4 cm may form aneurysms that rupture. [S1]
- **Everolimus is advised for enlarging angiomyolipomas >3 cm at any age; sirolimus is a reasonable alternative.** Response is usually seen by 3 months, with maximal shrinkage by 6–12 months; safety and efficacy are shown for up to 4 years. [S1]
- **Evidence:** in EXIST-2 (118 adults), everolimus shrank angiomyolipomas ≥3 cm by ≥50% in 42% at 3 months and 55% at 6 months, versus 0% on placebo. In EXIST-1 it reduced SEGA volume by ≥50% in 35% versus 0%. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Acute haemorrhage (such as Wunderlich syndrome): mTOR inhibitors have no role; renal artery embolisation is preferred over nephrectomy.** [S1]
- **Monitor eGFR and proteinuria before starting mTOR inhibitors and every 3–12 months;** they can cause or worsen proteinuria. [S1]
- **Consider biopsy for angiomyolipomas growing >0.5 cm a year,** especially if they do not respond to mTOR inhibition, since fat-poor lesions can mimic renal cell carcinoma. [S1]
