# Type 1 diabetes in adults: glycaemic targets, insulin regimens, monitoring and hypoglycaemia

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** non-pregnant adults with type 1 diabetes: HbA1c, glucose and CGM targets, cardiovascular risk targets, glucose and ketone monitoring, choice of insulin regimen and delivery, injection technique, and recognising, treating and preventing hypoglycaemia, plus sick days and SGLT-inhibitor ketoacidosis risk. **Children and adolescents are not covered by this source:** see `diabetes-new-presentation-children`, `diabetes-sick-day-management-children`, `diabetes-unwell-in-hospital-children`, `diabetes-and-surgery-children`, `diabetic-ketoacidosis-children` and `hypoglycaemia-children`. Adult hypoglycaemia treatment across insulin and sulfonylurea users: `hypoglycaemia`. Type 2 diabetes: `type-2-diabetes`. No adult diabetic ketoacidosis guideline exists in this compendium.

## Targets

- **For most adults the HbA1c goal is below 53 mmol/mol (7.0%), as long as it is reached without significant hypoglycaemia.** Set the target for the person: diabetes duration, age and life expectancy, comorbidity, established cardiovascular disease or advanced microvascular disease, and impaired hypoglycaemia awareness all shift it, and it can change over time. [S1]
- **Going lower than 53 mmol/mol (7%) is acceptable, even beneficial, if it is safe; a looser goal such as under 64 mmol/mol (8.0%) suits limited life expectancy or when treatment harms outweigh benefits.** Any fall from a high starting HbA1c helps, even short of the goal. [S1]
- **Finger-prick targets:** before meals 4.4–7.2 mmol/l for many people; if HbA1c stays high despite that, target the peak after meals, measured 1–2 h after starting to eat, at under 10.0 mmol/l. [S1]
- **CGM targets for most adults: time in range (3.9–10 mmol/l) above 70%, time below 3.9 mmol/l under 4%, and time below 3.0 mmol/l under 1%.** For older people with long-standing diabetes the main target is time below range under 1%. A 70% time in range equates approximately to HbA1c 53 mmol/mol (7.0%). [S1]
- **Blood pressure: aim for under 140/90 mmHg at lower cardiovascular risk (10-year risk under 15%), and under 130/80 mmHg at higher risk or with microvascular complications, especially kidney disease.** An ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker is first-line. [S1]
- **Consider a moderate-intensity statin from age 40, and at 20–39 years when there are other atherosclerotic risk factors or a 10-year cardiovascular risk above 10%** on a calculator suited to type 1 diabetes. Consider aspirin or another antiplatelet agent for everyone with established cardiovascular disease. [S1]

## Monitoring

- **Review glucose with the care team as often as needed and at least once a year; check glycaemic status at least every 3 months after a therapy change or when goals are not being met.** [S1]
- **Continuous glucose monitoring is now the standard for most adults,** but everyone should still have a finger-prick meter, for when readings seem not to match symptoms. HbA1c alone is not enough, because it shows neither variability nor hypoglycaemia. [S1]
- **Offer blood ketone strips and a meter.** Urine strips miss β-hydroxybutyrate, so they can understate ketosis, and they may stay positive for 48 h after ketoacidosis has resolved. [S1]

## Insulin therapy

- **Use a regimen that mimics normal physiology:** multiple daily injections of a basal analogue plus a rapid-acting (or ultra-rapid) mealtime analogue, or a pump infusing a rapid-acting analogue with manual meal boluses. Analogues are the insulins of choice, because newer basal analogues cause less hypoglycaemia than older basal analogues and NPH, and rapid-acting analogues cover meals better than regular human insulin. [S1]
- **First-generation basal analogues and NPH are often given once daily, but twice-daily dosing may cover basal needs better and add flexibility.** [S1]
- **Hybrid closed-loop systems (pump, CGM and a control algorithm) are the most effective way to keep glucose in range;** adding CGM to injections or a pump improves HbA1c, time in range and time below range. [S1]
- **When analogues, pumps or CGM are unaffordable or unwanted,** regular human insulin with NPH, or premixed insulin, plus finger-prick checks as often as feasible, is an option, at the price of more glucose variability, more hypoglycaemia and less flexibility. [S1]
- **Teach injection technique at the start and repeat it:** 4 mm needles at 90° reach the subcutaneous layer in most adults; rotate sites, because lipohypertrophy from reusing sites drives up doses and causes erratic glucose. Examine injection and pump sites at least once a year. [S1]
- **Ask about weight worries:** insulin causes weight gain and some people cut their doses to avoid it. [S1]

## Hypoglycaemia

- **Three levels:** Level 1 is glucose below 3.9 but at least 3.0 mmol/l (an alert value); Level 2 is below 3.0 mmol/l (clinically important); Level 3 is any episode with altered mental or physical state that needs another person's help to recover. Prevent Levels 2 and 3 above all. [S1]
- **Risk factors for severe hypoglycaemia** include long diabetes duration, older age, a recent Level 3 episode, alcohol, exercise, chronic kidney disease and impaired awareness; hypothyroidism, adrenal or growth hormone deficiency and coeliac disease can also precipitate it. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Screen for impaired awareness at visits:** ask whether symptoms usually start at 3.0 mmol/l or above, below it, or not at all. People who feel them only below 3.0 mmol/l, or never, have a more than 4-fold higher risk of Level 3 hypoglycaemia; impaired awareness overall raises that risk sixfold and affects about 25%. [S1]
- **Treat glucose below 3.9 mmol/l with about 15 g of glucose or other simple carbohydrate by mouth; repeat every 15 min until symptoms settle and glucose is above 3.9 mmol/l.** More may be needed below 3.0 mmol/l. Confirm recovery with a finger-prick test, because CGM lags capillary glucose by 5–15 min. Once recovered, give about 20 g of longer-acting carbohydrate and look for the cause. [S1]
- **On automated insulin delivery, less carbohydrate (5–15 g) may be enough,** because the system will already have cut or stopped basal insulin. [S1]
- ⚠️ **If consciousness is reduced, give nothing by mouth (aspiration risk).** Bystanders give glucagon by subcutaneous or intramuscular injection or nasally; health professionals can give intravenous glucose for Level 3 events. [S1]
- **Prevent it with structured education in insulin self-adjustment (e.g. DAFNE, blood glucose awareness training), analogue regimens and hybrid closed-loop systems.** Strictly avoiding hypoglycaemia can restore awareness; sometimes the glucose target must be raised. [S1]

## Sick days and ketoacidosis

- **Agree a written sick-day plan in advance:** during illness, check glucose and ketones more often, keep up fluids and carbohydrate, adjust insulin, and know when to seek urgent care. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ketoacidosis can occur with near-normal glucose.** It follows absolute insulin lack (new diagnosis, missed insulin) or relative lack (infection or stress without extra insulin). Modifiable risks include a previous episode, high HbA1c, missed insulin, psychiatric illness, infection and alcohol or drug misuse. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Adjunct SGLT inhibitors raise ketoacidosis risk by about 4% a year in absolute terms, often euglycaemic.** The FDA refused them for type 1 diabetes; the EMA licensed dapagliflozin 5 mg or sotagliflozin 200 mg only when BMI was 27 kg/m2 or more. If used: careful selection, avoid cutting insulin too far, start low, and check ketones regularly. [S1]
