# Functional somatic symptoms in children

> **Diagnose FSS positively, not by exclusion.** Explain it early and clearly, keep investigations to a minimum, and put function (especially an **early return to school**) first.

---

## Understand it

- **FSS are real physical symptoms** caused by a change in how the nervous system and brain–body interaction function, not by structural disease. **They are not deliberate, imagined or exaggerated, and not under conscious control.** [S1]
- **Medical conditions and FSS can co-exist**; illness, injury, stress or developmental factors may trigger or worsen FSS. [S1]
- **Not malingering or factitious disorder.** Psychological and social factors may contribute but **are not required for the diagnosis.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Unnecessary investigations and fragmented care can worsen symptoms** and functional impairment. [S1]
- **Neurodivergent children may need early specialist input**: overlapping symptoms, sensory sensitivities and communication differences can change presentation and management. [S1]

## Assess

- **Approach:** red-flag screen → look for positive features → keep investigations minimal → explain the diagnosis → functional rehabilitation plan. [S1]
- **History:** symptom pattern and triggers; psychosocial assessment (children and families) or **HEADSSS** (adolescents); functional impact on school, social life, activity and mobility; medications; past and family history of FSS; development. [S1]
- **Examine thoroughly** to confirm FSS and to exclude reversible or serious organic contributors. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Red flags:** persistent consistent focal deficits · progressive symptoms without fluctuation · weight loss, growth failure or persistent fever · symptoms waking the child at night · **syncope with exertion or cardiac symptoms** · reproducible, congruent abnormal neurology · new seizures concerning for epilepsy. [S1]
- **Red flags do not exclude FSS**; they may point to co-existing pathology. [S1]
- **Positive features:** fluctuation with context, attention or stress · inconsistency with recognised disease patterns · variable examination · **improvement with distraction** · impairment out of proportion to findings · co-existing fatigue, pain, sleep or autonomic symptoms · hyperventilation · repeated low-yield presentations. [S1]
- **Make the diagnosis positive, proportionate and collaborative**; it does not require exhaustive exclusion of all organic disease. [S1]
- **Refer early for multidisciplinary (including mental health) assessment** if symptoms need admission, cause significant sustained impairment such as school non-attendance, the team disagrees on the diagnosis, there is social complexity or risk (e.g. family violence), or there is concern that symptoms are intentionally produced. [S1]

## Investigations

- **No test confirms FSS. Investigations are generally not required**, and should stop once a functional diagnosis is made unless new symptoms or red flags emerge. [S1]
- **If done, make them targeted and justified.** Investigations can reinforce illness beliefs, raise anxiety, delay recovery and cause iatrogenic harm. [S1]
- **Document and share results with every clinician involved**; agreeing which results would prompt reassessment helps. [S1]

## Explain

- **A clear, confident explanation is a core treatment:** name the diagnosis as FSS, explain it as nervous-system functioning rather than damage, reassure that symptoms are real and potentially reversible, and set out a recovery plan. [S1]
- **Raise a suspected functional cause early**, as part of the working diagnosis; delaying it while awaiting exclusion of organic causes can be counterproductive. [S1]
- **Many children need no multidisciplinary assessment**: acknowledge the symptoms, give a mind–body explanation that makes sense to the family, and focus on returning to usual activities despite symptoms, reassuring that this will not be harmful. [S1]
- ⚠️ **If a carer is thought to be intentionally producing the symptoms**, refer urgently to a forensic service and consider mandatory reporting. [S1]

## Rehabilitate

- **Treatment is mainly about improving function, and getting back to school early is a priority**, with graded, paced activity that avoids 'boom-bust' cycles. [S1]
- **Symptom improvement often follows functional improvement**, not the other way round. Continuity of care avoids significant iatrogenic harm. [S1]
- **Refer early to allied health** (physiotherapy for power or gait, OT for function, social work for supports) and, if the family agrees, to mental health services (e.g. CBT, family therapy). **Liaise with the school**, with letters or an individual learning plan if needed. [S1]
- **Medicines for symptom relief, such as analgesia, usually do not work** and should be kept to a minimum. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Avoid:** repeated re-investigation · conflicting explanations · telling families 'nothing is wrong' · blaming symptoms solely on anxiety or stress · holding back the diagnosis until every test is done. [S1]

## Consult and discharge

- **Consult the local paediatric team** for recurrent or persistent symptoms, or red flags suggesting undiagnosed pathology. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consult CAMHS** for complex or severe symptoms, co-existing mental health problems needing acute management beyond the clinician's comfort, or **significant risk to the child or others.** [S1]
- **Discharge once follow-up is arranged** — GP, or community rehabilitation support such as a physiotherapist or psychologist. For older adolescents, plan a smooth handover to adult services. [S1]
