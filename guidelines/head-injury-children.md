# Head injury in children

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** severity assessment, neuroimaging decisions, observation, severe head injury care and disposition for head injury in children. Seizure drug doses are in `seizures-acute-management-children`; bleeding-risk children overlap `immune-thrombocytopenia-children`.

> ⚠️ **Plain skull X-ray or head ultrasound is not a substitute for CT.** In moderate or severe head injury: urgent CT of head and early specialist or neurosurgical consultation.

> ⚠️ **Consider non-accidental injury, especially in infants.** Non-verbal children, particularly under 6 months, need a more cautious assessment and may need longer observation.

## Recognise and assess

- **Sort every child into one of four groups:** moderate–severe injury needing immediate management, investigation and referral · mild injury fit for immediate discharge · injury that needs observation and/or neuroimaging · suspected child abuse or other significant injuries. [S1]
- **Most head injuries are mild**, but an injury can still be significant without loss of consciousness. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider non-accidental injury, especially in infants.** Inflicted head injury is more likely in infants and in children who are non-verbal or non-ambulant; non-verbal children, particularly under 6 months, need a more cautious assessment and may need longer observation. [S1]
- **Suspected drug or alcohol intoxication: assume the conscious level is due to the injury** and keep a lower threshold for referral and neuroimaging. **Assess the cervical spine at the same time.** [S1]
- **Risk factors:** loss of consciousness · post-traumatic seizure · persistent vomiting · severe headache · abnormal neurology · persistent altered mental status or acting abnormally · non-frontal scalp haematoma in a child under 2 years · severe mechanism · palpable skull fracture, or signs of a base-of-skull fracture · suspected child abuse · VP shunt · bleeding disorder or anticoagulation · neurodevelopmental disability. [S1]
- **Severe mechanism** = vehicle accident with ejection, rollover or a passenger death · pedestrian or cyclist struck by a vehicle · fall of 1 m or more under 2 years, or over 1.5 m over 2 years · head struck by a high-impact object. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Raised intracranial pressure — look for:** pupil dilatation (one or both sides) · abnormal posturing · new focal neurological signs · a fall in GCS of more than 2 points. **The Cushing reflex (bradycardia, hypertension, irregular breathing) is a late sign.** [S1]
- **Feel for a palpable skull fracture and look for base of skull fracture:** haemotympanum, CSF otorrhoea or rhinorrhoea, raccoon eyes, Battle sign. [S1]
- **Severity can change: reassess every observed child regularly** for signs or symptoms of deterioration. [S1]

## Neuroimaging

- **Imaging balances clinical benefit against radiation and sedation risk; discuss it with a senior doctor or neurosurgeon.** In mild head injury, whether to image depends on which risk factors are present, and indications may emerge while the child is observed. [S1]
- **Give greater consideration to imaging for:** age under 6 months · bleeding disorder, anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy · immune thrombocytopenia · VP shunt · neurodevelopmental disorders · drug or alcohol intoxication. With no other risk factors, structured observation may be preferred to imaging straight away, with senior, haematology or subspecialty advice. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not use plain skull X-ray or head ultrasound instead of CT.** MRI may be equivalent but only where it can be done quickly and safely. [S1]
- **Look for the cause of a fall**, e.g. alcohol, other ingestions, arrhythmias, hypoglycaemia. [S1]

## Treat: mild and moderate

- **Mild, no risk factors** (GCS 15, no concern for abusive trauma, over 6 months, no special conditions, non-severe mechanism): **may go home with parents** once back to a normal conscious state and acting normally, with no other concerns. [S1]
- **Mild, with risk factors: observe for up to 4 hours after injury**, with neuro obs (conscious state, PR, RR, BP, pupils, limb power) **every 30 minutes for the first 2 hours, then hourly.** Simple analgesia; consider anti-emetics. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Persistent headache, ongoing vomiting, GCS 14 or persistent altered mental status needs longer observation and probably investigation** — talk to a senior clinician. **Discharge once normal conscious state for at least an hour**, acting normally and tolerating oral fluids. [S1]
- **Moderate head injury: urgent CT of head** (consider c-spine imaging), senior or neurosurgical advice and early specialist consultation, **with a low threshold to escalate as for severe injury.** [S1]

## Treat: severe head injury

- ⚠️ **The aim is to prevent secondary brain damage:** maintain oxygenation, ventilation and circulation, and avoid rises in ICP. **Urgent CT of head and early neurosurgical consultation.** [S1]
- **Consider intubation if** GCS persistently under 8 · unresponsive, or no purposeful response to pain · protective laryngeal reflexes lost · breathing irregular or hypoventilation suspected. **While intubating, prevent hypoxia, hypotension and hypoventilation**, and keep c-spine movement to a minimum. [S1]
- **Ventilated children: titrate morphine and midazolam carefully** — opioid sensitivity is often increased after head injury. Muscle paralysis may be considered (e.g. pancuronium or vecuronium). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Uncorrected hypotension is a significant cause of secondary brain damage.** Maintain blood pressure with isotonic crystalloid (e.g. 0.9% sodium chloride) or inotropes if necessary. [S1]
- **Raised ICP, with the neurosurgical team:** nurse 30° head-up (after correcting shock) with the head midline; **ventilate to PaCO2 35–40 mmHg.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider IV hypertonic saline — 3 mL/kg of 3% sodium chloride across 10–20 min — or IV 20% mannitol 0.25–0.5 g/kg across 20–30 min.** [S1]
- **Seizures: benzodiazepines immediately**, early neurosurgical advice, then a **phenytoin or levetiracetam loading dose**; watch for hypotension or hypoventilation afterwards. [S1]
- **Keep sodium and glucose normal, maintain normothermia, and check for coagulopathy.** [S1]

## Disposition and follow-up

- **Consult the paediatric or paediatric neurosurgical team for:** not back to normal within 4 hours · uncertainty about imaging · suspected child abuse (also child protection and forensic service) · any ventricular shunt · any bleeding disorder, anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy (discuss with a paediatric haematologist). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider tertiary transfer for:** all severe head injuries · suspected inflicted injury · CSF leak · definite or suspected penetrating injury · focal neurological signs · incomplete recovery after a seizure · falling conscious level (especially motor changes). [S1]
- **Discharge when** the parent says the child has been acting normally for at least an hour, neurological examination is normal and fluids are tolerated — and nothing else warrants admission (other injuries, medical concerns, intoxication, social factors, possible abusive injury). [S1]
- **Concussion causes a temporary change in brain function**; symptoms after concussion are common, so advise rest and a gradual return to activity. [S1]
- **GP follow-up within 1–2 weeks** after mild to moderate injury for post-concussive symptoms. **Recovery from anything beyond a trivial injury can take up to 4 weeks**; graded return to activity. [S1]
