# Tumour-induced osteomalacia: recognition, diagnosis and management

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation

## Recognise

- **TIO is a rare paraneoplastic syndrome:** mesenchymal tumours secrete FGF23, causing progressive bone pain, muscle weakness and fragility fractures. [S1]
- ⚠️ **It is usually missed at first:** the initial misdiagnosis rate is 95.1%, and a delay of 2.5–3.5 years to tumour treatment has been reported (over 2 years in 80% of cases). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Measure serum phosphate in anyone with chronic muscle pain or weakness, fragility fractures or bone pain.** It is not in standard chemistry panels, so it must be requested. [S1]
- **Examine for myopathy and palpate the whole body for masses,** which are often very small. [S1]

## Biochemical work-up

- **Take serum phosphate fasting in the morning and use an age-specific range.** In adults hypophosphataemia is below 0.8 mmol/L (2.5 mg/dL); chronic hypophosphataemia needs two low values at least 2 weeks apart. [S1]
- **Full panel:** blood count, fasting phosphate, creatinine, calcium, albumin and total ALP, plus PTH, 25(OH)D, 1,25(OH)2D and FGF23. [S1]
- **Confirm renal phosphate wasting:** collect fasting serum and second-morning-void urine phosphate and creatinine together, and calculate TRP and TmP/GFR. A low TmP/GFR or %TRP raises suspicion of TIO. [S1]
- **Vitamin D deficiency does not rule TIO out.** Hypophosphataemia that persists after vitamin D is corrected should prompt consideration of TIO. [S1]
- **1,25(OH)2D is typically low or inappropriately normal.** Interpret FGF23 with caution (assays are not standardised); a low or low-normal FGF23 points away from TIO. [S1]

## Refer, test genes, localise

- **Refer once renal phosphate wasting and TIO are suspected** to a metabolic bone specialist or endocrinologist at a centre experienced in TIO. [S1]
- **Genetic testing:** ideally in all children and young adults suspected of TIO, and strongly considered in adults with renal phosphate wasting, especially when no tumour is found. [S1]
- **Localise with functional imaging first** (somatostatin-receptor imaging such as 68Ga-DOTATATE, or FDG-PET, usually whole-body), then MRI or CT to pinpoint the lesion and plan surgery. [S1]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **Complete resection is curative and preferred:** plan a wide excision with negative margins. Surgery succeeded in over 90% of patients whose tumour was found. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Pre-operative biopsy is a warning sign:** in one Italian study, every patient biopsied before surgery had a recurrence. [S1]
- **Radiofrequency ablation or cryoablation** can be used when a tumour is hard to resect completely; success varies and long-term outcomes are unknown. [S1]
- **Start medical therapy as soon as the biochemical diagnosis is made,** to limit hungry bone syndrome and prepare the skeleton for surgery. [S1]
- **Conventional therapy is phosphate salts plus active vitamin D (calcitriol or alfacalcidol).** ⚠️ Never give phosphate alone: it drives hyperparathyroidism and works poorly. [S1]
- **Conventional therapy is reasonable while searching for the tumour or awaiting surgery.** For severe symptoms, an unresectable tumour or a non-localisable tumour needing long-term treatment, burosumab may be advantageous. [S1]
- **Burosumab (anti-FGF23 antibody) restored phosphate and improved osteomalacia, pain and mobility in phase 2 studies,** but neither it nor conventional therapy halts tumour growth. FGF23 levels cannot be interpreted during burosumab treatment. [S1]

## Follow-up

- **After resection, watch for hungry bone syndrome** and treat it with calcium and vitamin D. Phosphate usually normalises within 5 days; measure it frequently until normal, then annually. [S1]
- **Recurrence can come up to 20 years later.** For persistent or recurrent TIO, repeat the full biochemical and imaging work-up to find metastases or multiple foci. [S1]
