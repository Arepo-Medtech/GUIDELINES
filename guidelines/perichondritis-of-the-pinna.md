# Perichondritis of the pinna (auricular perichondritis)

> ⚠️ **Australian divergence.** The Australian counterpart (`otitis-externa-and-ear-wax`) says perichondritis of the pinna generally needs hospital admission and IV antibacterials. This source would allow topical or oral treatment for well patients with no abscess and no comorbidity. On the PBS, oral ciprofloxacin for this condition is an authority listing.

## Recognise

- **Auricular perichondritis is inflammation of the perichondrium of the ear, most often an infection, usually with *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.** Organisms get in through small breaks in the skin (injury, insect bites, self-injury) or bigger insults such as piercing or burns. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Untreated, it can deform the ear permanently ('cauliflower ear') and spread to deeper tissues,** so diagnosis and treatment should be prompt. [S1]
- **Cartilage ('high') piercing is a common trigger.** Infection afterwards is not rare, because most piercers are not health professionals and may not know or follow antiseptic practice. [S1]
- **Two patterns.** Piercing-related cases were much younger (mean age 25 against 51 years). Cases without a piercing followed herpes zoster, recent ear surgery such as tympanoplasty, allergic skin reactions, trauma or other skin breaks. [S1]
- **Cases without a piercing more often had comorbidities (diabetes, smoking, immunosuppression), involved the ear diffusely, and caused fever or enlarged nodes.** Piercing cases were more local and formed an abscess more often (28.3% against 13.4%). [S1]
- **Diabetes may delay presentation,** perhaps because of reduced sensation. The authors suggest this delay, together with comorbidity, explains why cases without a piercing are more often complicated. [S1]

## Assess

- **Of cultured cases, *P. aeruginosa* and *Staphylococcus aureus* were the main organisms.** *P. aeruginosa* was more frequent after piercing (52.4% against 20.6%). Other organisms, including *Enterobacter*, *Morganella*, *Aspergillus* and *Actinomyces*, were much more frequent without a piercing. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Culture before starting systemic antibiotics in every new case, piercing-related or not.** Two reasons: cases without a piercing often involve organisms other than Pseudomonas, and fluoroquinolone resistance is rising. [S1]
- ⚠️ **An abscess is the strongest warning sign.** It was the only independent predictor of complications (odds ratio 35.75 across the whole cohort). Complications affected 29.5% of patients overall. [S1]

## Treat

- **Usual empirical treatment is ciprofloxacin, with incision and drainage of any abscess under the perichondrium;** necrotic cartilage often has to be debrided carefully as well. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Abscess: incise and drain and give intravenous antibiotics from the start,** whether or not there is fever or lymphadenopathy. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give antibiotics intravenously as well when the patient has diabetes, is immunosuppressed or smokes heavily.** [S1]
- **With no comorbidity, no abscess and no systemic features,** the authors consider topical treatment, alone or with an oral antibiotic, a safe way to start. [S1]

## Monitor

- **Expect a real chance of treatment failure:** 18.3% needed at least one change of regimen for poor response and 29.5% needed further drainage in hospital. Mean hospital stay was 5.3 days and mean total treatment 11.1 days. [S1]
- **Recent ear surgery was associated with a complicated course,** and patients with complications had been admitted later than those without (a mean of 12.2 against 4.7 days). [S1]
