# Perichondritis of the pinna (auricular perichondritis)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with infective perichondritis of the ear (pinna), with or without a cartilage piercing: recognition, cultures, choosing oral or intravenous antibiotics, drainage and debridement. Otitis externa, including necrotising (malignant) otitis externa, is in `otitis-externa-and-ear-wax`; children's skin infections are in `cellulitis-and-skin-infections-children`. > ⚠️ International source (The Laryngoscope, 2025). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **This is not a guideline.** No society guideline on auricular perichondritis was found. S1 is a retrospective study of 217 adults from one tertiary centre, and its treatment algorithm is proposed by the authors from limited literature. The StatPearls review was behind a bot challenge and was not used.

> ⚠️ **S2 is also a single-centre retrospective study, not a guideline:** 127 inpatients with refractory perichondritis (most had already had outpatient antibiotics) at one tertiary ENT hospital in eastern China, 2013–2022. Its organism and susceptibility figures are local and hospital-based.

> ⚠️ **Australian divergence.** The Australian counterpart (`otitis-externa-and-ear-wax`) says perichondritis of the pinna generally needs hospital admission and IV antibacterials. This source would allow topical or oral treatment for well patients with no abscess and no comorbidity. On the PBS, oral ciprofloxacin for this condition is an authority listing.

## Recognise

- **Auricular perichondritis is inflammation of the perichondrium of the ear, most often an infection, usually with *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.** Organisms get in through small breaks in the skin (injury, insect bites, self-injury) or bigger insults such as piercing or burns. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Untreated, it can deform the ear permanently ('cauliflower ear') and spread to deeper tissues,** so diagnosis and treatment should be prompt. [S1]
- **Cartilage ('high') piercing is a common trigger.** Infection afterwards is not rare, because most piercers are not health professionals and may not know or follow antiseptic practice. [S1]
- **Two patterns.** Piercing-related cases were much younger (mean age 25 against 51 years). Cases without a piercing followed herpes zoster, recent ear surgery such as tympanoplasty, allergic skin reactions, trauma or other skin breaks. [S1]
- **Cases without a piercing more often had comorbidities (diabetes, smoking, immunosuppression), involved the ear diffusely, and caused fever or enlarged nodes.** Piercing cases were more local and formed an abscess more often (28.3% against 13.4%). [S1]
- **Diabetes may delay presentation,** perhaps because of reduced sensation. The authors suggest this delay, together with comorbidity, explains why cases without a piercing are more often complicated. [S1]
- **Why it damages the ear:** the cartilage has no blood vessels of its own and is fed by the perichondrium, so infection there can starve it, leading to necrosis and liquefaction. The disease moves fast, and poor management risks deep soft-tissue or systemic infection. [S2]
- **In a hospital cohort the leading cause was ear surgery, not piercing:** 37.8% followed aural surgery (29.1% tympanoplasty) and 18.1% trauma, of which only six were piercings and five frostbite. No cause was found in 37.0%. [S2]

## Assess

- **Of cultured cases, *P. aeruginosa* and *Staphylococcus aureus* were the main organisms.** *P. aeruginosa* was more frequent after piercing (52.4% against 20.6%). Other organisms, including *Enterobacter*, *Morganella*, *Aspergillus* and *Actinomyces*, were much more frequent without a piercing. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Culture before starting systemic antibiotics in every new case, piercing-related or not.** Two reasons: cases without a piercing often involve organisms other than Pseudomonas, and fluoroquinolone resistance is rising. [S1]
- ⚠️ **An abscess is the strongest warning sign.** It was the only independent predictor of complications (odds ratio 35.75 across the whole cohort). Complications affected 29.5% of patients overall. [S1]
- **Cultures are often negative once antibiotics have started:** swabs were taken in 73.2% and only 59.1% grew anything, which the authors put down to prior outpatient antibiotics and the little discharge these ears produce. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Ask the laboratory for fungal culture, especially after ear surgery.** Fungi made up 23.0% of isolates (*Aspergillus* 18.0%); fungal infection was found in 41.7% of post-surgical cases against 7.1% after trauma, and went with immune-related comorbidity and longer admissions. [S2]

## Organisms and antibiotic susceptibility (S2, one Chinese centre)

- ***P. aeruginosa* was the commonest isolate (49.2%), and in piercing-related cases it was the organism every time (100%).** Other series report from 20.4% (Denmark) to 60.6% (India). [S2]
- **Gram-negative isolates were almost all susceptible to amikacin (100%), cefepime (93.9%), meropenem (97.1%) and ceftazidime (90.9%),** but only 67.4% to levofloxacin and 65.2% to ciprofloxacin. [S2]
- **Gram-positive isolates were all susceptible to vancomycin (100%),** but few to penicillin G (30.8%), ceftriaxone (20.0%), levofloxacin (41.7%), ciprofloxacin (30.8%) or erythromycin (15.4%). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Be cautious with a fluoroquinolone as the first empirical choice.** Pseudomonas susceptibility to fluoroquinolones fell over the decade, and multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas appeared (22.2% of Pseudomonas cases in 2018–2022, none in 2013–2017), all resistant to gentamicin, ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin. [S2]
- **Gram-positive organisms fell from 34.6% to 11.4% of isolates between 2013–2017 and 2018–2022,** while gram-negative and fungal isolates rose; the authors link this to wide outpatient use of amoxicillin-clavulanate and first- and second-generation cephalosporins. [S2]

## Treat

- **Usual empirical treatment is ciprofloxacin, with incision and drainage of any abscess under the perichondrium;** necrotic cartilage often has to be debrided carefully as well. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Abscess: incise and drain and give intravenous antibiotics from the start,** whether or not there is fever or lymphadenopathy. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give antibiotics intravenously as well when the patient has diabetes, is immunosuppressed or smokes heavily.** [S1]
- **With no comorbidity, no abscess and no systemic features,** the authors consider topical treatment, alone or with an oral antibiotic, a safe way to start. [S1]
- **In hospital, start empirical cover against *Pseudomonas*.** In S2, 78.7% got an anti-pseudomonal first regimen, most often ceftazidime alone (47.2%); the best option for people allergic to cephalosporins is still unknown. [S2]

## Monitor

- **Expect a real chance of treatment failure:** 18.3% needed at least one change of regimen for poor response and 29.5% needed further drainage in hospital. Mean hospital stay was 5.3 days and mean total treatment 11.1 days. [S1]
- **Recent ear surgery was associated with a complicated course,** and patients with complications had been admitted later than those without (a mean of 12.2 against 4.7 days). [S1]
- **Refractory disease responds slowly:** in S2 only 62.2% had fully settled by discharge, and six of the 71 followed up had a recurrence within 30 days. [S2]
