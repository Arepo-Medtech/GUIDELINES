# Disorders of keratinisation: Darier disease and oral retinoids

**Edition:** 2.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** Darier disease (dyskeratosis follicularis), the disorder of keratinisation with a current open consensus: recognition, triggers and infection, skin care and topical treatment, and oral retinoids (acitretin, isotretinoin, alitretinoin), the drug class the PBS lists for this condition. The congenital ichthyoses, pityriasis rubra pilaris and the keratodermas are not covered by this source. Edition 2.0 adds topical and oral retinoid use in the ichthyoses and other disorders of cornification in children and adolescents (S2). Psoriasis (acitretin's commoner use): see `psoriasis`; general topical principles: `topical-treatment-of-skin-conditions`.

## Recognise

- **Darier disease is a rare autosomal dominant genodermatosis** from ATP2A2 variants (the SERCA2 calcium pump). It usually starts after puberty, with triggers and variable severity. [S1]
- **Typical signs:** symmetrical crusted keratotic papules in seborrhoeic areas, which can merge into thick, fissured, smelly plaques in the folds; palmar pits, wart-like papules on the hands and feet, and nail changes. [S1]
- **Diagnosis is mainly clinical,** backed by family history, histology or cytology and, where possible, ATP2A2 testing. Offer genetic counselling to the patient and relatives at risk. [S1]
- **Flares follow heat, sweating, friction, UV, stress, hormonal change, surgery, infection or vaccination,** and drugs such as lithium, oral contraceptives, calcium-channel blockers, azathioprine and interferon. [S1]
- **Late or sudden onset should prompt a search for a paraneoplastic cause, HIV infection, recent major surgery or immunosuppression.** Early clues are nail changes and wart-like papules on the backs of the hands and feet. Examine the whole skin with the mouth, eyes and genitals, and the nails for red or white longitudinal bands, a V-shaped notch at the free edge and subungual hyperkeratosis. [S1]
- **The main differential is Hailey–Hailey disease:** painful, malodorous, erosive lesions in the folds, white nail bands in more than 70% but no red lines or fragile nails, earlier onset and less itch; histology separates the two. Others are acrokeratosis verruciformis of Hopf (an allelic ATP2A2 disorder on the backs of the hands and feet) and Grover disease, a transient acquired eruption mostly of middle-aged and older men. [S1]
- **For suspected mosaic disease, sequence ATP2A2 in both lesional skin and blood.** A negative test does not exclude Darier disease: unusual variants can be missed, and some mild, atypical, sporadic or mosaic cases never reach a molecular diagnosis. [S1]

## Red flags

- ⚠️ **Suspect secondary infection** (Staphylococcus aureus, Candida, HSV and others) when lesions worsen fast, stop responding, become painful, or show vesiculopustules, confluent erosions or white exudate, or with fever, malaise or nodes. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Disseminated infection can become Kaposi varicelliform eruption, erythroderma or sepsis,** and occasionally fatal multi-organ failure. [S1]
- **Keratinocyte cancers (squamous and basal cell) arise more often on Darier lesions,** so check persistent or changing lesions. [S1]
- ⚠️ **When infection is suspected, take swabs, PCR or cultures and start empirical antiviral or antibacterial treatment straight away; admit Kaposi varicelliform eruption to hospital at once.** With recurrent infections, screen the patient and family members for S. aureus carriage on skin and in the nose, and always exclude active infection before immunosuppressants or systemic steroids. [S1]

## Skin care and topical treatment

- **First-line care is trigger avoidance, antiseptics, emollients and topical anti-inflammatories;** systemic treatment is for moderate-to-severe or refractory disease. [S1]
- **Use octenidine or chlorhexidine antiseptics regularly** to lower colonisation and secondary infection. [S1]
- **Treat localised bacterial infection with a short course of fusidic acid 2% cream;** avoid prolonged or repeated topical antibiotics because of resistance. [S1]
- **Topical retinoids help:** adapalene 0.1% once daily for 2 months reduced lesions and was well tolerated. Sun protection, avoiding temperature extremes and emollients reduce early irritation. [S1]
- **For flares, moderate-to-potent topical corticosteroids are often needed.** Tacrolimus 0.1% ointment (then 0.03% maintenance) or pimecrolimus is preferred in skin folds. [S1]
- **Topical corticosteroid classes (S1's treatment table):** class II–III for temporary use, and class IV only briefly for acute flares, each matched to the body site and situation. [S1]

## Oral retinoids

- ⚠️ **Oral retinoids are the first-line systemic treatment for Darier disease** and have transformed care of keratinisation disorders, but they are highly teratogenic: post-menarchal females need a strict pregnancy prevention programme. [S1]
- **Acitretin works, but the benefit lasts only while it is taken.** Safety data from congenital ichthyosis are largely reassuring. Watch for haemorrhagic blisters on the hands and feet after minor trauma, a Darier-specific effect. [S1]
- **Isotretinoin:** in an open study of 104 patients about 70% improved within 1–4 weeks, but 11 stopped for side effects; long-term use in ichthyosis has been linked to bone toxicity. [S1]
- **Alitretinoin 30 mg/day** has helped young women in whom acitretin failed or was unsuitable because of childbearing potential; relapse on dose reduction or stopping was common. [S1]
- **Other oral options are anecdotal,** for example doxycycline 100 mg/day in two case reports, with no long-term data. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Acitretin is the preferred oral retinoid** for its efficacy and better safety, notably for bone: 0.25–0.5 mg/kg/day or less, set to the clinical situation, with liver tests, lipids and (where relevant) skeletal checks. Isotretinoin suits short courses, given doubts about its long-term bone safety; alitretinoin is the option for women who may want a pregnancy, since contraception is needed for only 1 month after it is stopped. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Before an oral retinoid, screen for self-harm and suicide risk,** particularly with a past psychiatric history, extensive disease or poor quality of life; depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, epilepsy and other neuropsychiatric conditions are looked for systematically. If the patient takes lithium, consider an alternative. [S1]
- **JAK, IL-23, IL-17 and IL-4/13 inhibitors rest only on case reports and series,** with inconsistent benefit and safety; watch for skin infection if one is tried. MEK inhibitors, dantrolene and HSV-1-based gene therapy remain under investigation. [S1]

## Ichthyoses and other disorders of cornification: retinoids in children and adolescents

- **The ichthyoses and related disorders of cornification arise from variants in more than 50 genes.** Care is built on frequent bathing, humidified air, daily emollients and keratolytics, with topical and oral retinoids as a key addition. [S2]
- **Topical retinoids (tazarotene, tretinoin, adapalene) lessen scale and can correct ectropion.** They carry less risk than oral retinoids and suit milder disease, but irritate, more so in ichthyosis vulgaris, atopic skin and fragile skin. [S2]
- **Offer a risk-benefit discussion about an oral retinoid to families of children with moderate to severe disease and real functional or psychological impact.** Genetic testing does not predict who will respond. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Aim for the lowest dose that reaches and holds the goal, usually 1 mg/kg/day or less.** Treatment is often lifelong because the skin returns to baseline when it stops; continuing topical care may allow a lower oral dose or breaks. [S2]
- **Isotretinoin and acitretin work about equally.** Acitretin peels palms and soles more (favouring it for palmoplantar disease) but causes more persistent hair loss; isotretinoin's short half-life eases dose changes, breaks and family planning. In Netherton syndrome, skin toxicity usually outweighs benefit. [S2]
- **Neonates with harlequin ichthyosis whose scale restricts breathing or endangers the limbs may benefit from an oral retinoid;** milder cases may not need one. [S2]
- **Subtypes where oral or topical retinoids have improved scale:** lamellar ichthyosis, epidermolytic ichthyosis, harlequin ichthyosis, some genotypes of congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma, erythrokeratodermia variabilis, ichthyosis with confetti, recessive X-linked ichthyosis, loricrin keratoderma, the KID, KLICK and IFAP syndromes, neutral lipid storage disease with ichthyosis and Sjögren–Larsson syndrome. **No data, or no benefit:** CHILD, CHIME, Conradi–Hünermann–Happle, MEDNIK and Refsum syndromes, ichthyosis prematurity syndrome, the ichthyosis–hypotrichosis syndromes (with or without sclerosing cholangitis), peeling skin disease and trichothiodystrophy. [S2]
- **Giving a retinoid capsule to a child:** it can be swallowed whole or chewed, pierced (the shell is tough) and squeezed onto food, softened for 2–3 minutes in a little warm milk or water, for an infant, stirred into a small amount (1 oz) of formula or breast milk, or frozen inside a small soft-centred chocolate; fatty food helps absorption. Keep capsules out of direct sunlight and use each one as soon as it is unpacked. [S2]

## Oral retinoids: monitoring and contraception

- ⚠️ **Lipids and liver tests at baseline, again 1–2 months after starting, then every 6–12 months while normal.** Combined oral contraceptives also raise lipids; persistently high lipids can be treated while the retinoid continues if the balance of risk is acceptable. [S2]
- **Bone:** premature epiphyseal closure is uncommon but serious, reported above 1 mg/kg/day over 4–6 years. Check growth yearly, ask about musculoskeletal symptoms and diet, and on long-term treatment consider baseline imaging repeated after 3–5 years (sooner for symptomatic sites). [S2]
- **Bone monitoring in detail (S2 Table 8):** for children, a yearly growth check (height, weight, BMI and growth curve), questions about diet (enough vitamin D, no extra vitamin A) and, once growth is complete at 16–18 years, a baseline skeletal survey (lateral cervical and thoracic spine, lateral calcanei including the Achilles tendon, hips and any painful site) with a bone density scan such as DEXA. Adults expected to stay on treatment get the same baseline survey, repeated every 3–5 years or when symptoms appear. [S2]
- **Eyes:** a baseline examination by an experienced ophthalmologist, lubricants or preservative-free tears during treatment, review after 4–6 months and then every 6–12 months on long-term treatment; pause or stop if retinal function worsens. [S2]
- **Ask about mental health at baseline and at follow-up in everyone with a disorder of cornification,** whatever the treatment: depression and anxiety are common in ichthyosis itself. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Avoid pregnancy for 3 years after stopping acitretin** (with alcohol it forms etretinate, which is stored in fat). Prefer isotretinoin for anyone of reproductive potential, and consider switching from acitretin to isotretinoin at puberty, or at least long-acting reversible contraception. [S2]
- **Raise contraception early with adolescents on a retinoid,** review it every visit, and agree when it will start (for example at 16 years, or before first sexual activity), with help from a gynaecologist if needed. [S2]

## Beyond drugs

- **Ablative laser, cryotherapy and photodynamic therapy are for localised lesions that resist treatment.** Care should be multidisciplinary (neuropsychiatric, eye, dental, obstetric); lasting remission is not yet achievable, so treatment is individual. [S1]
- **Of the physical options, ablative CO2 or Er:YAG laser (fractional or not) has the best evidence** and can give lasting results with an experienced operator. Cryotherapy and local surgery risk pigment change, scarring and recurrence; botulinum toxin can reduce flares provoked by sweating, but only for a time. [S1]
- **Let the gynaecologist know about the diagnosis:** Darier disease can give abnormal Pap smear results. In pregnancy, watch for flares and obstetric problems (skin, intrauterine or perinatal infection, traumatic delivery, difficulty breastfeeding). [S1]
