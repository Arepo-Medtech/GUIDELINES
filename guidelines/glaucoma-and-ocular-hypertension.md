# Raised intraocular pressure and glaucoma suspects: risk grading, referral and shared monitoring

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** how to measure intraocular pressure reliably, grade the risk of people with raised IOP or suspicious discs, decide who needs an ophthalmologist and how urgently, and how often to monitor under shared ophthalmologist-optometrist care, in adults. It does not cover drug choice: for IOP-lowering drops (including the fixed combinations the PBS lists), angle closure and treatment see `glaucoma`. Steroid-induced IOP rise in uveitis: see `non-infectious-uveitis`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Why raised pressure needs a plan

- **Glaucoma is the world's leading cause of preventable, irreversible blindness,** and treating it late risks permanent loss. [S1]
- **Speed of progression varies enormously:** some people never lose useful sight untreated, while in others advanced disease can blind the eye only 3 months after a routine visit found it stable. [S1]
- **Most people whose IOP is above the 95th percentile will not develop glaucoma within 5 years,** though the share who do keeps climbing with time. [S1]
- **Starting treatment is a real decision,** given its harms and costs and the fact that some patients would never lose significant vision in their lifetime. [S1]

## Measure it properly

- ⚠️ **Measure IOP with a calibrated, regularly checked Goldmann applanation tonometer.** Non-contact tonometry is too error-prone for diagnosis or monitoring (mean errors of 0.5–2.9 mmHg against Goldmann in one study) and its readings cannot be swapped for Goldmann values. [S1]
- **Measure corneal thickness too:** Goldmann reads low in thin corneas and high in thick ones, and a thin cornea independently raises the chance that ocular hypertension turns into glaucoma. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not be falsely reassured by a thick cornea:** in a sizeable minority the true pressure inside the eye is as high as the Goldmann figure or higher. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Van Herick grading is not enough to judge the angle; every eye needs assessment for angle closure,** and anyone thought to have it goes to an ophthalmologist because the definitive treatment is surgical. Narrow angles warrant referral as soon as possible, as laser may be needed early. [S1]

## Grade the risk (no glaucoma yet)

- **Lower risk** (otherwise healthy eye, normal field and nerve): IOP over 21 but under 28 mmHg with a very thick cornea, a family history of glaucoma, or past blunt eye injury with no after-effects. The optometrist can keep reviewing as needed, and may refer for an opinion. [S1]
- **Higher risk:** IOP over 21 but under 25 mmHg with average corneal thickness; an isolated disc haemorrhage; pseudoexfoliation or pigment dispersion with normal IOP; a rise in measured IOP of 50% or more over time; or drugs that can lift IOP (steroids, antidepressants with narrow angles, topiramate). Keep reviewing, with a much lower bar for referral. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Very high risk — almost always refer, and always if newly identified:** several risk factors; raised IOP from a secondary cause (those drugs, trauma, pseudoexfoliation, pigment dispersion, uveitis, iris or angle new vessels); IOP over 27 mmHg; over 24 with average corneal thickness; over 21 with a thin cornea; or using pressure-lowering drops without any ophthalmologist involved. [S1]
- **Repeated disc haemorrhages count as several risk factors,** moving the patient to very high risk. [S1]

## Refer and stage

- **Anyone an optometrist labels glaucoma or glaucoma suspect should see an ophthalmologist** to confirm the diagnosis and set a plan, with urgency matched to risk and stage. Both the diagnosis and any progression need an ophthalmologist to confirm them. [S1]
- **Follow-up is lifelong** for glaucoma, suspects and ocular hypertension; up to 25% keep losing field despite treatment and close watching. [S1]
- **Stable early glaucoma** means repeatable field loss under 6 dB, sparing the central 10 degrees, and unchanged for 3 years. **Advanced glaucoma** (loss over 12 dB or inside 10 degrees of fixation) is generally unsuitable for shared care because functional vision is at stake. [S1]
- **Unstable glaucoma goes back to the treating ophthalmologist promptly:** newly diagnosed with unknown rate of progression, IOP above target or swinging by more than 3 mmHg, structural or field progression within 3 years, disc haemorrhage, drop intolerance, or new eye disease. Laser or surgery may be needed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Acutely raised IOP is an emergency:** blurred vision, nausea and pain, pressure often above 35 mmHg, with a red eye, shallow chamber, fixed pupil, cells or blood in the chamber, or corneal oedema. Causes include acute angle closure, uveitis, rubeosis, pseudoexfoliation and pigment dispersion. Contact an ophthalmologist at once for definitive treatment. [S1]

## Shared (collaborative) monitoring

- **Keep high-risk patients with the ophthalmologist, not in shared care:** complex or secondary glaucoma (other than pseudoexfoliation or pigment dispersion), glaucoma after eye trauma, one seeing eye, several eye operations, advanced or unstable disease, or IOP above 35 mmHg. [S1]
- **The ophthalmologist writes the plan and sets target pressures,** and is consulted regularly. The arrangement is put in writing, with monitoring intervals and referral timeframes, and the patient gives informed consent. [S1]
- **Monitoring once glaucoma is excluded (lower-risk suspect):** field test and nerve imaging every 6–12 months under age 80, yearly over 80, and an ophthalmologist review at least every 3–4 years. [S1]
- **Higher-risk suspect:** field and imaging at least 6-monthly under 80 (yearly over 80) and an ophthalmologist at least every 2 years. **Very high-risk suspect:** the same testing, with an ophthalmologist at least every year. Any change prompts immediate re-referral. [S1]
