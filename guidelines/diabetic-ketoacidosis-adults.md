# Diabetic ketoacidosis (and hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state) in adults

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosis, severity triage, fluids, insulin, potassium, bicarbonate and phosphate, resolution criteria, transition to subcutaneous insulin and discharge planning for adults with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), with the key differences for the hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state (HHS). ## Recognise and diagnose

- **DKA needs all three: hyperglycaemia (or known diabetes), raised ketones and metabolic acidosis.** The glucose threshold has been lowered to 11.1 mmol/L, and a person with known diabetes qualifies at any glucose level. [S1]
- ⚠️ **About 10% of DKA is euglycaemic (glucose below 11.1 mmol/L).** Causes include SGLT2 inhibitors (now the majority of cases), reduced food intake, pregnancy, alcohol and liver failure. [S1]
- **Measure blood β-hydroxybutyrate (venous or capillary) to diagnose and to monitor treatment**, not urine nitroprusside ketones. A level of 3.0 mmol/L or more has over 90% sensitivity and specificity for DKA. [S1]
- **First tests:** glucose, electrolytes, venous blood gas, full blood count and ketones. Examine for infection, ischaemia and other precipitants, and do an ECG (hyperkalaemia, ischaemia). [S1]
- **Look for the trigger:** infection is the commonest worldwide (14–58% of cases), and missed insulin is a major cause. Glucocorticoids, antipsychotics and immune checkpoint inhibitors can also precipitate a crisis. [S1]
- **Other causes of ketosis to exclude:** starvation ketosis, alcoholic ketoacidosis, and ketosis of pregnancy or hyperemesis. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Abdominal pain may be caused by DKA or may be its trigger.** Re-examine if it does not settle once dehydration and acidosis have been corrected. [S1]

## Where to treat and how to monitor

- **Uncomplicated mild or moderate DKA can be managed in the emergency department or a step-down unit** with close nursing monitoring. Severe DKA, HHS, a critical-illness trigger or altered mental state belongs in ICU. [S1]
- **Capillary glucose every 1–2 h; bloods every 4 h** (electrolytes, phosphate, creatinine, β-hydroxybutyrate, venous pH) until DKA resolves. [S1]

## Fluids

- **Start isotonic saline or a balanced crystalloid at 500–1000 ml/h for the first 2–4 h** if there is no renal or cardiac compromise, and correct the estimated deficit over 24–48 h. Balanced crystalloids resolved DKA faster than saline in recent studies. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Older adults, heart failure and dialysis patients:** use smaller boluses (e.g. 250 ml) and reassess haemodynamics often. [S1]

## Insulin

- **Start insulin as soon as possible after diagnosis: intravenous short-acting insulin by fixed-rate infusion at 0.1 U/kg/h** (or a nurse-driven variable-rate protocol). Give a 0.1 U/kg bolus IV or IM only if venous access will be delayed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **When glucose falls below 13.9 mmol/L, add 5–10% dextrose and halve insulin to 0.05 U/kg/h**, aiming for glucose around 11.1 mmol/L. Keep the insulin running until the ketoacidosis has resolved. [S1]
- **Continue the patient's usual basal insulin.** Adding low-dose basal insulin (0.15–0.3 U/kg) during the infusion shortened time to resolution and hospital stay without more hypoglycaemia. [S1]
- **Mild or moderate uncomplicated DKA can be treated with subcutaneous rapid-acting analogue every 1–2 h** instead of an infusion. Do not use this for severe or complicated DKA or for HHS. [S1]

## Electrolytes and bicarbonate

- ⚠️ **Potassium: once below 5.0 mmol/L, replace to keep 4–5 mmol/L; 20–30 mmol per litre of fluid is usually enough.** If potassium is below 3.5 mmol/L at presentation, give 10 mmol/h and **hold insulin until it is above 3.5 mmol/L**. [S1]
- **Check potassium 2 h after insulin starts and then every 4 h** until DKA resolves. [S1]
- **No routine bicarbonate.** Consider it only if pH is below 7.0: 100 mmol sodium bicarbonate (8.4%) in 400 ml sterile water every 2 h until pH is above 7.0. [S1]
- **No routine phosphate.** Replace only with muscle weakness, or respiratory or cardiac compromise, and phosphate below 1.0 mmol/L; 20–30 mmol potassium phosphate can go into the fluids. [S1]

## Resolution and transition

- **DKA has resolved when ketones are below 0.6 mmol/L and venous pH is 7.3 or more (or bicarbonate 18 mmol/L or more)**, ideally with glucose below 11.1 mmol/L. Do not use the anion gap or urine ketones to judge resolution. [S1]
- **Overlap subcutaneous and IV insulin by 1–2 h.** For insulin-naive patients estimate a total daily dose of 0.5–0.6 U/kg/day, or about 0.3 U/kg/day with frailty or kidney failure. If baseline therapy was inadequate or a drug contributed, change the regimen before discharge. [S1]

## HHS: what differs

- **HHS insulin: fixed-rate 0.05 U/kg/h without significant ketonaemia or acidosis; 0.1 U/kg/h if it is mixed DKA/HHS.** Glucose should fall by no more than 5–6.7 mmol/L per hour, and sodium by no more than 10 mmol/L in 24 h. [S1]

## Discharge and prevention

- **Before discharge:** education on the episode and on diabetes self-management, sick-day advice, an insulin supply, and ketone testing. Offer CGM after discharge and follow up within 2–4 weeks. [S1]
- **Screen everyone with a hyperglycaemic crisis for mental health problems** (for example PHQ-9 for depression). Suicide-attempt risk is highest in the 12 months after DKA. [S1]

## Australian practice (S2)

- 🇦🇺 **Insulin infusions differ across Australia:** in a national survey covering 31 hospitals, 39% of DKA protocols used a fixed rate, 45% a variable (glucose-titrated) rate and 16% a mix, and the responding clinicians were evenly divided in which they preferred. [S2]
- **What the fixed or mixed protocols used:** nearly all set the rate by weight, most often 0.1 and/or 0.05 u/kg (one used 0.07 u/kg; one used a flat 4 u/h). Only 10% started with an IV insulin bolus (all variable-rate protocols), and 68% said to keep giving subcutaneous basal insulin. [S2]
- **Where DKA is managed in Australia varies:** mainly the emergency department in 29% of hospitals, ICU in 26%, a high-dependency unit in 13% and the ward in 32%; 77% had written criteria for ICU admission. [S2]
- **Most Australian protocols still name 0.9% sodium chloride** (86% of those naming a fluid; only three chose a balanced crystalloid) and 70% of clinicians prefer it, for reasons such as premixed potassium bags, familiarity and cost, and a view that hyperchloraemia matters little beside ketosis. [S2]
- ⚠️ **SGLT2-inhibitor ketoacidosis is under-addressed:** 55% of protocols mentioned SGLT2 inhibitors but only 16% managed it differently. Because it runs closer to normal glucose, a variable-rate infusion may under-treat it unless extra dextrose is given early, and a fixed-rate infusion needs added dextrose to prevent hypoglycaemia. [S2]
- **Resolution criteria, where written (67% of protocols), are close to S1's:** most used pH above 7.3 (two used above 7.35), and 77% used ketones below 0.6 mmol/L, with the rest ranging from below 0.3 to below 1.0 mmol/L. [S2]
- **The S2 authors dispute the international push for fixed-rate infusions:** no regimen has been shown superior, and fixed rates bring more hypoglycaemia (28% in a 2014 UK audit, after which UK guidance added halving from 0.1 to 0.05 u/kg/h once glucose is below 14 mmol/L). Variable rates usually give about 2.6–3.5 u/h, against 7 u/h for a 70-kg adult on a weight-based protocol. Until better evidence exists they would support both, chosen by local capacity with a focus on avoiding hypoglycaemia and hypokalaemia. [S2]
- **S2 calls for an Australian national consensus,** starting with how DKA is diagnosed and when it has resolved, so outcomes of the different infusion regimens can be compared. [S2]
