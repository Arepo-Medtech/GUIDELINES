# Inflammation after corneal transplant: preventing and treating graft rejection

## How common rejection is

- **After penetrating keratoplasty, rejection occurs in roughly 19% to 41% of grafts,** mostly in the first 1–3 years, though a 23% rate has been reported at 15 years. [S1]
- **In the Australian Corneal Graft Registry, 28% of 20,336 grafts had failed, and rejection caused 28% of those failures.** [S1]
- **Endothelial keratoplasty carries less rejection risk than a full-thickness graft:** at 2 years one series reported 1% after DMEK, 12% after DSEK and 18% after penetrating keratoplasty. [S1]
- **For endothelial failure without corneal scarring, endothelial keratoplasty has replaced penetrating keratoplasty as the operation of choice** (AAO): vision recovers sooner, immune rejection is less likely and less astigmatism is induced. [S2]
- **Registry and series figures (AAO):** in the Swedish registry 13% of penetrating grafts for Fuchs dystrophy or pseudophakic bullous keratopathy had rejected by 1 year; one DSAEK series reported 6.0% by year 1, 14.0% by year 2 and 22.0% by year 3. [S2]
- **Graft type and rejection, where the evidence is mixed:** two studies of PK against DSAEK for Fuchs dystrophy and bullous keratopathy found no significant difference, but large cohorts show significantly less rejection after DMEK than after either PK or DSAEK. [S2]
- **At 5 years, graft survival for both PK and DSAEK is 95% for Fuchs dystrophy and 73% for pseudophakic bullous keratopathy;** loss of endothelial function, with or without an earlier rejection episode, is the main cause of failure for both. [S2]
- **Anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) rejects far less often than PK (1.9% against 7.8%) and never has endothelial rejection;** stromal rejection occurs in 2% to 12% and usually reverses with topical corticosteroid. [S2]

## Who is at high risk

- **Recipient factors matter more than donor factors:** the original diagnosis (infective or traumatic scarring is worse), young age (under 40 in one large study), a repeat graft, glaucoma, corneal new vessels, recent eye surgery and anterior synechiae all raise risk. [S1]
- ⚠️ **A 'high-risk' cornea has new vessels in two or more quadrants extending 2 mm or more into the stroma, or has already rejected a graft.** Risk climbs with each extra vascularised quadrant. [S1]
- **Loose, broken or exposed sutures irritate the eye, draw in vessels and can trigger rejection,** as can later surgery or removal of sutures. [S1]
- **Sutures left in long term to hold a good, low-astigmatism result** carry a risk of late breakage, irritation, infection or rejection (AAO). [S2]
- **A history of recurrent eye inflammation (herpes simplex keratitis, herpes zoster ophthalmicus, uveitis) marks a high-risk graft:** after PK the usual regimen may need to be stepped up with more corticosteroid per day or an oral antiviral. [S2]
- **Prepare the eye before grafting:** control IOP well; settle inflammation and infection of the lids, lacrimal sac and eye (such as dacryocystitis, blepharitis, keratitis); and repair lid problems. Deep stromal vessels and ocular surface disease reduce graft survival. [S2]

## Recognise rejection

- ⚠️ **Most episodes come in the first six months but can occur at any time.** Symptoms are redness, blurred vision, light sensitivity or discomfort, yet almost 30% have no symptoms. [S1]
- **Signs:** circumciliary injection is often first; then keratic precipitates, anterior chamber cells and flare, and segmental corneal oedema. A Khodadoust line (a wave of white cells moving across the endothelium) is pathognomonic. Raised pressure or a new epithelial defect can be the only clue, especially in children. [S1]
- **After endothelial keratoplasty, rejection is subtler:** in one DSEK series 35% had no symptoms and were picked up at routine review, so serial specular microscopy may help detect it early after DMEK. [S1]
- **Herpes simplex endotheliitis can mimic rejection;** focal keratic precipitates on both host and donor endothelium, iritis and a pressure rise point to the virus. [S1]
- **Primary graft failure is not rejection:** the donor cornea never clears in the first 8 weeks, with no other cause of stromal oedema such as rejection, raised IOP or an epithelial defect. It reflects poor donor endothelium and usually needs a regraft. [S2]

## Treat an episode

- ⚠️ **Outcome depends on catching it early and starting corticosteroids at once.** Topical steroids are the mainstay for every type of rejection. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Endothelial rejection: prednisolone acetate 1% every 1–2 hours for the first few weeks, then taper slowly over 2–3 months once it settles;** consider lifelong low-dose drops in high-risk or one-eyed patients. **Other types:** 4–6 times a day, tapered over 6–8 weeks. [S1]
- **For severe endothelial rejection seen early, add intravenous steroid:** a single 500 mg methylprednisolone pulse raised reversal to 80%, against 39% with hourly topical drops alone, and one, two or three pulses worked equally well. [S1]
- **Intravenous dexamethasone 100 mg has been proposed as a cheaper alternative;** a non-randomised series found better reversal than methylprednisolone 500 mg (72.3% against 45%). [S1]
- **For rejection that does not respond, intracameral triamcinolone (4 mg in 0.1 ml) is an extra option,** and an intravitreal dexamethasone implant has been used, though without randomised trial evidence. [S1]
- **Supportive care as needed:** antibiotic prophylaxis, cycloplegics, lubricants and pressure-lowering drops. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Rejection is the most frequent cause of graft failure (AAO); prompt, aggressive corticosteroid, topical, periocular and systemic, may reverse endothelial rejection.** [S2]

## Prevent rejection

- ⚠️ **Topical corticosteroid for the first six months after keratoplasty is universal practice** and is the main reason rejection is uncommon then. Prednisolone is used most, whatever the risk or lens status; difluprednate, loteprednol and fluorometholone are alternatives. [S1]
- **Keep topical steroids going long term if needed, but not systemic ones,** which carry serious risks. Long-term topical use can cause glaucoma, cataract and infection. [S1]
- **After anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) only epithelial and stromal rejection can occur;** a longer steroid course helps prevent stromal rejection, and close monitoring is advised. [S1]
- ⚠️ **After endothelial keratoplasty, stopping steroids is itself a risk factor for rejection** (DSAEK). [S1]
- **High-risk grafts:** treat corneal new vessels (anti-VEGF, fine-needle diathermy or laser) where available, start an immunomodulator (preferably mycophenolate), and extend the steroid course. Topical cyclosporine has a limited role; topical tacrolimus 0.03% is a promising second-line option. [S1]
- **Plan follow-up carefully and counsel patients,** because early detection is what makes rejection treatable. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Tell patients that new or worsening pain, redness, light sensitivity or blurred vision needs to be seen at once,** as it can signal infection, inflammation or recurrence; at follow-up, recheck intraocular inflammation and IOP regularly (AAO). [S2]
