# Huntington disease — symptomatic treatment

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** symptomatic drug and non-drug treatment of Huntington disease (HD) in adults: chorea (tetrabenazine or a second-generation neuroleptic), other motor problems, swallowing, depression, suicide risk, irritability, psychosis, sleep and weight loss. The source gives no doses. The PBS condition *Hyperkinetic extrapyramidal disorders* (tetrabenazine) is wider than HD chorea; S2 adds tardive (drug-induced) hyperkinetic disorders and neuroleptic malignant syndrome; other hyperkinetic disorders are not covered. Related: `parkinsons-disease`, `cervical-dystonia`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Principles

- **HD is an autosomal-dominant CAG-repeat disorder of the HTT gene; 40 or more repeats are fully penetrant.** It causes motor, cognitive and psychiatric disorders, and death occurs about 20 years after symptoms start. [S1]
- **Stress, fatigue and intercurrent illness (anxiety, digestive, infectious or painful conditions) can worsen any HD symptom;** assess and treat them alongside the HD symptoms. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Worsening involuntary movements or a change in behaviour should prompt a search for discomfort, especially pain.** [S1]

## Chorea

- **Treat chorea with drugs when it distresses or discomforts the patient.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Tetrabenazine is a first-line treatment for chorea (Grade A), unless depression is poorly controlled or there are suicidal thoughts.** [S1]
- **A second-generation neuroleptic is first line (Grade B) particularly when personality, behavioural or psychotic disorders accompany the chorea.** [S1]
- **Use one antichorea drug, not a combination:** combinations raise the risk of adverse effects and complicate management of non-motor symptoms. [S1]
- **Deutetrabenazine (Grade A) may be an alternative to tetrabenazine where it is authorised**, as in the USA. [S1]

## Adverse effects of antichorea drugs

- **Tetrabenazine and neuroleptics can cause or worsen rigidity.** If function suffers, consider lowering the dose or stopping, weighing benefit on chorea or behaviour against the rigidity. [S1]
- **Akathisia: look for a drug cause first.** Tetrabenazine, neuroleptics and SSRIs can cause it; lowering the dose or switching may help. [S1]
- **Antichorea drugs can help or hinder:** they may improve gait and balance, but side effects (sedation, parkinsonism) can impair swallowing and walking, so reassess them regularly. [S1]
- **Akathisia usually eases once the causative drug is stopped.** Clonidine, mirtazapine, amantadine, benzodiazepines, beta blockers and anticholinergics have all been tried, with variable effect and little evidence. [S2]
- **Drug-induced parkinsonism** (from dopamine-blocking drugs) is usually symmetrical and akinetic-rigid, comes on over days to months, responds poorly to levodopa, dopamine agonists and anticholinergics, and normally resolves fully when the drug is stopped. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Tetrabenazine, like antipsychotics, lithium and metoclopramide, can cause neuroleptic malignant syndrome.** Suspect it with dopamine-blocker exposure (or dopamine-agonist withdrawal) in the last 72 hours plus temperature over 38 °C on two or more readings, rigidity, altered mental state, raised creatine kinase and autonomic instability. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Suspected neuroleptic malignant syndrome means hospital admission:** stop the causative drugs at once, give supportive care (intensive care if severe) and a dopaminergic drug, usually bromocriptine; benzodiazepines can ease rigidity. It usually settles within 2–3 weeks, so continue bromocriptine for several weeks. [S2]

## Tardive (drug-induced) hyperkinetic disorders

- **Tardive disorders start during a drug or within weeks of stopping it and persist for at least one month,** after at least three months of exposure (one month in adults over 60). Antipsychotics, the antiemetics metoclopramide and prochlorperazine, and cinnarizine or flunarizine are the usual causes. [S2]
- **Tardive forms include orobuccolingual dyskinesia, stereotypies, akathisia, dystonia, myoclonus, tremor, tics and parkinsonism.** Stopping long-term antipsychotics abruptly can trigger withdrawal dyskinesia, especially in children; it settles when the drug is restarted and then tapered slowly. [S2]
- **There is no good evidence on how to treat tardive disorders.** The usual approach is to withdraw the causative drug and trial a combination of drugs (clonazepam especially for myoclonus). Sometimes the drug has to be restarted or switched to an atypical antipsychotic, with clozapine preferred in chronic psychosis. [S2]
- **The VMAT2 inhibitors deutetrabenazine and valbenazine are the newest proposed options;** tetrabenazine, amantadine and propranolol have also been tried. [S2]
- **Vitamin B6 and Ginkgo biloba are probably helpful** (vitamin E results conflict), but Ginkgo's antiplatelet effect needs care with antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs. Preventive anticholinergics have no supporting evidence. Botulinum toxin can help focal tardive dystonia, and pallidal deep brain stimulation can work very well in severe cases. [S2]

## Other motor problems and swallowing

- **Akinetic-rigid symptoms, especially in juvenile HD, may respond partially and temporarily to levodopa (Grade C);** start slowly, and the daily dose is usually lower than in Parkinson disease. [S1]
- **Myoclonus affecting function: sodium valproate or clonazepam, alone or together, in escalating doses (Grade C);** levetiracetam is an alternative. [S1]
- **Assess swallowing regularly and refer to a speech and language therapist as soon as problems appear (Grade C).** Discuss gastrostomy (PEG) early, while the patient can still weigh it. [S1]
- **Refer early to physiotherapy;** exercise programmes help overall function and independence (Grade B). [S1]

## Psychiatric symptoms

- ⚠️ **Assess suicide risk at every stage,** especially at diagnosis and when the disease begins to affect daily life. [S1]
- **Depression (Grade B):** use an SSRI or SNRI, or mianserin or mirtazapine when sleep is disturbed. Consult a psychiatrist for resistant or psychotic depression. [S1]
- **Irritability:** SSRIs are first line (Grade C), often near the maximum dose; for aggressive behaviour a neuroleptic is first line (Grade C). [S1]
- **Hallucinations and delusions:** stop other psychotropic drugs, then use a second-generation neuroleptic (Grade C); clozapine is first line in akinetic HD with disabling parkinsonism. [S1]

## Other symptoms

- **Cognitive symptoms: no drug is recommended;** rehabilitation may help for a time (Grade B). [S1]
- **Weight loss: intervene when unintended loss exceeds 10% over 3–6 months, or 5% over 3–6 months with a BMI below 20 kg/m2.** Use dietitian-supervised high-calorie, high-protein supplements (Grade C). [S1]
