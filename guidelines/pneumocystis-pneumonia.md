# Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP/PCP) — diagnosis and treatment

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** suspecting, diagnosing, treating and following up PJP in people with and without HIV (haematological malignancy, transplant, other immunosuppression). ## Who is at risk

- **HIV:** risk rises in people who are not on (or not adherent to) antiretroviral therapy and have a CD4 count under 200 cells/mm³. [S1]
- **Without HIV, the highest risk is after haematopoietic stem cell transplant (especially beyond day 100) and lung or kidney transplant.** Moderate risk: cancer or autoimmune disease on combined immunosuppression including glucocorticoids (median 30 mg prednisone-equivalent daily for 2–3 months or more). B-cell-depleting drugs appear to carry moderate to high risk; a single immunomodulator carries low risk. [S1]

## Recognise

- **Classic triad: breathlessness, fever and dry cough, often with tachypnoea and hypoxia.** People with HIV usually have the triad; people without HIV present less specifically, more often with breathlessness and respiratory failure, and deteriorate faster. [S1]
- **Imaging:** bilateral symmetric reticular shadowing on chest X-ray; on CT, ground-glass opacity with peripheral sparing, thickened interlobular septa and crazy paving. [S1]
- ⚠️ **PJP is more lethal without HIV:** attributable mortality is 21.9–22.8% in non-HIV patients versus 3.1–9.9% in people with HIV. [S1]

## Diagnose

- **Bronchoalveolar lavage is the best sample for everyone.** Induced sputum performs comparably in HIV but less consistently without HIV; nasopharyngeal swabs and expectorated sputum have low sensitivity and are a last resort. [S1]
- **Immunofluorescence microscopy is the standard test;** conventional stains are less sensitive. A negative microscopy result does not exclude PJP, especially without HIV. [S1]
- **PCR is highly sensitive (negative predictive value 100%) but cannot reliably separate infection from colonisation;** no validated cycle-threshold cut-off exists. [S1]
- **Raised LDH and serum beta-D-glucan support the diagnosis;** interpret beta-D-glucan against co-medications and co-infections, and it is unclear whether a negative result excludes PJP without HIV. [S1]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **First line is co-trimoxazole: trimethoprim 15–20 mg/kg plus sulfamethoxazole 75–100 mg/kg.** Oral is fine for mild and moderate disease when absorption is not impaired; give it IV in moderate to severe disease and switch to oral once improving. Treat for at least 14 days if moderate and 21 days if severe. [S1]
- **Alternatives (weaker evidence):** pentamidine 4 mg/kg/day; primaquine 30 mg/day with clindamycin 600 mg 8-hourly; atovaquone 750 mg every 8–12 hours; or dapsone 100 mg with a second drug at 15 mg/kg/day. Before switching, judge whether co-trimoxazole side effects can be managed. [S1]
- **Co-trimoxazole side effects are common (20–80%):** rash (rarely Stevens–Johnson syndrome), fever, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia, azotaemia and hyperkalaemia. Treat mild reactions symptomatically; stop and switch for severe or life-threatening ones. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Adjunctive corticosteroids for moderate to severe PJP in HIV,** started as early as possible and tapered per guideline; they reduce mortality. Without HIV they are not routine but may be considered case by case in respiratory failure. [S1]
- **In HIV, start antiretroviral therapy within 2 weeks of the PJP diagnosis;** early start was non-inferior or superior to delay. [S1]

## Follow up and secondary prophylaxis

- **Repeat imaging to follow response** (radiographic improvement at a median of 13 days). Do not use beta-D-glucan or BAL PCR to monitor treatment: both can stay positive after cure. [S1]
- ⚠️ **HIV: start secondary prophylaxis straight after treatment and continue until CD4 is over 200 cells/mm³ for at least 3 months** (or CD4 100–200 with undetectable viral load for 3–6 months). Restart if CD4 falls below 100, or is 100–200 with detectable virus. [S1]
- **Without HIV, secondary prophylaxis is also recommended after treatment,** stopping on an individual basis once immunity has recovered (ECIL). The transplant guideline (AST-IDCOP) does not recommend it for solid organ recipients. [S1]
