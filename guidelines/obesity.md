# Obesity in adults — pharmacotherapy alongside health behaviour change

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults (18 years and over) with obesity: assessment beyond BMI, goals of treatment, choice of medicine (semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide, naltrexone–bupropion, orlistat, setmelanotide), long-term use and use in obesity-related conditions. Lifestyle is covered only as the base to which medicines are added. Children: out of scope. Diabetes-specific choices: see `type-2-diabetes`.

> 🇦🇺 **S2 is the Australian source (Australian Diabetes Society algorithm, January 2026)** for the treatment pathway by BMI, waist and complications, diet options, TGA-approved medicines with starting doses, bariatric surgery criteria and pregnancy. **S1** (Obesity Canada 2025) supplies the trial evidence for each medicine. Where they differ, both are shown.

> ⚠️ International guideline (Obesity Canada, 2025). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **What the PBS lists vs what the guideline recommends.** The guideline recommends five medicines for general obesity management, with the largest weight loss from **semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly and tirzepatide**. **The PBS 'Obesity' listing covers orlistat only** (see table below): orlistat gives the smallest effect in the guideline (about 3% placebo-subtracted at 1 year). GLP-1 receptor agonists are PBS-listed for **type 2 diabetes** (see `type-2-diabetes`), not for obesity.

## Why treat, and the role of lifestyle

- **Lifestyle (health behaviour) change alone usually gives only 3%–5% weight loss**, often not kept off and not enough to improve most complications. Medicines are one of the mainstays of treatment, alongside psychological support, bariatric surgery, nutrition therapy and physical activity. [S1]
- **Always pair obesity medicines with health behaviour change**, including medical nutrition therapy and physical activity, after a full assessment that addresses contributing factors where possible. [S1]
- **Reduce weight stigma and bias**; care should be patient-centred with shared decision-making. [S1]

## Assess

- **BMI alone is not enough**: it says nothing about body composition or fat distribution. Sex- and ethnicity-specific anthropometric measures should also be used, so people below ethnicity-specific BMI thresholds are not missed. [S1]
- **Agree goals before starting a medicine**: realistic expectations, benefits and risks. Targets can include cardiometabolic risk, complications, weight maintenance, appetite control and quality of life, not weight alone. [S1]
- 🇦🇺 **Australian pathway: BMI band, waist circumference and complications decide the treatment.** Abdominal obesity is a waist above 88 cm in women or 102 cm in men. For South Asian, South-East Asian, East Asian and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, use BMI 27.5–37.5 and above 37.5 in place of 30–40 and above 40. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **Weight-loss targets (a guide, to be agreed with the person):** 10–15% for BMI 30–40, or for BMI above 40 without complications; more than 15% for BMI above 40 with complications. Even about 5% lowers diabetes risk. [S2]

## Diet options (Australian algorithm)

- **Three levels of energy restriction:** a reduced energy diet (a deficit of 2000–4000 kJ a day); a low energy diet (4200–5000 kJ a day, which can replace one or two meals with formulated meal replacements); and a very low energy diet (under 3300 kJ a day, meals replaced by formulated products), offered when the first two have not worked or rapid loss is needed, such as before surgery. [S2]
- **Very low energy diet in practice:** usually up to 12 weeks, or 6–12 months under close supervision, with review at least monthly. The partial regimen replaces two meals a day and the complete regimen all three; add a teaspoon of olive oil daily to reduce gallstone risk. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **In DiRECT-Aus (25 NSW general practices), a very low energy diet put 56% into diabetes remission at 12 months,** among people who had had type 2 diabetes for 6 years or less. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Not for a very low energy diet:** pregnancy or breastfeeding; severe psychiatric illness, alcoholism or drug dependence; recent myocardial infarction, stroke or unstable angina; porphyria; and use caution over 65. **On insulin or a sulfonylurea, halve the dose at the start** and adjust by frequent glucose checks; on an SGLT2 inhibitor, monitor ketones or consider stopping it; on warfarin, check the INR after one week. [S2]

## Choosing a medicine

- **Semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly** (trial entry BMI ≥ 30, or ≥ 27 with a complication, no diabetes) gave −14.9% weight change at 68 weeks vs −2.4% on placebo. Nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea were common and mostly transient; gallbladder problems and, rarely, pancreatitis occurred. [S1]
- **Tirzepatide 5, 10 or 15 mg weekly** gave −15.0%, −19.5% and −20.9% at 72 weeks vs −3.1% on placebo; gastrointestinal effects occur mainly during dose escalation. [S1]
- **Liraglutide 3 mg daily** gave −8.0% vs −2.6% at 56 weeks; **naltrexone 16 mg with bupropion 180 mg, taken twice a day,** gave −6.1% vs −1.3% at 56 weeks, with more stopping for adverse effects (20.1% v. 9.6%). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Orlistat 120 mg three times daily (the only PBS-listed obesity medicine)** gave a placebo-subtracted change of −2.9% at 1 year in a meta-analysis of 16 trials. Oily stools, faecal urgency and spotting affect 15%–30% and lead some to stop. [S1]
- **Consider rare monogenic obesity** with onset before age 5 and hyperphagia, parental relatedness, intellectual disability, structural defects or complex endocrine disease; refer to a geneticist. Setmelanotide reduces weight and hunger in these conditions. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Avoid compounded GLP-1 and GIP–GLP-1 products** not approved by the regulator, because their content, safety, quality and efficacy are uncertain. [S1]
- 🇦🇺 **The TGA has approved six obesity medicines:** semaglutide 2.4 mg, tirzepatide, liraglutide, naltrexone–bupropion, orlistat and phentermine. When lifestyle change is not enough, the Australian algorithm usually adds semaglutide 2.4 mg (Wegovy) or tirzepatide (Mounjaro). [S2]
- **Starting doses and titration (S2):** liraglutide 0.6 mg daily, raised by 0.6 mg a week to a maximum of 3.0 mg; semaglutide 0.25 mg weekly, raised monthly to 2.4 mg; tirzepatide 2.5 mg weekly, raised monthly to a maintenance dose of 5, 10 or 15 mg; naltrexone 8 mg/bupropion 90 mg, one tablet a day, adding one a week up to two tablets twice a day. [S2]
- **Phentermine (S2 only; not among S1's recommended medicines)** is licensed only for up to 3 months as an add-on to lifestyle change and gives 5–10% loss over 12 weeks with a low-energy diet. Avoid it alongside antidepressants and in coronary heart disease, heart rhythm disorders or uncontrolled high blood pressure; it can cause a fast heart rate, raised blood pressure, insomnia and dry mouth. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Semaglutide can worsen pre-existing diabetic retinopathy,** most likely through the rapid fall in glucose, so people with a history of retinopathy need close eye monitoring. [S2]

## Bariatric surgery (Australian criteria)

- 🇦🇺 **Consider bariatric surgery** (NHMRC criteria as restated by ADS) for BMI above 40; BMI 35.0–39.9 with complications that weight loss may improve; or BMI 30.0–34.9 with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes and raised cardiovascular risk. **In type 2 diabetes, ADS endorses metabolic surgery** at BMI 40 or more whatever the glucose control, or BMI 35.0–39.9 with inadequate control despite optimal therapy, and suggests considering it at 30.0–34.9 when hyperglycaemia remains uncontrolled. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Pregnancy (Australian categories):** semaglutide, tirzepatide and topiramate are category D. If bariatric surgery is indicated, do it at least 12–18 months before pregnancy. With BMI above 30, total gestational weight gain should not exceed 9 kg. [S2]

## Long-term treatment

- ⚠️ **Obesity medicines are long-term treatment.** Trials consistently show weight regain and loss of health benefit when they are stopped, even with continued lifestyle change. [S1]
- **Stopping semaglutide after a 20-week run-in led to regain of 6.9%**, while continuing gave a further −7.9%; stopping tirzepatide led to regain of 14.0%. [S1]
- **Medicines also help keep off weight lost through lifestyle change:** liraglutide, orlistat (after a very low-energy diet) and tirzepatide all reduced regain in trials. [S1]
- **Doses can be tailored**: the treatment goal may be reached below the maximum dose. [S1]

## Obesity-related conditions

- **Established cardiovascular disease:** semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly cut major adverse cardiovascular events by 20% (HR 0.80) in people aged 45 or over with BMI ≥ 27 and no diabetes. [S1]
- **Prediabetes:** liraglutide cut progression to diabetes by 79%, tirzepatide by 93%, and orlistat reduced progression (6.2% vs 9.0% at 208 weeks). [S1]
- **Type 2 diabetes:** all five recommended medicines improved both weight and glycaemic control in trials. [S1]
- **Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, steatohepatitis (MASH), obstructive sleep apnoea and knee osteoarthritis** can also benefit from obesity medicines combined with lifestyle change. [S1]
