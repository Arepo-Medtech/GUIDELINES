## Key points

- **Definition:** sepsis is organ dysfunction that threatens life, caused by a deregulated host response to infection. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Recognise it fast, but do not over-call it:** most febrile children, with or without a focus, are not septic. [S1]
- **Septic shock** is sepsis with cardiovascular organ dysfunction. ⚠️ Low blood pressure comes late. [S1]
- **Essentials:** fast vascular access, empiric antibiotics started early, and fluid resuscitation titrated with care. [S1]
- **Inotropes and vasopressors can safely run through a peripheral cannula or an IO line** in children during the initial resuscitation. [S1]

## Background

- **Sepsis is a leading cause of illness and death in children**, and what happens in the first hour after it is identified is critical. [S1]
- **Higher-risk groups:** neonates · immunocompromised children · children with a central venous access device · Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children. [S1]
- **Under 2 months, the main pathogens** are *Escherichia coli* and Group B *Streptococcus*; *Listeria monocytogenes* is uncommon. Keep HSV in the differential. [S1]
- **Beyond infancy:** *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Staphylococcus aureus* (MSSA or MRSA), *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and Group A *Streptococcus* (GAS). [S1]

## Assessment

- **Think of sepsis or septic shock** when a suspected or proven bacterial infection comes with any of the features below. [S1]
- **Altered consciousness** (lethargic, irritable, floppy, weak cry) · **an unwell look**, with or without a non-blanching rash. [S1]
- **Cardiovascular dysfunction:** poor peripheral perfusion · pale, cool or mottled skin · central capillary refill prolonged (CRT >2) · tachycardia · urine output below 1 mL/kg/hr · a narrow pulse pressure. [S1]
- **Cold shock:** narrow pulse pressure and slow capillary refill; seen more in neonates and infants. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Warm shock:** bounding pulses, a wide pulse pressure, and flushed skin that refills quickly; seen more in older children and adolescents, and often missed. [S1]
- **Also:** fast breathing, with or without hypoxia or grunting, that a respiratory illness does not fully explain · pain without an explanation · fever or hypothermia (temperature can be normal in neonates or the immunocompromised). [S1]
- **Toxin-mediated sepsis** comes from superantigens made by toxin-producing *S. aureus* or GAS. It can present with fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, myalgia, red eyes (conjunctival injection), confusion, collapse and a widespread red rash. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Red flags:** parents who are very worried · a return visit within 48 hours · getting worse despite treatment · recent surgery or burns. [S1]

## Management principles

- **Principles for severe sepsis or septic shock:** recognise early and get senior help · assess airway and breathing, with oxygen if needed · rapid vascular access · empiric antibiotics · carefully titrated fluids · inotropes started early · critical care involved early · source control · reassess often. [S1]
- **Involve the local paediatric team** for every child with suspected sepsis. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider ICU transfer** when care must go beyond the local centre: shock persisting after 40 mL/kg fluid · any inotrope need · venous lactate staying above 3 mmol/L · neutropenia (<1000/mm3) not caused by chemotherapy · a big pleural effusion (the hemithorax almost whited out) · coagulopathy (INR >1.6, APTT >60, fibrinogen <1). [S1]
- **Second-tier resuscitation** (a second inotrope or vasopressor, steroids, haemofiltration, ECMO) is a discussion with Retrieval Services or PICU. [S1]

## Flowchart: first 15 minutes (S2)

- **First 5 minutes:** call senior help · attach cardiorespiratory monitoring · deal with airway or breathing compromise · give oxygen. [S2]
- **First 15 minutes: get vascular access**; if an IV line is not in quickly, place an IO cannula. [S2]
- **Bloods:** blood culture and a VBG with lactate and glucose first; then FBE, CRP, UEC, LFT, coagulation and, if needed, group and hold. Leave urinalysis and lumbar puncture until the child is stable. [S2]
- **Antibiotics by IV push**; if IV or IO access is delayed, consider the IM route. [S2]

## Flowchart: fluids, inotropes and airway (S2)

- **First 30 minutes, fluids:** sodium chloride 0.9% pushed as a 20 mL/kg bolus (10 mL/kg in neonates), then reassess; further 10 mL/kg boluses if needed, up to 40 mL/kg in total. [S2]
- **Keep reassessing** fluid status, perfusion (heart rate, urine output, CRT), overall condition and signs of fluid overload; if fluids help little, think about starting inotropes early. [S2]
- ⚠️ **A child who has needed 40 mL/kg of fluid belongs in a critical care setting.** [S2]
- **First 60 minutes:** circulatory failure persisting after 40 mL/kg of fluid calls for adrenaline, 0.05-0.2 mcg/kg/min. [S2]
- **Adrenaline can run peripherally (IV or IO)** while waiting for PICU transfer; get paediatric intensive care advice on site or through Retrieval Services. Push-dose inotropes are an option with an experienced clinician. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Any child on inotrope or vasopressor support should be in a critical care setting.** [S2]
- **Respiratory support:** conscious state normal → consider non-invasive ventilation (HFNC, CPAP or BiPAP); conscious state altered → consider intubation, after talking to retrieval services and with local ICU and anaesthetics involved. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Induction agents carry a high risk of cardiac arrest** in children in septic shock. [S2]

## Flowchart: empiric antibiotics (S2)

- **Age 7 days or less:** benzylpenicillin 60 mg/kg IV every 12 h plus cefotaxime 50 mg/kg IV every 12 h. [S2]
- **Over 7 up to 28 days:** IV benzylpenicillin 60 mg/kg every 6-8 h, plus either ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg IV once daily or cefotaxime 50 mg/kg IV every 6-8 h. [S2]
- **1 to 2 months (inclusive):** benzylpenicillin 60 mg/kg IV every 4-6 h plus ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg IV once daily. [S2]
- **Over 2 months:** ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg IV once daily (up to 4 g) or cefotaxime 50 mg/kg IV every 6 h (up to 2 g); plus vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV every 6 h (up to 750 mg) and clindamycin 15/kg as printed (up to 600 mg) IV every 8 h, and IVIG, for toxic shock syndrome. [S2]
- **Oncology patients:** piperacillin/tazobactam 100 mg/kg IV every 6 h (up to 4 g). If severely unwell or high risk, add amikacin 22.5 mg/kg IV once daily (18 mg/kg over 10 y; up to 1.5 g) and vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV every 6 h (up to 750 mg). [S2]
- **Central venous access, not oncology:** vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV every 6 h (up to 750 mg) with ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg IV once daily (up to 4 g) or cefotaxime 50 mg/kg IV every 6 h (up to 2 g). [S2]
- **Possible HSV under 3 months** (skin lesions, seizures, hepatitis): consider adding aciclovir 20 mg/kg IV every 8 h. [S2]
- **Extra empiric cover** may be justified by local epidemiology, at the senior clinician's discretion, eg vancomycin when MRSA is suspected. [S2]
- **No IV access:** IM ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg once daily (up to 4 g); usable at 2 months or younger too. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Past anaphylaxis to an antibiotic:** get specialist advice. [S2]

## Flowchart: adrenaline infusion and other measures (S2)

- **Adrenaline infusion when no local protocol exists:** 6 mg (six 1 in 1000 ampoules) made up in 1 L of 0.9% NaCl, so that 1 mL/kg/hr delivers 0.1 mcg/kg/min. [S2]
- **Rate:** begin at 0.5 mL/kg/hr, which is 0.05 mcg/kg/min, and adjust in 0.5 mL/kg/hr steps, up to 10 mL/kg/hr, which is 1 mcg/kg/min. Prime the line and any 3-way taps with the infusion. [S2]
- **Lactate above 3 mmol/L at the start** points to poor tissue perfusion: resuscitate, recheck at 2 hours, and aim for below 2 mmol/L. [S2]
- **Source control**, such as taking out an infected device or draining an abscess; for procedural sedation, ketamine 0.5-1 mg/kg IV. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Think myocarditis or cardiomyopathy** with cold shock and tachycardia, or if a fluid bolus makes the child worse: chest x-ray for cardiomegaly, and echo. [S2]
- **Stress-dose steroids** for children on long-term steroids or with known adrenal insufficiency. BGL below 3.3 mmol/L: follow the hypoglycaemia guideline. [S2]

## About the source page

- **Local pathways are linked** for NSW, Queensland and Western Australia, plus national resources. [S1]
- **PIC endorsed; last updated March 2020.** [S1]
- **Fluid caution in the references:** Maitland et al. (N Engl J Med 2011; 364), reporting higher mortality after fluid boluses in African children with severe infection. [S1]
- **Timing of antibiotics:** Kumar et al. (2006), in which hypotension lasting longer before effective antimicrobials predicted death in septic shock, and Weiss et al. (Crit Care Med 2014; 42), in which delayed antimicrobials in paediatric sepsis raised mortality and prolonged organ dysfunction. [S1]
- **Two definitions are cited:** Sepsis-3 (Singer et al., 2016) and the 2005 international paediatric sepsis consensus (Goldstein et al., Pediatr Crit Care Med 2005; 6). [S1]
