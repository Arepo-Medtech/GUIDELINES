# Neurogenic bladder (neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction) in adults

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with bladder dysfunction from a neurological disorder (spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, spina bifida, Parkinson's disease and others): assessment, protecting the kidneys, bladder emptying and catheterisation, drugs, botulinum toxin, urinary tract infection and follow-up. Reconstructive surgery, sexual function and fertility are not covered. Counterparts: `urinary-incontinence` (adult incontinence in general practice), `daytime-wetting-children`, `cerebral-palsy-children`, `parkinsons-disease`, `spasticity`. PBS access is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ⚠️ International guideline (European Association of Urology, 2026). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **The PBS drug for neurogenic urinary retention is phenoxybenzamine, which this guideline does not name.** It recommends α-blockers as a class to lower bladder outlet resistance and gives the selective agents tamsulosin, naftopidil and silodosin as examples. Phenoxybenzamine is a non-selective α-blocker; its dose, adverse effects and place relative to the selective agents are not addressed here.

## Recognise

- ⚠️ **Autonomic dysreflexia is an emergency to recognise:** it usually occurs with spinal cord lesions at or above T6 and is defined by a rise in systolic pressure of more than 20 mmHg over baseline, typically with severe headache, blurred vision and flushing and sweating above the lesion. A full bladder or bowel or an indwelling catheter can set it off, and it can be fatal if poorly managed. [S1]
- **Diagnose and treat early,** in both congenital and acquired neurological disease, to stop permanent damage to the urinary tract. [S1]

## Assess

- **Take a history of all four functions (bladder, bowel, sexual and neurological)** and look for alarm features such as pain, infection, haematuria or fever that need their own work-up. (Strong) [S1]
- **Baseline and routine tests:** urinalysis, blood chemistry, a bladder diary, post-void residual, a measure of leakage, and imaging of the urinary tract. (Strong) [S1]
- **Urodynamics is the only objective test of lower urinary tract function.** Do non-invasive tests first; for invasive studies use video-urodynamics, or if unavailable a filling cystometry that continues into a pressure-flow study. Monitor blood pressure and heart rate in anyone at risk of autonomic dysreflexia. (Strong) [S1]
- ⚠️ **The kidneys are at risk, especially when the bladder fills at high pressure.** Spinal cord injury and spina bifida carry more risk of renal failure than slowly progressive conditions such as MS or Parkinson's disease. With low muscle mass, a cystatin C-based GFR detects kidney disease better than a creatinine-based estimate. [S1]
- **Everyone referred needs a urinalysis and a post-void residual at the first assessment (Grade B).** After spinal cord injury, the first urological review should happen within 6 months, earlier if there are worries (Grade A). [S2]
- **Spinal cord injury, spina bifida and advanced MS need baseline urodynamics, a renal ultrasound and renal function tests (Grade A);** other causes have them when referred with a specific urological problem. Then class each patient as high, moderate or low risk to guide first treatment and surveillance (Grade B). [S2]
- **Hydronephrosis or reflux caused by high bladder pressure is treated by lowering storage pressure first;** anti-reflux surgery or ureteric stenting should not be used for it. [S2]

## Treatment goals and bladder emptying

- ⚠️ **Priorities, in order: protect the upper tracts, achieve continence, restore lower tract function, and improve quality of life.** Holding bladder pressure within safe limits during filling and voiding is the top priority, because it cuts deaths from urological causes. [S1]
- **When the bladder is overactive or poorly compliant, the aim is a low-pressure reservoir,** even if that leaves residual urine. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Discourage Credé (suprapubic pressure) and Valsalva straining** unless urodynamics shows bladder pressure stays safe: the pressures are harmful to the urinary tract, and these methods can worsen stress incontinence. [S1]
- **Triggered reflex voiding can also generate high pressure and can provoke autonomic dysreflexia in high spinal injuries,** so patients need training and close urodynamic follow-up. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Never use a penile clamp with detrusor overactivity or low compliance:** it raises bladder pressure and risks pressure necrosis where sensation is reduced. [S1]

## Drugs

- **For neurogenic overactive bladder, use an antimuscarinic or a beta-3 agonist;** antimuscarinics are first-line for detrusor overactivity. Do not choose a beta-3 agonist to lower urodynamically proven overactivity, which it does not improve. (Strong) [S1]
- ⚠️ **For poor emptying caused by outlet resistance, prescribe an α-blocker** (examples given: tamsulosin, naftopidil, silodosin); these lower outlet resistance, residual volume and autonomic dysreflexia. (Strong) See the banner on phenoxybenzamine, the PBS drug. [S1]
- **Do not use parasympathomimetics (such as bethanechol) for an underactive detrusor.** (Strong) [S1]
- **Oral antimuscarinics, with the dose pushed up as tolerated, are the first-line drug (Grade A);** evidence covers oxybutynin, tolterodine, propiverine, darifenacin and solifenacin. Combining two may help when dose escalation fails, and they are worth giving even with catheter drainage, because going without them is a risk factor for upper tract damage. [S2]

## Catheterisation

- **Intermittent catheterisation, by the patient or a carer, is the preferred method** when a neurological patient cannot empty the bladder. Sterile technique is not routine, and people need careful counselling before they start. [S1]
- **Typical practice: catheterise four to six times a day with a 12–16 Fr catheter, keeping each drained volume to no more than 400–500 mL.** Hydrophilic catheters are linked with fewer UTIs. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Avoid indwelling urethral and, to a lesser degree, suprapubic catheters where possible,** because of complications and infection. If one is needed, prefer silicone (less encrustation, and latex allergy is common in this group); intermittent clamping brings no benefit. Investigate standard bladder-cancer red flags promptly in long-term catheter users. [S1]
- **Decide between intermittent and indwelling catheters with the patient,** and teach the technique and its risks thoroughly. (Strong) [S1]
- **Match the drainage method to the person's motor function, anatomy, bladder behaviour, past complications and quality of life (Grade A),** and keep revisiting it. Self-catheterisation works best with a low-pressure bladder holding at least 350-400 mL, an unobstructed urethra and good hand function; strictures still occur in 4–13%. [S2]
- **Catheter care:** keep a closed drainage system closed, the bag below the bladder, and use pre-connected systems where possible. Indwelling catheters are usually changed every 2 to 4 weeks (monthly is commonest), though this is not evidence-based. Do not routinely use antibiotic- or silver-coated catheters, antiseptics in the bag or routine saline irrigation. [S2]

## Botulinum toxin

- **If antimuscarinics fail, inject botulinum toxin A into the detrusor** to reduce neurogenic overactivity. (Strong) The effect wears off after about nine months and repeat injections keep working. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Urinary retention is a common adverse effect, so intermittent catheterisation may become necessary,** particularly in MS, where patients often do not catheterise beforehand; a 100 U dose may lower that need in MS. [S1]
- ⚠️ **CUA dose: onabotulinumtoxinA 200 units into the detrusor** for spinal cord injury or MS when an anticholinergic has not worked or is not tolerated (Grade A). 300 units works no better and makes catheterisation more likely; for abobotulinumtoxinA the best dose is 750 units (Grade B). [S2]

## Urinary tract infection

- ⚠️ **Do not screen with urine dipsticks, and neither look for nor treat bacteriuria without symptoms,** in neurological patients. (Strong) [S1]
- **For recurrent UTIs, first improve bladder management and remove foreign bodies such as stones or indwelling catheters.** Avoid continuous long-term antibiotics; weekly cycling of antibiotic prophylaxis can be offered to selected spinal cord injury patients who self-catheterise. (Strong) [S1]
- **Call it a UTI only when leukocyturia, bacteriuria and symptoms are all present (Grade A).** Symptoms can be unusual: fever, new leakage, more spasticity, malaise, cloudy or smelly urine, back or bladder pain, dysuria, or autonomic dysreflexia. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Send a urine culture before any antibiotic (Grade A);** a catheter in place over 2 weeks should be replaced and the sample taken from the new one. Use narrow-spectrum drugs guided by local resistance, for 7 days when the response is prompt and 10–14 days for severe infection or a slow response (Grade A). [S2]
- **How the bladder is emptied is the main driver of UTI:** use clean intermittent catheterisation where possible (Grade A). A urethral indwelling catheter carries a more than 5-fold risk of recurrent UTI compared with suprapubic or intermittent catheterisation; suprapubic catheters bring more bladder stones. [S2]
- **Most patients should not have routine oral antibiotic prophylaxis (Grade A):** a meta-analysis of 15 RCTs found no support and about twice the resistance. Evidence is too thin for cranberry, probiotics, methenamine, D-mannose and similar measures; daily gentamicin bladder irrigation cut symptomatic UTI by 75% in people catheterising intermittently with recurrent UTI. [S2]

## Follow-up

- **Follow up for life, tailored to risk:** in high-risk patients check the upper tracts and repeat urodynamics at regular intervals, and investigate any significant clinical change with a specialist. (Strong) [S1]
- **CUA surveillance (Grade C, consensus):** see every patient yearly (rehabilitation physician, neurologist or GP), with a urologist involved at moderate or high risk, such as spinal cord injury, spina bifida or advanced MS. Renal and bladder ultrasound yearly at moderate or high risk; urodynamics yearly at high risk and every 2-5 years at moderate risk. No routine surveillance cystoscopy for bladder cancer, even after augmentation; use it for suspected urethral or bladder problems. [S2]
