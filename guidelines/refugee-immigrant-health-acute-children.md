# Refugee and immigrant children — acute presentations

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** emergency-department considerations for recently arrived refugee and asylum-seeker children: fever and malaria screening, immunisation status, presentation-specific differentials, escalation and follow-up. ## In the emergency department

- **In ED, the key concern is fever in a child who has recently arrived**; other health screening is for the outpatient setting. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Use an interpreter — not a family member.** [S1]
- **In NSW, Queensland and Victoria, hospital care is free for asylum seekers.** [S1]
- **Post-arrival screening is advised within 1 month, though it is not mandatory** — so many children won't have had it. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Screen for malaria in a febrile child from a malaria-endemic area who has been in Australia under 3 months** — thick/thin films and a rapid diagnostic test, which can be done on finger-prick blood. [S1]
- **When bloods are drawn in ED for a child who hasn't had post-arrival screening:** with consent, store extra blood — a large gel serum tube (7.5 mL) and an EDTA tube (2.7 mL) — and notify the immigrant health team. [S1]
- ⚠️ **A verbal immunisation history is not reliable: without a written record, assume the child has not been immunised.** [S1]

## Presentation-specific considerations

- **Fever:** beyond viral and bacterial infection, consider **hepatitis, typhoid, dysentery, dengue and arboviral infections, and malaria (endemic areas).** [S1]
- **Respiratory:** pertussis (vaccine may have been unavailable); **TB with chronic cough or a contact history**; rarely parasites causing wheeze; **sickle cell disease presenting as acute chest syndrome.** [S1]
- **Abdominal pain:** also consider parasites, **Helicobacter pylori gastritis** (family history, epigastric pain, nausea/vomiting, early satiety) and hepatitis. [S1]
- **Diarrhoea:** also consider parasites, lactose intolerance (more common in some groups), and bacillary or amoebic dysentery. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Hypocalcaemia** (tetany, cramps, stridor, seizures) — think vitamin D deficiency, more likely under 12 months; examine for rickets. **Rickets or symptomatic hypocalcaemia need ED screening** (vitamin D, Ca, Mg, PO₄, ALP, PTH, UEC, urine Cr/Ca/PO₄) **and specialist management.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Rashes — Strongyloides** can cause an intermittent urticarial rash (larva currens), typically buttocks/perianal. **Untreated Strongyloides can progress to hyperinfection syndrome if immunosuppressants, including steroids, are given.** [S1]
- **Continence/UTI:** chronic UTI may be undetected; consider mental health in secondary enuresis; **in girls, consider female genital mutilation** (get advice on how to raise it). [S1]
- **Musculoskeletal pain:** low vitamin D is an extremely frequent cause in at-risk refugee children and adolescents. [S1]
- **Nutrition concerns:** malnutrition may need admission; consider food insecurity, **iron deficiency (common)**, B12 deficiency, H. pylori, dental disease limiting chewing, and rickets restricting growth. [S1]
- **Disability or developmental concerns:** children may have had no treatment — check nutrition, clarify seizures, **link urgently with care**; don't attribute delay to English as an additional language; check age and interrupted schooling. [S1]
- **Mental health:** consider PTSD and experience of violence, including sexual violence; clarify family separations, migration history, parental mental health and **detention experience.** [S1]

## Escalation, discharge and follow-up

- **Consult the paediatric team for:** escalation of care or admission; age assessment; **every unaccompanied minor or orphan relative visa holder**; developmental delay or disability; **malnutrition (weight/BMI below the 5th centile, out of proportion to the family)**; complex medical issues; offshore or prolonged immigration detention. [S1]
- **Discharge when stable**, with clear advice on when to return and how to access emergency services. [S1]
- **Update contact details** (families move often early in settlement), consider contacting the settlement case worker or refugee health nurse, and **refer to a paediatrician** for any issue above. [S1]
