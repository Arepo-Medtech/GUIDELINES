# Neovascular (wet) age-related macular degeneration: recognition, urgent referral and anti-VEGF care

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising neovascular AMD, the urgent-referral trigger, the anti-VEGF treatment approach, and the early/intermediate AMD care (supplements, self-monitoring, review) that decides who gets referred, in adults. Written for optometrists, so injection technique and drug choice are covered only in outline. Other anti-VEGF indications: see `diabetic-macular-oedema` and `retinal-vein-occlusion`. Cataract in the same age group: see `cataract`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ✅ **COMPILED FROM 2 SOURCES.** 32 claims; **42 fragments or anchors re-checkable by machine; 3 doses.** **S1** Optometry Australia (approved 8 February 2019 (due for revision 2022); AU): 20 claims, paraphrased, hash-anchored · **S2** Iida T (published 14 July 2025; international): 12 claims, quoted

> ⚠️ **S1 is a 2019 guide, past its 2022 revision date.** It names only ranibizumab, aflibercept and off-label bevacizumab. The PBS now also lists **faricimab and brolucizumab** for subfoveal choroidal neovascularisation (see the table below), and **bevacizumab is not PBS-listed** for this. S2 (2025) names faricimab, brolucizumab and aflibercept 8 mg and sets out current regimens, but its dose table was not captured.

> ⚠️ International guideline (S2: Japanese clinical guidelines, 2025, written for Asian including Japanese patients). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

## Recognise

- **AMD is the leading cause of irreversible vision loss in Australians over 50.** [S1]
- **Smoking is the biggest risk you can change:** it at least doubles AMD risk, so advise every tobacco user to stop. [S1]
- **Five-year progression risk rises steeply with stage:** about 3.1% for early AMD (medium drusen only), but around 47.3% once both eyes have large drusen and pigment change. [S1]
- **Differentials in which neovascularisation forms without drusen:** polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, ocular histoplasmosis, pathological myopia, choroidal rupture, angioid streaks and idiopathic CNV. Consider diabetic macular oedema when the macula shows blood, lipid, exudate or fluid. [S1]
- **Warn about Charles Bonnet syndrome:** people with late AMD and poor sight in both eyes can get visual hallucinations, which are distressing and often need counselling. [S1]
- **AMD can present before 50:** S2 removed age from its diagnostic criteria for this reason. [S2]
- **Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) is a major neovascular AMD subtype in Asian populations;** its first-line treatment has moved to anti-VEGF monotherapy rather than photodynamic therapy alone or in combination. [S2]

## Refer urgently

- ⚠️ **Suspected or definite new choroidal neovascular membrane: send to an ophthalmologist as an urgent referral, to be seen within one week.** Where ophthalmology is hard to reach, get a telehealth opinion by e-referral in the same week. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Urgent-referral triggers:** vision loss, distortion reported unprompted, or a new missing or blurred patch in central vision within the last 3 months; or signs of new CNV, fluid under or within the retina at the macula, or bleeding there that nothing else explains. [S1]
- **Speed matters:** vision at presentation is among the strongest predictors of long-term acuity, so the sooner treatment starts, the better the chance of keeping or gaining vision. [S1]
- **OCT is among the most useful tests:** it shows active neovascular disease such as retinal fluid. Get an OCT whenever new symptoms appear or neovascular AMD is suspected, and refer if needed. [S1]

## Treat (ophthalmologist)

- ⚠️ **Intravitreal anti-VEGF injection is the mainstay of treatment.** In Australia the main agents are ranibizumab and aflibercept, with bevacizumab used off-label. Their efficacy is similar, so availability, injection frequency and cost usually decide the choice. [S1]
- **Start with injections at fixed monthly intervals until the lesion shows no activity** (for example, no fluid on OCT). [S1]
- **Treat and extend:** each time there is no sign of active CNV (no OCT fluid, no loss of 5 letters, no new haemorrhage), the gap to the next injection grows by 2 weeks. Visual results match monthly dosing. [S1]
- **As-needed (PRN), monthly-or-bimonthly and observe-and-plan schedules are options,** but they generally do worse than treat and extend or monthly injections. [S1]
- **Thermal laser and photodynamic therapy are now rare,** kept mostly for polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy with a well-defined lesion away from the fovea. [S1]
- **Current agents (S2, Japan):** ranibizumab (and a biosimilar), aflibercept 2 mg and 8 mg, brolucizumab and faricimab, which targets both VEGF and angiopoietin-2. All improved vision in their pivotal trials, including TENAYA/LUCERNE (faricimab), HAWK/HARRIER (brolucizumab) and PULSAR (aflibercept 8 mg). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Brolucizumab has higher rates of retinal vasculitis and retinal vascular occlusion,** so intraocular inflammation must be found and managed early; inflammation can occur with every anti-VEGF agent. [S2]
- **Loading phase: monthly injections, usually three or four in a row depending on the drug,** followed by a maintenance phase to hold the vision gained. [S2]
- **Maintenance: treat-and-extend over as-needed (PRN).** PRN after loading lost vision over two years against monthly injections (CATT, HARBOR), and long-term PRN in practice often let vision slip; treat-and-extend matched fixed dosing and beat PRN, at the cost of more injections. There is no consensus on how long to continue it; fit the plan to the patient's circumstances and the fellow eye. [S2]
- **Switch anti-VEGF drug** when the response is inadequate or wanes (acquired tolerance), or to ease the treatment burden; for an inadequate response, adding PDT to anti-VEGF is another option. [S2]
- **PDT, usually with anti-VEGF, remains an option for PCV** (it improves vision and can fully regress polyps) and for disease resistant to anti-VEGF. Avoid long-term PDT with a thin choroid or existing macular atrophy, and do not use it for type 3 MNV. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Massive submacular haemorrhage causes sudden, irreversible loss.** Caught early, it can be displaced with intravitreal gas or vitrectomy, sometimes with anti-VEGF or off-label tPA, to improve vision. [S2]
- **Neovascular AMD cannot be cured and needs long-term management:** activity recurs, and the fellow eye often develops MNV. Eyes with scarring, very poor vision and little activity are better observed than treated further. [S2]
- **Judge activity on OCT by exudation** (intraretinal, subretinal or sub-RPE fluid, haemorrhage, fibrin) and scan the whole macula or lesion: the most active part (polyps in PCV, intraretinal vessels in type 3 MNV) may lie away from the fovea. [S2]

## Geographic atrophy (the other late form)

- **The guide notes no approved treatment for geographic atrophy;** review it every six to twelve months, with the interval set by vision, driving status and risk of progression, and offer low-vision care. It can convert to neovascular AMD, so teach Amsler-grid use. [S1]

## Early and intermediate AMD: supplements, self-monitoring, review

- **For intermediate AMD, the AREDS2 formula may help:** vitamin C 500 mg, vitamin E 400 IU, zinc 80 mg (as zinc oxide), copper 2 mg (as cupric oxide), lutein 10 mg and zeaxanthin 2 mg. ⚠️ That zinc dose is above the Australian and New Zealand upper intake limit, so discuss it with the GP. [S1]
- **S2 describes AREDS2 differently:** lutein/zeaxanthin in place of beta-carotene (which raises lung-cancer risk in smokers) and zinc cut from 80 mg to 25 mg, with no loss of benefit. Supplements do not stop early AMD becoming intermediate, and excess vitamins and zinc can cause harm. [S2]
- **Supplements are not advised** for early AMD, for normal age-related changes only, when both eyes already have late AMD, or for at-risk people without any AMD signs. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Teach home Amsler-grid monitoring at every visit,** and tell the patient to come in at once for distortion, central blur or vision loss. [S1]
- **Review without new symptoms:** early AMD at least every 12 months; intermediate AMD every 6 to 12 months (per the guide's review table). [S1]
- **Early or intermediate AMD does not need an ophthalmologist** unless there is a strong family history (possible inherited dystrophy), atypical findings including onset before age 50, or the patient wants to join a trial. [S1]
