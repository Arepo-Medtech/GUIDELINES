# Chronic breathlessness in advanced illness

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising, assessing and treating breathlessness (dyspnoea) that persists despite treatment of the underlying disease in advanced illness (cancer, COPD, heart failure and others): non-drug measures, opioids (morphine), benzodiazepines, corticosteroids, oxygen, equity and care settings, and carers. The source gives no doses. Breathlessness in the terminal phase: see `palliative-care-terminal-phase-medicines`. ## Recognise

- **Breathlessness is a subjective sense of respiratory discomfort** (air hunger, chest tightness, suffocation), shaped by gas exchange, anxiety and social circumstances such as isolation; anxiety amplifies it. [S1]
- **It is common:** about 70% of people with cancer, particularly thoracic cancer, and 50% to 100% of people with COPD or heart failure. [S1]
- **Chronic breathlessness syndrome** (international consensus definition): breathlessness that continues despite the best treatment of its underlying cause and leads to disability. ESMO puts its prevalence in cancer at 20% to 70%, highest in thoracic and advanced cancer and in the final weeks of life. [S2]

## Assess

- **Ask the patient to rate it** with the modified Borg scale, a visual analogue scale or the mMRC scale; track change over time. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Oxygen saturation and respiratory rate often do not match how breathless the patient feels;** people with advanced cancer can be severely breathless with normal saturation. [S1]
- **In dementia or cognitive impairment, look for non-verbal signs** (agitation, laboured breathing); the Respiratory Distress Observation Scale can help. [S1]
- **Screen every patient at each inpatient and outpatient visit (III, B)** with a single-dimension intensity scale of your choice, and ask which activities they have reduced or given up. Patients who avoid exertion often say they are not short of breath, so the symptom goes unseen. [S2]

## Non-drug measures first

- **A fan blowing cool air on the face eases air hunger within minutes;** it is cheap, non-invasive and works at home or in hospital. [S1]
- **Teach breathing retraining** (pursed-lip and diaphragmatic breathing) and **positioning** (sitting upright or leaning forward). [S1]
- **Mindfulness and relaxation may reduce the anxiety that worsens breathlessness;** acupuncture, acupressure and reflexology have emerging, mixed-quality evidence. [S1]
- **Graded non-drug measures:** refer to exercise-based rehabilitation, pulmonary or cardiac where lung or heart disease coexists (I, A). Consider a hand-held fan aimed at the face, with or without oxygen; breathing retraining or physiotherapy referral; a trial of a walking aid; and teaching pacing, relieving positions and distraction (each II, B). Offer acupressure or acupuncture if the patient wants it (II, C). [S2]

## Drug treatment

- ⚠️ **Opioids, especially morphine, are the mainstay when non-drug measures are not enough;** they lower the brain's perception of breathing discomfort, even with normal oxygen levels. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Start morphine low and titrate to response,** watching for sedation and constipation, and reassess the dose regularly because of the risk of respiratory depression. [S1]
- **Benzodiazepines (lorazepam, midazolam) are a second-line option when anxiety drives breathlessness;** direct benefit is inconsistent, short-acting agents are preferred, and combining them with opioids risks respiratory depression. [S1]
- **Corticosteroids such as dexamethasone help when inflammation or airway oedema contributes,** usually only short term because of immunosuppression, muscle wasting and hyperglycaemia. [S1]
- **Oxygen helps hypoxaemic patients but offers little to those with normal oxygen levels;** weigh any comfort against equipment burden, restricted movement and carer load. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Morphine dose (S2):** regular low-dose oral morphine is the first-choice drug when severe chronic breathlessness continues despite non-drug measures (II, B). For an opioid-naive patient, start scheduled morphine at 10–30 mg across 24 hours and titrate to symptoms (II, B), for example 5 to 15 mg of extended-release morphine twice a day, lower in renal failure. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **Australian licence:** the TGA has extended one extended-release oral morphine product (morphine sulfate pentahydrate) to chronic breathlessness at 10–30 mg per 24 hours. Extra immediate-release morphine is not to be given for the breathlessness itself, though coexisting pain may need it. [S2]
- **Already taking opioids for pain:** consider raising the baseline dose by 25%–50% (V, C). For severe breathlessness on exertion with predictable triggers, a dose taken before the activity may be considered, used sparingly (at most twice daily) and closely monitored, as long-term safety is unknown (II, C). [S2]
- **With every opioid start, offer a laxative, plus an antiemetic if needed (I, A);** teach safe opioid use and monitor over time (III, A). ESMO also allows a rescue immediate-release morphine dose of roughly one-sixth of the total daily dose if needed. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Benzodiazepines are not a first-line drug for breathlessness** because of sedation and delirium (III, D). Use them with caution for breathlessness with anxiety only when opioids have failed (V, C), or for palliative sedation in the last days of life when breathlessness is refractory (IV, C). [S2]
- **Corticosteroids may be tried when cancer-related breathlessness resists other treatment (II, C).** Do not give palliative oxygen when resting SpO2 is 90% or higher (II, D); if oxygen is trialled, review it within a few days and stop it when the patient notices no benefit. High-flow oxygen (especially in hypoxaemic respiratory failure) and a trial of non-invasive ventilation (especially in hypercapnic failure) may be considered (II, B). [S2]
- **Not recommended for breathlessness:** sertraline (II, D) and cannabinoids (IV, D); other antidepressants only within clinical trials (V, C). [S2]

## Access, settings and carers

- **Rural and remote patients may wait longer for opioids and oxygen;** CALD patients may decline opioids over addiction worries, so use interpreters and culturally tailored education. [S1]
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients:** access can be limited and conventional treatments less acceptable; combining culturally appropriate practices with medical care may improve uptake. [S1]
- **Teach carers fan use and repositioning, and support them emotionally;** breathlessness frightens carers and contributes to burnout. [S1]
- **Refer to a specialist multimodal breathlessness service where one exists (I, A);** otherwise consider timely referral to palliative care (II, B). Such services usually run 4–6 contacts spread across 4–6 weeks. [S2]
- **Assess the carer as well:** their psychological state, what information they need and who supports them (III, B); a structured carer needs-assessment tool may help (II, B). [S2]
