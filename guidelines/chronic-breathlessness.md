# Chronic breathlessness in advanced illness

## Recognise

- **Breathlessness is a subjective sense of respiratory discomfort** (air hunger, chest tightness, suffocation), shaped by gas exchange, anxiety and social circumstances such as isolation; anxiety amplifies it. [S1]
- **It is common:** about 70% of people with cancer, particularly thoracic cancer, and 50% to 100% of people with COPD or heart failure. [S1]

## Assess

- **Ask the patient to rate it** with the modified Borg scale, a visual analogue scale or the mMRC scale; track change over time. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Oxygen saturation and respiratory rate often do not match how breathless the patient feels;** people with advanced cancer can be severely breathless with normal saturation. [S1]
- **In dementia or cognitive impairment, look for non-verbal signs** (agitation, laboured breathing); the Respiratory Distress Observation Scale can help. [S1]

## Non-drug measures first

- **A fan blowing cool air on the face eases air hunger within minutes;** it is cheap, non-invasive and works at home or in hospital. [S1]
- **Teach breathing retraining** (pursed-lip and diaphragmatic breathing) and **positioning** (sitting upright or leaning forward). [S1]
- **Mindfulness and relaxation may reduce the anxiety that worsens breathlessness;** acupuncture, acupressure and reflexology have emerging, mixed-quality evidence. [S1]

## Drug treatment

- ⚠️ **Opioids, especially morphine, are the mainstay when non-drug measures are not enough;** they lower the brain's perception of breathing discomfort, even with normal oxygen levels. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Start morphine low and titrate to response,** watching for sedation and constipation, and reassess the dose regularly because of the risk of respiratory depression. [S1]
- **Benzodiazepines (lorazepam, midazolam) are a second-line option when anxiety drives breathlessness;** direct benefit is inconsistent, short-acting agents are preferred, and combining them with opioids risks respiratory depression. [S1]
- **Corticosteroids such as dexamethasone help when inflammation or airway oedema contributes,** usually only short term because of immunosuppression, muscle wasting and hyperglycaemia. [S1]
- **Oxygen helps hypoxaemic patients but offers little to those with normal oxygen levels;** weigh any comfort against equipment burden, restricted movement and carer load. [S1]

## Access, settings and carers

- **Rural and remote patients may wait longer for opioids and oxygen;** CALD patients may decline opioids over addiction worries, so use interpreters and culturally tailored education. [S1]
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients:** access can be limited and conventional treatments less acceptable; combining culturally appropriate practices with medical care may improve uptake. [S1]
- **Teach carers fan use and repositioning, and support them emotionally;** breathlessness frightens carers and contributes to burnout. [S1]
