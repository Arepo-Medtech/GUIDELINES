# Venous leg ulcer — assessment

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with or at risk of a venous leg ulcer: aetiology, comprehensive and vascular assessment before compression, ankle and toe brachial index interpretation, referral and healing expectations. This is the ASSESSMENT section only; compression, dressings and other treatment are not covered. PBS access is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **Venous ulcers are among the commonest lower leg ulcers:** about half of all hard-to-heal wounds and around 42% of leg ulcers seen in a typical Australian wound service. They affect all ages but more often older people. [S1]
- **They arise from sustained venous hypertension,** caused by venous obstruction (such as thrombosis), valve incompetence with reflux, or both. Post-thrombotic syndrome develops after up to 50% of DVTs. [S1]
- **Risk factors include genetics, prior varicose vein surgery, venous disease, phlebitis, DVT, heart failure, obesity, immobility and past leg trauma;** a weak calf pump, poor mobility and reduced ankle movement speed progression. Untreated venous disease worsens, so ulcers often recur. [S1]

## Assess before treating

- **Assess every person with a lower leg ulcer comprehensively at the start and repeatedly after,** to establish the cause, shape the plan and referrals, and track outcomes. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Establish the ulcer's cause before starting compression.** Compression can injure a critically ischaemic limb, and it can worsen ulcers of other causes such as pyoderma gangrenosum. [S1]
- **Every patient with a lower leg ulcer needs an initial vascular assessment** (vascular history and examination of the lower leg). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Screen for peripheral arterial disease when there are risk factors, a non-healing ulcer or a suggestive history or wound:** at minimum, palpate pulses and do an ABI, TBI or absolute systolic toe pressure (unless contraindicated). Transcutaneous oxygen or Doppler waveforms can be added as indicated. [S1]
- **Also examine the skin, sensation, oedema and mobility, and describe venous signs with the CEAP classification;** severity scales can track them over time. [S1]
- **Assess leg pain, and wound pain when an ulcer is open, with a validated tool;** assess quality of life with a validated tool, and screen nutrition and hydration. [S1]
- **Assess the wound fully at baseline and again at every dressing procedure:** history, healing phase, size, bed, edge, surrounding skin, exudate and signs of infection. [S1]

## Ankle and toe brachial index

- **ABI (handheld Doppler) is the usual point-of-care arterial screen;** TBI may be more accurate and is needed when vessels are calcified. The aims are to diagnose and follow PAD and to keep compression away from limbs with inadequate arterial supply. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Compression is not simply banned by any sign of PAD:** the ESVS guideline contraindicates sustained compression only below ABI 0.6, ankle pressure 60 mmHg or toe pressure 30 mmHg (PAD itself being ABI below 0.8). Guidelines allow cautious compression at ABI 0.5 to 0.8 when there are no arterial signs or symptoms. [S1]
- **TBI is more sensitive than ABI, especially with diabetes or kidney disease;** a TBI above 0.7 is normal and a reasonable threshold for applying compression. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Suspect calcified, incompressible arteries with weak or absent pulses or an ABI above 1.4 (above 1.3 with diabetes or chronic kidney disease):** do a TBI or toe pressure, or refer. Never diagnose PAD from one test alone, and repeat the vascular assessment regularly, with new symptoms or when healing stalls. [S1]
- **Duplex ultrasound is the investigation of choice** for vascular cause, site and extent, but needs specialist skills and is unavailable in most wound care settings. [S1]

## Refer

- **Refer to a vascular expert:** anyone with venous symptoms or an active ulcer never reviewed by one; anyone whose screening shows significant PAD; and anyone in a setting where PAD screening cannot be done. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Refer for vascular investigation at ABI below 0.6, TBI below 0.5 or toe pressure below 70 mmHg;** consider it at ABI below 0.9, TBI below 0.6, toe pressure below 95 mmHg or biphasic/monophasic Doppler waveforms. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Urgent:** a traumatic leg injury in someone with chronic venous insufficiency (DVT risk) goes to a vascular expert or wound clinic promptly. Also investigate thigh-level oedema and unequal arm systolic pressures. [S1]
- **Don't hold up initial care while waiting for multidisciplinary input;** refer anyone at nutritional risk to a dietitian. [S1]

## Expect healing

- ⚠️ **Re-examine the diagnosis if an ulcer has not healed by 25–40% within 2–4 weeks of best-practice care;** less than 25% healing at two weeks is an early warning of delay. [S1]
- **Perfusion adequate for healing (consensus):** a palpable dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial pulse, ABI above 0.6 and below 1.4, toe pressure 30 to 35 mmHg and an audible triphasic or biphasic Doppler signal. These markers need more validation. [S1]
