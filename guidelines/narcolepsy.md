# Narcolepsy and cataplexy

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising narcolepsy type 1 (with cataplexy or low CSF orexin) and type 2 in adults, the ICSD-3-TR diagnostic pathway and its Australian testing constraints, non-drug measures, drug choice as the PBS shapes it, cataplexy treatment, pregnancy, and emerging orexin-agonist therapy. Idiopathic hypersomnia is mentioned only in passing. The source is a narrative review, not a graded guideline. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **Narcolepsy is a central hypersomnolence disorder.** Its core is excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), accompanied to a variable degree by cataplexy, hallucinations on falling asleep or waking, sleep paralysis and broken night sleep. [S1]
- **Onset has two peaks:** one in adolescence and a second in the third decade; a small number are diagnosed as children. [S1]
- ⚠️ **In a patient with EDS, cataplexy is pathognomonic of narcolepsy type one (NT1):** a sudden loss of voluntary muscle tone while the person stays fully conscious. [S1]
- **Screen for common comorbidities:** depression, anxiety and ADHD lead the list, then metabolic problems (overweight, obesity, diabetes) and other sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), restless legs and REM sleep behaviour disorder. [S1]
- **Keep the differential for sleepiness wide:** insufficient sleep, circadian rhythm disorders and shift work, OSA and psychiatric illness can each mimic narcolepsy or coexist with it. [S1]
- **Diagnosis is often late, and sometimes wrong.** The median delay from first symptoms is 6.0 to 10.5 years, yet in one UK series half of those referred as narcolepsy did not have it on formal assessment. [S1]

## Diagnose

- **ICSD-3-TR criteria:** ongoing EDS plus either a multiple sleep latency test (MSLT) that meets the diagnostic threshold, or a qualifying CSF orexin level. An MSLT is an overnight polysomnogram followed by, usually, five daytime nap opportunities. [S1]
- **Separate NT1 from NT2:** NT1 needs cataplexy or a low CSF orexin. The distinction matters because NT1 is lifelong, while narcolepsy without cataplexy can remit. [S1]
- **Clear cataplexy plus a sleep-onset REM period on the overnight study is now enough for NT1**, without also needing a mean MSLT sleep latency of 8 min or less. [S1]
- ⚠️ **An MSLT cannot diagnose on its own.** Rule out other causes of short sleep latency first (sleep deprivation, medicines, shift work), and remember antidepressants can suppress REM and hide sleep-onset REM periods. [S1]
- **CSF orexin testing is available to Australians but hard to get:** it needs a lumbar puncture, the sample goes overseas through selected pathology companies, and Medicare does not cover it, so the patient pays. [S1]
- **HLA typing is not a diagnostic criterion.** Its negative predictive value for NT1 exceeds 99%, so a negative result can argue against NT1 when the picture is atypical. [S1]

## Treat

- **Non-drug measures support drug treatment:** a regular sleep schedule with planned short daytime naps for sleepiness, plus psychological support where needed, regular exercise and a balanced diet. [S1]
- **Match the drug to the symptom profile, comorbidities and patient preference.** Many narcolepsy drugs can be misused and are tightly regulated. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Australia differs on first-line drug for sleepiness.** European and AASM guidance prefers modafinil (with strong evidence also for pitolisant and solriamfetol, neither available here). Australian guidance lists modafinil or armodafinil, with dexamfetamine second; but the PBS requires a contraindication to dexamfetamine before it subsidises modafinil or armodafinil, making dexamfetamine the effective first subsidised option. [S1]
- **In New Zealand**, modafinil, dexamfetamine and methylphenidate are funded for narcolepsy through Pharmac Special Authority. Lisdexamfetamine may help, but both countries fund it only for ADHD. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Cataplexy:** sodium oxybate is the international first choice, but it is not on the PBS or the ARTG and is costly via the Special Access Scheme (Western Australia's state formulary is the exception). Antidepressants are used against cataplexy; clomipramine is the only one the PBS lists for it. [S1]

## Pregnancy

- ⚠️ **Plan for pregnancy.** European guidance strongly advises stopping narcolepsy drugs before conception, accepting that symptoms will worsen, or using a single drug if stopping is not practical. Modafinil and armodafinil carry higher rates of congenital anomalies and make the oral contraceptive pill less effective. [S1]
- **Dexamfetamine in pregnancy:** an Australian ADHD cohort found continued use was not linked to worse maternal or neonatal outcomes, though complications were somewhat lower in the unexposed group. [S1]
- **Counselling on pregnancy and contraception is often poor** and should be improved. [S1]

## Emerging treatment

- **Orexin receptor (OX2R) agonists are the coming change.** Oveporexton has reduced sleepiness and cataplexy and improved wakefulness testing in NT1, with trials under way in Australia; an earlier agent was stopped for liver toxicity. Immunotherapy has little evidence and carries risk. [S1]
