# Non-infectious uveitis: corticosteroids by route, and when to add steroid-sparing therapy

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** specialist (ophthalmologist-led) use of topical, periocular, intravitreal and systemic corticosteroids for non-infectious uveitis in adults, the contraindications to check first, and the point at which immunosuppressants or biologics are added. Diagnosis of uveitis type and infectious uveitis are not covered. ## Why it matters, and what to exclude first

- **Uveitis accounts for roughly 10% of visual impairment in developed countries,** and its complications (cataract, glaucoma, macular oedema) make prompt treatment essential. [S1]
- **Corticosteroids are the foundation of treatment for non-infectious uveitis,** and can be given as drops, by local injection or systemically. [S1]
- ⚠️ **No steroid drops if the cornea is infected** — herpes simplex, bacterial or fungal keratitis — because local immune suppression can make a sight-threatening infection worse. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Systemic steroids are contraindicated in active bacterial, fungal or viral infection, including TB,** since they can reactivate latent TB. Tuberculous uveitis may still get oral steroid once effective anti-TB treatment has begun. [S1]
- **Before systemic steroids, take a full history** (osteoporosis, uncontrolled diabetes or hypertension, peptic ulcer, heart failure, hypersensitivity) **and screen for latent infection such as TB and syphilis.** [S1]

## Anterior uveitis: steroid drops

- **Drops are the standard for anterior-segment inflammation;** anterior uveitis is the commonest form and is usually treated this way. [S1]
- **Most drops barely reach the back of the eye,** so intermediate, posterior and panuveitis need another route. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Severe anterior inflammation (anterior-chamber cells 3+ or more): a potent steroid such as prednisolone acetate 1%, as often as one drop hourly at first,** then a gradual taper. [S1]
- **Start tapering once anterior-chamber cells improve.** SUN defines improvement as a fall of two grades (for example 3+ to 1+) or to inactive (trace to 0). Aim for no inflammation, or minimal inflammation without relapse, on the fewest drops. [S1]
- **For chronic anterior uveitis, a gentler maintenance drop is advised:** fluorometholone 0.1%, one drop twice a day. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Avoid steroid drops in known steroid responders or primary open-angle glaucoma,** and with corneal thinning or perforation; long use raises IOP and can cause irreversible optic-nerve damage. [S1]

## Periocular and intravitreal steroids (including the dexamethasone implant)

- **Consider local injection** to add to systemic treatment or replace it when contraindicated, when adherence to drops or tablets is poor, and for severe one-sided uveitis or cystoid macular oedema that has not responded. [S1]
- **Match the route to the time needed:** for control under 3 months, periocular or intravitreal betamethasone, dexamethasone or triamcinolone; for control beyond 3 months, an intravitreal steroid implant. [S1]
- **For uveitic macular oedema, intravitreal treatment may be the better first choice:** in the POINT trial, intravitreal triamcinolone and the dexamethasone implant both beat periocular triamcinolone, at the cost of slightly more mild IOP rises. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Implants carry intraocular risks — cataract, glaucoma and infection — so monitor for them.** The longer-acting fluocinolone implant (Retisert) causes more steroid complications. [S1]

## Systemic steroids

- ⚠️ **Use systemic steroids when sight is threatened** (retinal vasculitis, retinal detachment, choroidal neovascularisation, corrected acuity 6/60 or worse, or no view of the fundus), **when local therapy fails, or for bilateral or systemic disease** such as sarcoidosis, Behçet disease or Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada. [S1]
- **Oral prednisone is the usual first choice, starting at 0.5 to 1 mg/kg/day by severity,** then tapered to response. [S1]
- **In severe uveitis, consider IV methylprednisolone pulses of 500–1000 mg a day for three days,** followed by a gradually reducing oral dose. [S1]
- **Taper in ever-smaller steps to limit relapse.** For long-term use, 7.5 mg a day is regarded as the safer maintenance dose. [S1]

## Steroid-sparing therapy

- ⚠️ **If a safe maintenance steroid dose cannot be reached, add an immunosuppressant or a biologic.** Methotrexate, azathioprine or mycophenolate spare steroid; TNF inhibitors come in when conventional agents fail or are not tolerated. [S1]
