# Hepatic encephalopathy in cirrhosis

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with cirrhosis and hepatic encephalopathy (HE): recognition, grading, covert HE testing, lactulose and rifaximin, shunt embolisation, transplant referral, and the nutrition that goes with it. This is the HE section of a wider GESA portal hypertension consensus; varices, ascites and other complications are not covered here. Drug-induced or other causes of encephalopathy are out of scope. PBS access is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **HE is one of the events that define decompensated cirrhosis,** along with ascites, variceal bleeding and non-obstructive jaundice; decompensation markedly shortens survival without a transplant. [S1]
- **HE is diagnosed clinically:** neuropsychiatric features from subtle cognitive change through to coma, split into covert and overt HE and graded by the West Haven criteria. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not measure ammonia routinely.** Although ammonia is part of the mechanism, the blood level rarely helps establish the diagnosis or its severity. [S1]
- **West Haven split:** covert HE takes in minimal HE and grade 1, found only on psychometric testing or as a short attention span and slight unawareness; overt HE is grades 2–4, with lethargy, dyspraxia, asterixis, confusion and disorientation. [S2]

## Test for covert HE

- **Covert HE tests** include PHES, the Stroop test, EEG, critical flicker frequency, the inhibitory control test and continuous reaction time. PHES and Stroop need no major equipment, though PHES has no Australian norms. [S1]
- **For quick screening, use the animal naming test or QuickStroop:** each takes less than a minute. [S1]

## Treat overt HE

- ⚠️ **Start lactulose as soon as overt HE is suspected;** it is the first-choice treatment. It is cheap, is not absorbed, and lowers the ammonia load in the gut. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Find and treat the trigger:** constipation, infection, gastrointestinal bleeding, electrolyte disturbance, dehydration, and sedatives or opioids are the usual ones. Leaving a precipitant untreated makes recurrent or chronic HE more likely. Patients and carers adjust lactulose, keep up fluids and avoid constipating drugs. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Add rifaximin 550 mg twice daily** if lactulose is not tolerated or overt HE recurs on lactulose. It works on gut bacteria to cut ammonia production, is barely absorbed, and is well tolerated enough for long-term use. [S1]
- ⚠️ **After overt HE, recurrence runs up to 40% a year and is highest in the first months.** Risk is higher with prior minimal HE, Child–Pugh B or C cirrhosis, sarcopenia, older age and continued drinking; follow up regularly for cognition, triggers and adherence, and support protein intake and abstinence from alcohol. [S2]
- **Branched-chain amino acids can be an adjunct** within nutritional supplementation. [S1]
- **Look for a large spontaneous portosystemic shunt** (present in up to 28% of people with cirrhosis); radiological embolisation reduces HE admissions in carefully chosen patients. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Refer for liver transplant assessment** when overt HE persists with poor quality of life despite optimal treatment. [S1]

## Nutrition, sarcopenia and frailty

- **Malnutrition, sarcopenia and frailty are common and each independently predicts death;** treat sarcopenia as part of HE care. BMI under 18.5 kg/m2 or decompensated cirrhosis marks high malnutrition risk; otherwise screen quickly, e.g. with the Royal Free Hospital tool (fluid overload, intake, weight loss). [S1]
- **All decompensated patients with sarcopenia should see an accredited dietitian** for a high-energy, high-protein diet; start oral supplements promptly if diet alone falls short. [S1]
- **Protein target: 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day by ideal body weight,** and in HE branched-chain amino acids can make up 0.2–0.25 g/kg/day of it. Eat often, include a late-evening snack with carbohydrate and protein, and avoid fasting. [S1]
- **If an oral diet cannot meet needs in a malnourished patient,** consider enteral feeding for at least 4 weeks to rebuild nutrition and muscle strength. [S1]

## Surgery

- **Encephalopathy is among the reasons surgery goes worse in cirrhosis.** Stratify risk with MELD, Child–Pugh, VOCAL-Penn and HVPG plus multidisciplinary review, and talk early to a centre with hepatology, surgery, ICU and interventional radiology when risk is not low. [S1]

## Driving

- **HE impairs the skills driving needs** (attention, visuospatial perception, reaction time, executive function); on the road, patients struggle with car control and lane keeping, and they have more crashes and traffic violations than people with cirrhosis without HE. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **Australian rules:** the Austroads fitness-to-drive standard says cognitive fluctuation in liver failure does not usually change licence status, but advises against driving while cognitive impairment is likely to affect vehicle control. In South Australia and the Northern Territory clinicians must notify the licensing authority of an unfit driver; elsewhere reporting is not mandatory, but there is an ethical duty to advise the patient. [S2]
- ⚠️ **No driving during an episode of overt HE.** When assessing return to driving, ask about the episode, how many there have been, triggers, adherence to HE treatment and past crashes, and get collateral history from family or carers. [S2]
- **Low risk (a first episode with a clear, reversible trigger that has been treated): no driving for 3 months,** then a conditional licence if there has been no further HE, the cirrhosis is stable and medicines are taken. [S2]
- **Moderate risk (a second episode, each with an obvious reversible trigger): no driving for at least 6 months,** then a conditional licence only with no recurrence, stable cirrhosis under regular review, adherence, a normal EEG and a passed occupational therapy driving assessment. [S2]
- ⚠️ **High risk (three or more episodes, unprovoked HE, or chronic HE): the authors advise permanent loss of the licence.** Commercial licences are decided case by case, with a more cautious return. [S2]
- **Covert HE:** patients often lack insight into their driving, and no simple, widely available test sorts safe from unsafe drivers. The inhibitory control test is hard to access in Australia and the standard paper tests do not predict crashes, but a Stroop score above 190 (smartphone app) has tracked crashes in simulator studies. [S2]
- **Evidence does not support taking the licence away for covert HE alone;** tell patients and families about the risk and about progression to overt HE, and decide case by case. [S2]
- **Weigh safety against the cost of losing a licence** (independence, access to care, work, mental health), which falls hardest in regional and remote Australia. Reassess regularly, teach patients and families to recognise HE, and involve the GP. [S2]
