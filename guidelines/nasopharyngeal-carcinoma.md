## Recognise and diagnose

- **NPC behaves differently from other head and neck SCCs.** Risk factors are EBV, genetics, smoking, alcohol and wood-dust work; it is endemic in Southeast Asia and uncommon in Europe. Men are affected two to three times as often. [S1]
- **A neck lump is often the first sign.** Confirm the diagnosis by endoscopic biopsy of the nasopharyngeal primary; if nothing is visible, biopsy tissue that is positive on MRI or PET. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Avoid open neck biopsy or node dissection:** it lowers the chance of cure and adds late harm. If a node sample is unavoidable, prefer ultrasound-guided core biopsy or dissection without capsule breach. Test the tissue for EBV (EBER in situ hybridisation). [S1]
- **EBV DNA screening is recommended only in endemic regions for higher-risk people** (men aged 40–62). [S1]

## Stage

- **Work-up:** head and neck examination with endoscopy and cranial nerves; MRI of nasopharynx, skull base and neck (request cranial-nerve imaging); bloods; baseline hearing, dental, nutrition, eye and endocrine checks; and pretreatment plasma EBV DNA, which adds prognostic value to AJCC 8th edition staging. [S1]
- **Add FDG-PET/CT for N3 disease, suspected metastases or EBV DNA of 4000 copies/mL or more**, as these patients have a high risk of distant spread. [S1]

## Early and locally advanced disease

- **Radiotherapy (IMRT) is the core of treatment,** planned by a multidisciplinary team in a high-volume centre. Treat 70 Gy in 33–35 fractions over 7 weeks to tumour and involved nodes, 50–60 Gy to at-risk sites, and include both sides of the neck and the retropharyngeal nodes. [S1]
- **Stage I–II: radiotherapy alone.** Consider concurrent chemotherapy in N1 or bulky T2 disease or when EBV DNA exceeds 4000 copies per mL. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Stage III–IVA: concurrent cisplatin 100 mg/m² every 3 weeks with radiotherapy is standard;** weekly cisplatin or carboplatin if that is contraindicated. [S1]
- **Induction gemcitabine–cisplatin before chemoradiation** improved relapse-free, distant relapse-free and overall survival in high-risk locally advanced disease, at the cost of more acute toxicity; choose patients so it does not compromise chemoradiation. [S1]
- **Adjuvant chemotherapy after chemoradiation is controversial;** adjuvant capecitabine has shown relapse-free and overall survival benefit in high-risk disease. [S1]

## Recurrent and metastatic disease

- **Locoregional recurrence may still be curable:** consider surgery (including endoscopic nasopharyngectomy for small rT1–2 recurrences) or carefully selected re-irradiation in a high-volume centre. Nasopharyngectomy is contraindicated with carotid or intracranial invasion. [S1]
- ⚠️ **First line for recurrent or metastatic NPC: gemcitabine–cisplatin plus an anti-PD-1 antibody** (toripalimab, camrelizumab or tislelizumab trials). With toripalimab (JUPITER-02), overall survival also improved. [S1]
- **Gemcitabine–cisplatin is the chemotherapy backbone,** superior to cisplatin–fluorouracil; for de novo metastatic disease responding to chemotherapy, adding locoregional radiotherapy improves survival. [S1]
- **No second-line standard exists.** Several chemotherapies and checkpoint inhibitors have activity, but pembrolizumab did not beat chemotherapy in KEYNOTE-122. Oligometastatic patients may benefit from local treatment after a good chemotherapy response. [S1]

## Follow-up

- **First response assessment 3 months after treatment** (PET is more specific than MRI after radiotherapy). Then review every 3 months in year one, every 6 months in years two and three, and yearly to year five, with nasopharyngoscopy, neck and cranial nerve checks. [S1]
- **At least yearly plasma EBV DNA, thyroid function (after neck radiotherapy) and chest imaging;** watch for late effects such as xerostomia, trismus, hearing loss, temporal lobe necrosis and cranial neuropathy. [S1]
