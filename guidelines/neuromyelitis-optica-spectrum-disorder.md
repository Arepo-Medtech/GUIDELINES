# Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (AQP4-IgG-positive NMOSD): maintenance biologics

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising NMOSD and choosing, combining, switching and monitoring the approved maintenance biologics (eculizumab, inebilizumab, satralizumab) in AQP4-IgG-seropositive NMOSD, including vaccination, infection, pregnancy and comorbidity. S1 does not cover acute relapse treatment, diagnostic criteria or AQP4-IgG-negative or MOG-antibody disease; S2 adds acute attack treatment, rituximab and ravulizumab. Diagnostic criteria and MOG-antibody disease remain out of scope. Related: `multiple-sclerosis` (the main differential). PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

## Recognise

- **NMOSD is a rare, disabling autoimmune disease of the central nervous system**, mainly causing optic neuritis and longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis. Prevalence ranges from 0.7 to 10 per 100,000, varying with ethnicity and region, and women are affected more often. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not mistake NMOSD for multiple sclerosis.** It is a separate disease, although the features overlap and misdiagnosis as MS happens. [S1]
- **About 90% test positive for AQP4-IgG**, a pathogenic circulating antibody and the key diagnostic marker. Lasting problems include loss of vision, weakness, numbness, bladder and bowel problems, nerve pain and fatigue. [S1]
- **Treat NMOSD in specialised reference centres** (79.5% agreement), because diagnostic error and misinformation are common. [S2]
- **AQP4-IgG-negative patients who meet the clinical and imaging criteria need re-evaluation for other diagnoses,** including MS and MOG-antibody disease (MOGAD), which is a separate entity outside this guidance. [S2]

## Acute attacks (Brazilian guideline)

- ⚠️ **Treat every attack as early as possible with high-dose IV methylprednisolone** (97.7% agreement), usually 1 g daily for 3 to 7 days; oral corticosteroid at 1 mg/kg/day with an individual taper often follows. Full recovery is uncommon. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Plasma exchange (at least 5 cycles), with or without methylprednisolone, as early as possible whatever the AQP4 status** (86.4%): first line in severe attacks, or on alternate days for 5 to 7 cycles when the steroid response is incomplete, ideally within 5 days of onset. Fewer than 5% improved when it was delayed 3 weeks. [S2]
- **IVIG is not indicated for NMOSD attacks** (75% agreement): it is less effective than steroids or plasma exchange. [S2]

## Maintenance therapy: the options

- **Treatment has two parts:** managing acute attacks, and maintenance therapy to prevent relapse. Before 2019, all maintenance drugs were off-label: rituximab, azathioprine, mycophenolate, methotrexate, tocilizumab and oral corticosteroids. [S1]
- **Eculizumab (a C5 complement inhibitor):** in the PREVENT trial, adjudicated relapse at about 211 weeks was 3% on eculizumab versus 43% on placebo. [S1]
- **Inebilizumab (anti-CD19):** in N-MOmentum, attacks in AQP4-IgG-positive patients were 11% on inebilizumab versus 42% on placebo, over up to 197 days. [S1]
- **Satralizumab (anti-IL-6 receptor), for adults and adolescents 12 and over:** relapse was 11% as add-on versus 43% on placebo (SAkuraSky), and 22% as monotherapy versus 57% on placebo (SAkuraStar). [S1]
- **Rituximab (S2):** recommended at diagnosis, after the first attack, or after other treatments fail, in adults and children regardless of AQP4 status (77.3%). In the RIN-1 trial (375 mg/m2 weekly for 4 weeks, then 2 g every 6 months), 37% on placebo relapsed and none on rituximab. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Ravulizumab (S2):** recommended with eculizumab, inebilizumab and satralizumab as monotherapy for AQP4-IgG-positive adults at diagnosis, after a first attack or after treatment failure (95.5%). In CHAMPION-NMOSD, 58 adults received IV loading doses of 2,400 to 3,000 mg and then 3,000 to 3,600 mg every 8 weeks, with no relapses, although the trial was single-arm with an external comparator. [S2]

## Choosing and starting

- **Choose on efficacy and safety from trials first**, then relapse severity, the patient's preference for how the drug is given, and comorbidities. How much safety risk the patient accepts also matters. [S1]
- **Practical differences (S2):** ravulizumab is an infusion every 8 weeks instead of eculizumab's fortnightly (biweekly) dosing; 6-monthly inebilizumab suits people far from a centre; monthly subcutaneous satralizumab can be self-injected at home. Eculizumab, ravulizumab and inebilizumab are also approved for generalised myasthenia gravis, and IL-6 receptor blockers may suit coexisting rheumatoid arthritis, giant cell arteritis or systemic sclerosis. [S2]
- **Dosing schedules differ, which shapes preference:** eculizumab is an IV infusion weekly at first then every 2 weeks; inebilizumab is two IV infusions 2 weeks apart, then one every 6 months; satralizumab is a subcutaneous injection every 2 weeks at first, then every 4 weeks. [S1]
- ⚠️ **After a treatment fails, pick one that works differently.** Satralizumab may not help after tocilizumab (both target the IL-6 receptor), and inebilizumab is similar to rituximab (B-cell depletion); consider eculizumab instead. [S1]
- **Leave patients who have no relapses on their present treatment where they are:** the biologic trials excluded stable, non-relapsing patients, so there is no evidence for switching. [S1]
- **With ongoing disease activity on azathioprine or mycophenolate, add more treatment.** About one-third respond poorly to these, especially after severe attacks, and stopping them can raise relapse risk even after 5 years of remission. [S1]
- **Long-term oral steroids** raise infection risk and worsen diabetes, osteoporosis and glaucoma, so moving earlier to a biologic may be appropriate. With a second autoimmune disease, let the more active disease guide the choice. [S1]

## Monotherapy, combination and switching

- **Use the biologic on its own where possible**, because combining it with immunosuppressants may increase infection risk. Clinicians may keep an immunosuppressant (for example, for another autoimmune condition), but should otherwise taper it once the biologic has started. [S1]
- **Do not combine monoclonal antibodies** (95.5% agreement): there is not enough evidence on safety or efficacy, and the trials tested monotherapy. [S2]
- **Before switching, allow enough time for the first drug to start working.** The commonest reasons to switch are severe relapse and adverse events. When switching, consider how the previous drug works and how long its effect lasts; trials used 24–48 weeks of follow-up. [S1]

## Safety and monitoring

- ⚠️ **Infection is the main long-term safety concern:** watch especially for opportunistic infections, meningococcal meningitis, shingles (herpes zoster) and progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy. Monitor more closely with infection-risk comorbidities, adolescents, older people, pregnancy and heavy immunosuppression. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Bring all vaccines up to date before starting**, not only meningococcal. For eculizumab, labels ask for meningococcal vaccines at least 2 weeks before the first dose, and some countries also require antibiotic cover. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Before lifelong preventive treatment (S2):** bring vaccines up to date (100% agreement) and screen for hepatitis B and tuberculosis. Before B-cell depletion give hepatitis B, pneumococcal, influenza and recombinant zoster vaccines. No live vaccines during immunosuppressive treatment. [S2]
- **Monitoring on preventive treatment (S2):** give infusions in centres able to manage reactions; watch for infections (especially respiratory and urinary) and check liver enzymes, full blood count with neutrophils and platelets, and immunoglobulin levels to catch cytopenia and hypogammaglobulinaemia; on IL-6 blockade also check lipids, and fibrinogen when needed. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **Satralizumab in Australia (S3):** approval covers only adults who are AQP4-antibody positive; efficacy and safety under 18 years and in pregnancy are uncertain. Monitor the white cell count (neutrophils can fall) and liver enzymes, and withhold treatment if needed, especially during infection. Rotate injection sites between abdomen and thighs. [S3]
- **Pregnancy:** involve a multidisciplinary team. Eculizumab data show no major safety concerns; evidence for inebilizumab and satralizumab is less clear, and inebilizumab in the second or third trimester may cause transient blood abnormalities in the baby. [S1]
- **Biomarkers are promising but not yet ready:** a raised serum GFAP predicted attacks and serum NfL after an attack tracked disability in N-MOmentum, but more evidence is needed. [S1]
