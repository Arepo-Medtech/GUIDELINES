# Panic disorder in adults

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with panic disorder, with or without agoraphobia: recognition and diagnosis, a panic attack presenting to the emergency department, stepped care from self-help to CBT and antidepressants, specialist referral, and monitoring. Generalised anxiety disorder: see `generalised-anxiety-disorder`. Children and adolescents: see `anxiety-children`. Comorbid depression: see `major-depressive-disorder`. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ⚠️ International guideline (NICE CG113, 2011, updated 2020). Australian practice follows the PBS listings below and may differ.

> ⚠️ **Benzodiazepines: NICE and the PBS disagree.** NICE says benzodiazepines should not be prescribed for panic disorder because long-term outcomes are worse. The PBS nonetheless lists alprazolam for panic disorder, alongside sertraline and paroxetine.

> ⚠️ **Why not the Australian guideline?** The RANZCP guideline for panic disorder, social anxiety disorder and GAD (Andrews et al., ANZJP 2018) has been removed from the RANZCP library. RANZCP says it is 'no longer considered current guidance' and is kept for reference use only. It is also only reachable via the publisher, which blocked automated access, so it was not used. NICE CG113 is current, but NICE's September 2026 review concluded that an update is needed and has not scheduled one. Most of its panic recommendations date from 2004. UK licensing and off-label notes do not transfer to Australia.

## Recognise and assess

- **Take a full history:** the person's background, whether they self-medicate, and any cultural or personal factors that will shape care. No self-report screening tool is validated well enough to recommend, so rely on consultation skills. [S1]
- **Expect comorbidity, especially depression and substance misuse.** Before an SSRI, ask about cocaine use (interactions) and do not stack serotonergic drugs. [S1]
- **Agree with the person which problem to treat first**; a timeline of when each problem began helps set priorities among comorbidities. [S1]
- **A single panic attack is not panic disorder.** In the emergency department, chest pain is more likely to be panic disorder when coronary disease is absent and the person is female or relatively young. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Panic attack in the emergency department:** ask whether they are already being treated, do only the minimum tests needed to exclude an acute physical cause, usually do not admit, and refer back to primary care with written information explaining why. [S1]

## Treat: stepped care

- **Mild to moderate panic disorder: start with low-intensity help,** either self-help alone or self-help with facilitator support. Offer information on support groups and discuss exercise. [S1]
- **Moderate to severe panic disorder, with agoraphobia or without it: consider referral for CBT,** or for an antidepressant when the problem is long-standing or psychological treatment has been refused or has not helped. [S1]
- **How to deliver CBT:** by trained, supervised therapists who stick to evidence-based protocols; 7 to 14 hours in total, usually as weekly sessions of 1 to 2 hours, finished within 4 months. [S1]
- **Brief CBT** should total about 7 hours and be built around structured self-help materials and focused tasks. [S1]

## Medicines

- ⚠️ **Do not prescribe benzodiazepines for panic disorder** (worse long-term outcome), and do not use sedating antihistamines or antipsychotics either. [S1]
- **Antidepressants are the only drugs for longer-term treatment.** SSRIs, SNRIs and tricyclics all have supporting evidence. [S1]
- **Offer an SSRI first**, unless there is a reason not to. If an SSRI is unsuitable, or a 12-week course has not helped, imipramine or clomipramine can be considered. [S1]
- **Choosing the drug:** weigh age, past response, accidental and deliberate overdose risk (highest with tricyclics), tolerability, interactions, the person's preference, and cost where drugs work equally well. [S1]
- **Warn before starting:** side effects, including a short-lived rise in anxiety early on; a delayed onset of benefit; and withdrawal symptoms if doses are missed, reduced or stopped suddenly. Give written information. [S1]
- **Start low and titrate slowly;** doses at the top of the range are sometimes needed. Once the person improves, keep going for 6 months or more from reaching the best dose, then taper. [S1]
- **No improvement after 12 weeks:** switch to an antidepressant from the other class, if a drug is still appropriate, or to another form of therapy. [S1]
- **Stopping:** reduce the dose gradually over a long period. Common withdrawal symptoms are dizziness, numbness and tingling, nausea and vomiting, headache, sweating, anxiety and poor sleep. If they are severe, restart the drug (or a longer-acting one of the same class) and taper more slowly. [S1]

## Specialist care

- **Refer to specialist mental health services after 2 interventions** (any mix of psychological therapy, medication or bibliotherapy) if significant symptoms remain. [S1]
- **Specialist reassessment** covers previous treatments and adherence, substance use (nicotine, alcohol, caffeine, recreational drugs), comorbidities, daily functioning, social networks, ongoing stressors and agoraphobic avoidance, with a full risk assessment and plan. [S1]

## Monitor

- ⚠️ **Review a new medicine within 2 weeks, then at 4, 6 and 12 weeks.** At 12 weeks decide whether to continue or change; if continuing, review every 8 to 12 weeks. [S1]
- **People on self-help** should have primary-care contact, probably every 4 to 8 weeks, and progress should be tracked with short self-rated questionnaires (for example, the agoraphobic mobility inventory's panic subscale). [S1]
