# Atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), excluding TTP, STEC-HUS and secondary causes, genetic testing, supportive care, C5-inhibitor treatment and vaccination, monitoring, dose spacing and discontinuation, and aHUS in children, pregnancy and kidney transplantation. ## Recognise

- **aHUS is an ultra-rare cause of TMA:** non-immune haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia and organ involvement, most often acute kidney injury (AKI). A complement-gene variant or factor H (CFH) autoantibodies are found in 60–70% of patients. [S1]
- **Suspect it from the TMA triad:** microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia (absolute, or progressive platelet consumption) and organ damage. [S1]
- **Look beyond the kidney:** it can affect the brain (confusion, seizures, coma), gut, pancreas and liver, lungs (alveolar haemorrhage), eyes, skin (ischaemic necrosis) and heart. [S1]
- ⚠️ **The presentation can be subacute:** renal impairment and hypertension, with TMA only on biopsy and no systemic haemolysis or thrombocytopenia. Consider TMA in any patient with kidney injury and low-grade haemolysis. (Grade 1B) [S1]
- **TMA can be triggered** by pregnancy or the postpartum period, severe hypertension, autoimmune disease, drugs, infection and solid-organ transplant. A trigger may coexist with aHUS, unmask it, or follow from it, so keep aHUS in the differential even when a trigger is found. [S2]

## Diagnose

- **aHUS is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion:** first rule out TTP, STEC-HUS and secondary TMA. [S1]
- **ADAMTS13 activity above 10% rules out TTP;** with negative STEC tests, suspect aHUS. Plasma C3 is low in fewer than 20% of patients, so a normal C3 does not exclude aHUS. (Grade 1B) [S1]
- ⚠️ **If ADAMTS13 is unavailable or pending, use the PLASMIC score** so that TTP can be treated early. A score of 6 or 7 means a high risk of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. [S1]
- **Kidney biopsy is not mandatory** when the clinical triad is present. It is recommended in special situations, such as kidney-graft dysfunction (TMA versus rejection), suspected underlying glomerulonephritis, or to gauge chronicity. (Grade 1B) [S1]
- **Genetic testing informs but does not decide the diagnosis:** a genetic basis is known for nearly two-thirds of cases, but a negative result does not rule out aHUS. [S1]
- **If a secondary TMA persists after the cause is treated, look for concurrent aHUS or TTP**, because this changes treatment and prognosis. [S1]
- **When a trigger is present, manage it first.** A personal or family history of TMA or aHUS, TMA on kidney biopsy, or low C3 with normal C4 (alternative-pathway activation) raises suspicion. If TMA settles, monitor and reassess on recurrence; if it persists despite proper treatment of the trigger, aHUS can be diagnosed once TTP (ADAMTS13 below 10%) and STEC-HUS are excluded. Modified French or PLASMIC scores that include proteinuria can predict severe ADAMTS13 deficiency while results are awaited. [S2]
- **Who to test genetically (S2):** everyone with severe or complicated TMA (for example, not responding to treatment of the trigger), everyone with confirmed aHUS, anyone being considered for a C5 inhibitor (results are not needed to start it), and anyone with suspected or past TMA/aHUS before a kidney transplant. Results take 1–3 months and up to half of cases have no causative variant, so the diagnosis stays clinical. Involve a renal geneticist and genetic counsellor where available. [S2]

## Treat

- **Supportive care follows AKI principles:** fluids and electrolytes, blood pressure, nephrotoxic drugs, dialysis and nutrition. Transfuse red cells for Hb <7 g/dL, and platelets only for active bleeding or surgery. Take a direct Coombs sample before any transfusion. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Every patient with aHUS is eligible for a C5 inhibitor as first-line treatment** (Grade 1A). Starting it in the acute phase improves kidney recovery. Before C5 inhibitors, 50% of patients needed chronic dialysis and up to 25% died in the acute phase. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Protect against meningococcus:** give MenACWY conjugate and MenB vaccines at least 15 days before starting, plus prophylactic antibiotics. If vaccination could not be done beforehand, continue antibiotic prophylaxis for as long as the C5 inhibitor is given. (Grade 1A) [S1]
- ⚠️ **Start the C5 inhibitor within 3–5 days, or sooner, once aHUS is suspected, and at once on a clinical diagnosis** (S2): kidney recovery is better the shorter the delay. Treat for 6 months initially, aiming for a stable blood and kidney response, then base continuation on the individual risk profile. [S2]
- ⚠️ **S2 vaccination schedule:** vaccinate at least 2 weeks before the first C5-inhibitor dose (MenACWY, plus MenB where available). If treatment starts less than 2 weeks after vaccination, cover with prophylactic antibiotics (usually penicillin) for at least 2 weeks after it; continuing prophylaxis may be advised. Consider boosters every 3–5 years during long-term treatment. [S2]
- **Ravulizumab** has a longer half-life, so maintenance is every 4 weeks (under 20 kg) or every 8 weeks (over 20 kg). When switching from eculizumab, give the ravulizumab loading dose 2 weeks after the last eculizumab dose. [S1]
- **If a C5 inhibitor is not immediately available** (for example in a child in the emergency department), start plasma therapy (plasma exchange or infusion). (Grade 1B) [S1]
- **Use the reference C5 inhibitors (eculizumab or ravulizumab) rather than eculizumab biosimilars**, because experience with the biosimilar is limited. [S1]
- **Proximal complement inhibitors are not yet proven in aHUS:** there are no pegcetacoplan studies, and the iptacopan phase II trial has no results yet. [S1]

## Monitor, space and stop

- **Monitor eculizumab blockade with CH50** (Grade 1A). CH50 is unproven for ravulizumab, so monitor clinically with serum drug levels (Grade 1A). [S1]
- **Eculizumab dose spacing:** do not review it before 3 months, and only once haematological, renal and systemic parameters have improved with no disease activity. Do not space without CH50 monitoring, never in kidney-transplant recipients, and not beyond 4 weeks. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Relapse after stopping is common:** about 31% relapsed in Italian and French cohorts, with CFH variants linked to earlier and more severe recurrence. [S1]
- **Stop only as a planned, shared decision**, when genetic testing, complement testing (e.g. CH50, C5b9) or drug levels are available, and only if the drug can be restarted immediately on relapse. (Grade 1C; shared decision Grade 1A) [S1]
- **Minimum course (S2): 6 months after the acute phase whatever the trigger, including at least 3 months after kidney function stabilises or recovers.** Weigh stopping after that against the relapse risk: complement-gene variants (CFH, C3, CFB, CFI, MCP), earlier TMA or family history of aHUS, previous relapse, severe extra-renal disease, CFH autoantibodies, age at onset and kidney transplant. [S2]
- ⚠️ **After stopping, watch for relapse for life:** blood pressure, symptoms, urine for blood and protein, and haemolysis tests (haemoglobin, platelets, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocytes), weekly to fortnightly at first, then every 3 months (higher risk) to 6 months. Patients can dipstick their urine at home and should report symptoms, especially around infection, vaccination, surgery and pregnancy. Restart the C5 inhibitor quickly on relapse. [S2]

## TMA with other triggers (Asia–Pacific algorithms)

- **Hypertension-associated TMA: control the blood pressure first.** Suspect aHUS if hypertension is refractory, or if TMA persists once pressure is controlled. In a hypertensive emergency that is hard to control, especially with severe organ damage, a C5 inhibitor can be given alongside stepped-up antihypertensives. [S2]
- **Autoimmune-associated TMA:** consider aHUS when TMA persists despite standard treatment of the autoimmune disease; the C5 inhibitor usually starts within a week of presentation. Examples: catastrophic APS not responding to plasma exchange and immunosuppression; SLE or lupus nephritis not responding after 72–120 hours, especially with brain or heart involvement; scleroderma failing an ACE inhibitor with progressive kidney failure. Continue at least until TMA has resolved. [S2]
- **Drug- and infection-triggered TMA:** stop the suspected drug (calcineurin or mTOR inhibitors, or cancer drugs such as carfilzomib, gemcitabine, mitomycin or bevacizumab) and consider aHUS and a C5 inhibitor only if TMA persists 1–2 weeks (or 3–5 half-lives) after withdrawal. With infection, treat it, rule out DIC in severe sepsis, and reserve a C5 inhibitor for TMA that persists or recurs after the infection has resolved. [S2]

## Pregnancy and transplantation

- **In pregnancy, aHUS usually appears late in the third trimester or postpartum.** A C5 inhibitor is the recommended treatment (Grade 1B): without it, 76% progressed to end-stage kidney disease despite plasma exchange. [S1]
- **Pregnancy (S2):** TTP and severe pre-eclampsia/HELLP are commoner in the second and third trimesters; TMA around or after delivery points to complement-mediated aHUS, though it can occur in any trimester. Consider aHUS if work-up is negative or TMA continues after delivery in HELLP or pre-eclampsia; the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio may help separate pre-eclampsia. Start a C5 inhibitor promptly on suspicion, ideally within 24 hours. Eculizumab is generally regarded as safe in pregnancy and is not found in breast milk. [S2]
- **Before a kidney transplant, stratify recurrence risk genetically** (Grade 1A) and plan prophylaxis before surgery. Recurrence in the graft runs at 50–80%. [S1]
- **Avoid living related donors** because of the donor's own risk after nephrectomy. If a related donor is considered, first confirm by genetic testing that they carry no complement-gene variant. (Grade 1B) [S1]
- **Kidney transplant with known aHUS (S2):** genetic profiling is mandatory. At moderate or high recurrence risk (for example CFH, CFI or CFB variants, or earlier graft loss from recurrence), give a C5 inhibitor right through to the operation day, or a first dose before reperfusion; lifelong treatment is generally advised for recurrence-causing variants, life-threatening disease or earlier graft loss. Monitor every recipient for TMA. S2 says live related donors are typically not considered, whatever the genetic result. [S2]
