# Snakebite in children

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** first aid, assessment for envenomation, antivenom, disposition and discharge for suspected snakebite in children in Victoria. The general (AMH) counterpart is `snakebite-antivenoms`, which names no antivenom and gives no dose.

## Scope and who to call

- ⚠️ **Do not use this guideline outside Victoria** — snake species differ by region. [S1]
- **Seek a clinical toxicologist's advice early** (Poisons Information Centre 13 11 26, 24 hours a day), especially for bites from snake-handlers' or other exotic snakes, or bites outside Victoria or Tasmania. [S1]
- **Victoria's 3 venomous snakes are the tiger, the brown and the red-bellied black. Brown and tiger antivenom cover all Victorian snakes.** [S1]

## Is the child envenomed?

- **Most people bitten by snakes in Australia are not significantly envenomed.** The bite site may show fang marks or scratches, but is **usually not painful, swollen or bruised** with Victorian snakes. [S1]
- **Envenomation is decided on circumstances, symptoms, examination and laboratory results together** — that decides the need for antivenom. [S1]
- **Ask about:** headache, sweating, abdominal pain, nausea or vomiting, diarrhoea, double or blurred vision, slurred speech, weakness, respiratory distress, bleeding, dark or red urine, local pain, muscle pain, painful draining lymph nodes, collapse or convulsions. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Examine for:** venom movement (swollen or tender draining nodes) · **neurotoxic paralysis** (ptosis, diplopia, ophthalmoplegia, dysarthria, limb or respiratory weakness) · **coagulopathy** (bleeding gums, oozing from venepuncture or wounds) · **muscle damage** (tenderness, weakness, dark or red urine). [S1]

## Investigations

- **Initial and serial bloods:** FBE and film, CK, EUC, and a coagulation screen (INR, APTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not use point-of-care coagulation devices** — they are inaccurate in snakebite envenomation. [S1]
- **A venom detection kit (VDK) is rarely indicated and is NOT used to diagnose envenomation.** Both Victorian antivenoms can be given without identifying the snake; **never rely on a witness's identification.** [S1]
- **If a VDK is used:** bite-site swab (urine if no bite is apparent, **never blood**); it takes 20–30 minutes, so **omit it in the unwell or arrested child**; results never override clinical and geographical data. [S1]

## First aid

- ⚠️ **Pressure immobilisation:** a broad, preferably elastic bandage, **as firm as for a sprained ankle**, from the bite site over the **entire limb**; splint the joints either side and **lay the child down.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not remove the bandage until the child is in a centre with full treatment facilities; if envenomed, not until antivenom has been given.** Remove it once antivenom is in. [S1]
- **Leave the bite site unwashed and uncleaned** in case a VDK is needed; wash it once a VDK is clearly not required or has been done. [S1]

## Antivenom

- ⚠️ **Antivenom for every child with evidence of envenomation**, in consultation with a clinical toxicologist: **one vial each of tiger and brown snake antivenom, given straight away.** [S1]
- **Each vial: dilute in 100 mL of 0.9% saline, infuse IV over 15–30 min.** ⚠️ **In cardiac arrest thought due to envenomation: undiluted, rapid IV push.** [S1]
- **No weight-based calculation** — one vial neutralises what one snake can deliver, and **extra vials do not speed recovery.** [S1]
- **Coagulopathy is slow to reverse: 10–20 hours to start improving, 24–30 hours to resolve.** More antivenom will not help; **FFP or cryoprecipitate** is controversial — discuss with a toxicologist, generally only if the child is bleeding. [S1]
- **Critical care environment with monitoring; 2 IV lines, at least one large bore.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Be prepared to treat anaphylaxis** to antivenom (per the anaphylaxis guideline, with a toxicologist). Because coagulopathy risks intracerebral haemorrhage and adrenaline can raise blood pressure, **a titratable IV adrenaline infusion may be considered** with an expert. [S1]
- **Check tetanus status is up to date.** [S1]

## Where to manage, admit, transfer

- **A regional centre can manage uncomplicated snakebite** only with a doctor available 24 hours, immediate critical care, immediate antivenom and a 24-hour laboratory. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Serial bloods and examinations for a minimum of 12 hours after the bite.** **Admit every child with evidence of envenomation.** [S1]
- **Discuss envenomed children with a toxicologist and consider tertiary transfer;** transfer complicated bites or where resources are lacking. ⚠️ **Significantly unwell with no antivenom on site: the retrieval team brings antivenom** to give before transfer. PIPER: 1300 137 650. [S1]

## Discharge

- ⚠️ **Discharge a child with suspected snakebite only in daylight hours** — subtle neurological signs may only show while the child is awake. [S1]
- **After antivenom, warn about serum sickness:** about 30% of children, **4–14 days later**, presenting with fever, rash, arthralgia, myalgia and flu-like symptoms. Write to the GP. [S1]
