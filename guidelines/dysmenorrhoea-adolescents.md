# Dysmenorrhoea in adolescents

## Recognise it

- **Primary dysmenorrhoea accounts for about 90% of cases** and **investigations are not routinely required.** [S1]
- **Primary pattern:** pain typically starts 6–12 months after menarche; cramping, intermittent lower abdominal pain starting before or with menses and lasting up to 72 hours. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Atypical features that point to secondary dysmenorrhoea:** pain present since menarche · pain getting worse as bleeding ends · pain after pelvic infection or features of PID · dyschezia or dysuria with menses · endometriosis in the family · persistent pain despite 3–6 months of adequate empiric treatment. [S1]
- **Endometriosis is the most common cause of secondary dysmenorrhoea**; others include obstructive reproductive tract anomalies, adenomyosis, infection including PID, and ovarian cysts. [S1]
- ⚠️ **First think of acute and serious causes of the abdominal pain.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **In an adolescent a vaginal examination is rarely indicated**; discuss with a senior clinician, and if needed perform it only once. [S1]

## Investigate selectively

- **Consider βhCG (with consent)**, particularly if sexually active, after unprotected intercourse or with a late/missed period; **consider an STI screen** if features suggest STI or PID. [S1]
- **Pelvic ultrasound, usually transabdominal,** for atypical features or no response to an adequate trial of hormonal therapy. [S1]

## Treat

- **Aim: quality of life** — minimise disruption to school and extra-curricular activities. **First line: NSAIDs and/or hormonal therapy.** [S1]
- **Continuous or extended hormonal contraception is recommended over cyclic use.** [S1]
- **NSAIDs:** naproxen BD, 5 mg/kg (max 500 mg), **or** mefenamic acid TDS, 500 mg, taken with food. **Begin 1–2 days ahead of menses and keep going for the first 2–3 days of the bleed.** [S1]
- **On heavy-bleeding days, tranexamic acid 1 gram TDS** — especially useful with concomitant heavy menstrual bleeding. [S1]
- **Non-hormonal medications can be used safely with hormonal ones.** Non-drug therapies can complement medicines, or replace them if medicines are unsuitable or unacceptable. [S1]
- **Combined oral contraceptive pill:** if pain is not adequately reduced, use it continuously (skip hormone-free tablets); check contraindications. Examples: levonorgestrel 150 microg/ethinylestradiol 30 microg (Levlen) daily, or Norimin (norethisterone 0.5 mg + ethinylestradiol 35 mcg) daily. [S1]
- **Oral progestogen if oestrogen is contraindicated** (no increased VTE risk): norethisterone (Primolut N) taken continuously, from a starting dose of 5 mg BD. [S1]
- **Etonogestrel implant (Implanon), 3-yearly:** may reduce pain and blood loss; 20% become amenorrhoeic. [S1]
- **Levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena or Kyleena), replaced every 5–8 years:** reduces blood loss and pain; safe post-menarche regardless of age; **in adolescents usually inserted under anaesthetic.** [S1]

## Follow-up and referral

- **Review 3 months after starting treatment**; sooner if side effects are unacceptable. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Refer to gynaecology** for suspected secondary dysmenorrhoea, atypical features, or pain not responding to first-line treatment — including **persistent pain despite at least 3–6 months of adequate empiric treatment.** [S1]
- **Discharge once management has started and a clear follow-up plan is made.** [S1]
