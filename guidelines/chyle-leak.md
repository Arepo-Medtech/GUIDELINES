# Chyle leak (postoperative chylothorax and chylous ascites) — nutritional management

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with a chyle leak after surgery (chylothorax, chylous ascites or cervical leak), especially after cancer surgery with lymph node dissection: recognition, nutritional screening, dietary fat restriction, medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) diets and supplements, enteral and parenteral nutrition, and monitoring of leak volume. For **cervical** leaks after neck dissection only, S2 adds drain management, local compression, octreotide and surgical thresholds. Lymphangiography, embolisation, pleurodesis and octreotide for chylothorax or chylous ascites are out of scope. Children and congenital chylothorax: out of scope. Head and neck surgery nutrition: see `head-and-neck-cancer-nutrition`.

Neither covers non-surgical or congenital chylothorax, or chylous ascites from cirrhosis or malignancy.

## Recognise

- CL is characterized by milky or white drainage fluid with triglyceride levels ≥ 110 mg/dL (1.2–1.4 mmol/L). [S1]
- ⚠️ Persistent leakage can lead to impaired wound healing, immunosuppression, malnutrition, extended hospitalization, and occasionally death; continuous CL significantly elevates mortality after surgery. [S1]
- Chyle leak follows intraoperative lymphatic damage, a significant complication after lymph node dissection in cancer patients. [S1]
- **After neck dissection for thyroid cancer, a chyle fistula occurs in roughly 1–3%.** A large leak can bring metabolic and electrolyte upset, low serum protein, bleeding, chylothorax or chylous ascites, breathing difficulty, and chest or abdominal infection. [S2]
- **Diagnosing a cervical leak (S2):** neck drain output rises and changes from serous to milky and cloudy, often more so after eating; a drain-fluid triglyceride above 100 mg/dL, or higher than the serum level, supports the diagnosis. [S2]

## Assess and monitor

- NRS 2002 and PG-SGA are recommended for nutritional assessment; assess weight change, triceps skinfold thickness, mid-arm circumference and grip strength. [S1]
- Monitor hepatorenal function, immunity and electrolytes during nutritional support, because chyle carries serum proteins, fat-soluble vitamins, minerals and immune cells. [S1]
- Many studies define a CL volume exceeding 1,000 mL within 24 h as high leak volume and < 1,000 mL as low leak volume; base dietary changes on chyle volume and check fluid balance daily. [S1]

## Diet first

- Dietary therapy, as the most conservative treatment for CL, achieves full recovery in 66–100% of cases and is the preferred approach after surgery. [S1]
- Form a multidisciplinary team (surgeons, nutritionists) to plan a personalised high-protein, low-fat diet, reviewed daily. [S1]
- For those with low postoperative CL volume, a high-protein, low-fat diet is recommended, emphasizing fats from MCTs, maintained for 7–10 days. [S1]
- ⚠️ Dietary fat restriction should be maintained for 2–4 weeks post-resolution of CL to prevent recurrence. [S1]
- **S2 (cervical leak) pairs fat restriction with fasting and parenteral nutrition** to lower chyle output and head off fluid and electrolyte imbalance. Plan nutrition individually with a registered dietitian, and keep checking nutritional status, liver and kidney function and electrolytes. [S2]

## Medium-chain triglycerides

- MCTs are absorbed directly into the portal vein, minimally affecting gut lymph production, which improves chyle colour and reduces drainage volume. [S1]
- ⚠️ MCT dose for adults: 50 to 100 mL daily, starting with 1 tablespoon 3–4 times daily and increasing gradually. Higher doses may cause steatorrhoea, mild GI discomfort and raised cholesterol in hyperlipidaemia. [S1]
- MCT can be taken orally as liquid or capsules, mixed into foods, sauces or cooking, or included in enteral feeds. [S1]
- If the patient cannot eat, give enteral feeding with MCT by nasogastric or gastrostomy tube until the leak resolves. [S1]

## Parenteral nutrition and micronutrients

- ⚠️ High leak volume: oral feeding can increase chyle, so start TPN promptly for complete bowel rest. TPN is also considered when a standard low-fat or MCT diet fails to reduce volume. [S1]
- When TPN is used, limit its duration to 6–30 days because of catheter complications and cost; stop fasting treatment when CL volume falls below 50 mL. [S1]
- Oral multivitamins are recommended on a fat-free or low-fat diet, with parenteral supplementation as necessary. [S1]
- ⚠️ If essential fatty acid deficiency risk is high, give a peripheral IV fat emulsion three times weekly at 250 mL of 20% solution per administration. [S1]

## Cervical leak after neck dissection: drain, compression, octreotide, surgery (S2)

- **Connect the neck drain to continuous suction** and nurse the patient in bed with the head up 30–40°. The leak usually lies near the internal jugular vein, so adjust the suction promptly to avoid vessel rupture, and keep the drain tip about 1–2 cm from the venous junction. [S2]
- **Drain removal:** the target is under 10 mL per 24 hours. If output stays above that despite suction, pull the drain back gradually, about 1–2 cm at a time, watching the output, and use ultrasound to look for fluid collecting as it comes out. [S2]
- **Local compression over the venous angle** in the supraclavicular fossa on the affected side (1–2 gauze blocks forming a loose 3–5 cm pad) can cut drainage quickly. Over-compression can injure the cervical or brachial plexus and cause tension blisters or flap necrosis. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Octreotide (S2; grade C evidence, grade C recommendation):** 100 µg by subcutaneous injection every 8 hours to start, increased to 200 µg if it does not work; stop it within 2 days of the leak meeting the treatment criteria. How somatostatin analogues reduce chyle is not known, and the best dose varies widely. [S2]
- ⚠️ **When to operate:** after failed non-surgical treatment, typically for drainage persistently above 1000 mL a day. Operate promptly for more than 2000 mL a day with a poor response, for a refractory low-flow leak (under 1000 mL a day), or for severe malnutrition, fluid and electrolyte disorders, infection, necrosis or bleeding. [S2]
- **A refractory high-output leak (peak 1000 mL or more) that has not responded after more than 2 weeks of treatment needs timely surgery.** [S2]
- **After endoscopic or robotic neck dissection, treat non-surgically first;** thoracoscopic thoracic duct ligation is the operation if that fails or the patient will not accept a neck incision. Ligation can raise lymphatic back-pressure and cause a chylothorax, so image the chest if breathlessness develops. [S2]
