# Androgen deficiency in men (male hypogonadism)

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adult men with suspected androgen deficiency: recognising pathological hypogonadism, confirming it biochemically, investigating the cause, when testosterone is and is not justified, and monitoring treatment. ## Recognise

- **Pathological hypogonadism comes from disease of the hypothalamus or pituitary (hypogonadotropic) or of the testes (hypergonadotropic).** It is diagnosed clinically, rests on a pathological cause, and is confirmed with hormone tests. [S1]
- **Primary testicular failure, with raised LH and FSH, is the most common form;** failure of hypothalamic–pituitary drive, before or after puberty, is less common. [S1]
- **Take a full history:** smoking, medicines, alcohol and other drugs (above all androgens), undescended testes or genital anomalies, puberty, past fertility, erections, libido, and pelvic surgery, genital trauma or infection. **Examine** height, weight (waist if obese), gynaecomastia, virilisation and testicular volume with an orchidometer. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Klinefelter syndrome, about one in 580 males, is missed in more than half of affected men,** often because nobody examines the genitals; the small testes give it away. [S1]

## Confirm biochemically

- **Test only when the clinical picture suggests pathological androgen deficiency, never as population screening.** Measure total testosterone with LH and FSH early in the morning (08:00 to 10:00); shift workers or the sleep-deprived can be tested soon after waking. [S1]
- **Mass spectrometry assays are preferred where available;** immunoassays are non-specific and method-biased. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Always repeat a low result:** up to 30% of men with one low testosterone are normal on retesting. Unless the first set is clearly diagnostic, base the diagnosis on at least two measurements of testosterone, LH and FSH. [S1]
- **Interpret against SHBG:** it rises with hyperthyroidism, liver disease and antiepileptic drugs, and falls with obesity, insulin resistance and exogenous androgens. A low testosterone with low SHBG and normal LH and FSH does not prove deficiency. **Calculated free testosterone should not guide decisions.** [S1]

## Find the cause

- **Suspected hypogonadotropic hypogonadism needs further tests** for treatable causes: prolactin, iron studies and full blood count, anterior pituitary function, and pituitary MRI when pituitary or hypothalamic disease is suspected. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not start testosterone before the work-up is finished and a definite pathological cause is found:** it can hide a pituitary tumour, suppresses the axis so later assessment is hard, commits the man to lifelong treatment, and harms fertility, whereas gonadotropins can restore sperm production in secondary hypogonadism. [S1]

## Who should (and should not) get testosterone

- **Men with pathological hypogonadism should receive testosterone replacement at any age.** Age alone neither justifies nor rules out treatment; in very old men with comorbidities weigh benefit against risk, and start slowly and carefully. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Without hypothalamic, pituitary or testicular disease, testosterone is not justified in older men with comorbidities.** Their low testosterone marks poor general health: manage the underlying illness or depression first, and seek endocrinology review if the diagnosis is uncertain. [S1]
- **In obesity, low testosterone with normal LH and FSH mostly reflects reversible axis suppression.** Encourage weight loss by diet and exercise (10% weight loss raises testosterone measurably); if testosterone stays low after successful weight loss, reassess for a missed pathological cause. [S1]
- **In metabolic syndrome or diabetes, testosterone gave only a marginal change in insulin sensitivity and no better glucose control;** prioritise lifestyle change, weight loss and glycaemic control. [S1]
- **Glucocorticoids and opioids lower testosterone, possibly reversibly.** Withdraw them where possible; if they must continue, an endocrinologist can review the balance of options. [S1]
- **S2 position (differs from S1):** confirm hypogonadism with consistent symptoms and a low testosterone (EAU and EAA use 12.0 nmol/L as the cut-off), and separate organic (primary or secondary) from functional hypogonadism. In functional hypogonadism, weight management and physical activity come first and must precede testosterone; S2 then treats testosterone as an option, whereas S1 does not justify it without a pathological cause. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Do not start testosterone** when paternity is wanted, with unexplained prostate or breast changes, or with a high haematocrit; avoid it with untreated sleep apnoea, severe heart failure, a recent cardiovascular event or hormone-sensitive cancer unless a specialist is guiding care. (S2) [S2]

## Treat and monitor

- **Replace with testosterone itself.** Depot intramuscular injection and transdermal gel, cream or liquid are convenient and cost-effective; aim to relieve symptoms and signs at standard doses, keeping levels within the eugonadal reference interval. [S1]
- **Judge efficacy by the man's leading symptom and by trough levels** (sampled just before the next dose) for injectable or transdermal products, aiming for the lower part of the eugonadal range. Random samples mean nothing, and oral testosterone cannot be monitored this way. Persistently raised LH in primary testicular failure suggests under-dosing or poor adherence; periodic bone density helps too. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Check haemoglobin or haematocrit 3 months after starting, then yearly;** a rise above normal is more likely in smokers. If polycythaemia develops, look for smoking, sleep apnoea or respiratory failure and lower the dose or frequency; venesection is rarely needed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Prostate:** there is no convincing excess risk in treated hypogonadal men, so monitor as for eugonadal men of the same age. But if substantive prostate disease is reasonably possible, do a digital rectal exam and PSA before starting. Routine PSA monitoring can turn into screening, with overdiagnosis. [S1]
- **Cardiovascular evidence is conflicting:** until better data exist, be cautious with testosterone in older men who have established heart or vascular disease, above all those without pathological hypogonadism. [S1]
- **Injections are relatively contraindicated with bleeding disorders or anticoagulants** (haematoma risk), so prefer a transdermal product; all injections can cause pain, bruising and a cough from oil microembolism. **Apply transdermal products after the morning shower and cover them** to avoid transfer to children or partners. [S1]
- **Obstructive sleep apnoea is not an absolute bar:** testosterone worsens it only briefly, unless it is severe and untreated. [S1]
- **When to sample testosterone (S2):** on gel, about 2–4 h after the last application; on long-acting testosterone undecanoate, preferably 1 week before the next injection (delay the injection if the level is still high-normal); on short-acting injections, just before the next dose. Titrate gels by daily dose and injections by the interval. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Haematocrit (S2 schedule): every 3–6 months in the first year, then yearly, keeping it below 54%.** If it rises too high, lower the dose, change formulation or pause treatment; phlebotomy may be needed. S1's schedule is at 3 months, then yearly. [S2]
- 🇦🇺 **Australian T4DM trial:** 22% of men on testosterone undecanoate injections had a haematocrit above 0.52 on fasting samples taken in a hot climate, but only 2.5% on repeat non-fasting samples. [S2]
- **PSA usually rises on testosterone:** a rise of up to 1.4 ng/mL a year is generally considered safe; more than that needs urological evaluation. Monitor lower urinary tract symptoms. (S2) [S2]

## Cardiovascular safety after TRAVERSE (S2)

- **TRAVERSE (5246 men aged 45–80 with functional hypogonadism, most with cardiovascular disease or high risk) found no significant increase in major adverse cardiovascular events** on 1.62% testosterone gel titrated to 350–750 ng/dL for a mean of 22 months. Heart failure and cardiovascular death did not differ. [S2]
- ⚠️ **But atrial fibrillation and non-fatal arrhythmias rose slightly but significantly,** and the trial's limits matter: an older, high-risk group (mean age 63.3, BMI 35, about 70% with T2DM), follow-up of only about 22 months, and too few events to settle arrhythmia and heart failure. [S2]
- **After TRAVERSE, the US FDA is removing the boxed-warning language on cardiovascular risk from testosterone labels,** while keeping the limitation-of-use wording for functional hypogonadism. S2 concludes that testosterone is cardiovascularly safe in clinically confirmed hypogonadism with careful selection and monitoring. [S2]
