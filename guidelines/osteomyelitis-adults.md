# Pyogenic osteomyelitis in adults: diagnosis, empirical cover, oral therapy and duration

**Edition:** 2.0 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-checked; awaiting clinical attestation

## Diagnosis (clinical review)

- **Do not order plain x-rays or CT routinely for suspected osteomyelitis;** they are neither sensitive nor specific enough. They still help when fracture, tumour or a foreign body is the leading concern, or when pretest probability is low (15% or less). [S1]
- **MRI and certain tagged white cell scans are the most accurate imaging.** Inflammatory markers are not accurate enough to diagnose osteomyelitis. [S1]
- **Take blood cultures if the patient is systemically unwell or at risk of bacteraemia** (e.g. injecting drug use): a likely pathogen such as S. aureus can target therapy and may make bone biopsy unnecessary. A positive bone biopsy is highly accurate, but a negative one does not exclude infection. [S1]
- **Diabetic foot:** probe-to-bone is a simple bedside test with reasonable accuracy and may avoid imaging. Use percutaneous bone biopsy for cultures where available; surface swabs are inaccurate and not recommended. [S1]
- **Osteomyelitis under a pressure ulcer:** avoid routine antibiotics unless deep bone biopsy confirms infection and surgical closure is planned, or there is sepsis or local soft-tissue infection. All patients need nutrition, debridement, off-loading and psychosocial care. [S1]

## Empirical therapy (clinical review)

- **In a clinically stable patient without a known pathogen, it is reasonable to delay antibiotics until bone or joint samples are taken.** Do not delay when there is sepsis or threatened spinal cord compression. [S1]
- **If antibiotics cannot wait for cultures, cover aerobic gram-positive cocci (especially S. aureus) and gram-negative bacilli,** guided by local susceptibility, patient risk factors and previous cultures, then narrow to culture results. [S1]
- **MRSA cover depends on local prevalence and risk:** known colonisation is the biggest single risk factor. With low local MRSA, no colonisation or prior positive cultures and little healthcare contact, it can be withheld. [S1]
- **Antipseudomonal cover is not routine.** Add it for chronic wounds with multiple prior antibiotic courses, previous Pseudomonas, gangrene, surgery within about 3 months (e.g. early PJI), or sites linked to Pseudomonas such as malignant otitis externa. [S1]
- **Adjunctive rifampicin: evidence is mixed.** Use it on culture results (mainly gram-positive cocci) weighing adverse effects and interactions (e.g. oral anticoagulants, opioids); 450–600 mg per dose is likely better than 300 mg given several times a day. [S1]

## Oral therapy (clear recommendation)

- ✅ **Oral antibiotics are a reasonable option for any type of osteomyelitis** (haematogenous, contiguous including vertebral and diabetic foot, or prosthetic), based on 8 RCTs against IV therapy, if the patient: is clinically stable; has adequate source control with no persistent bacteraemia; can absorb from a working gut; has a published oral regimen covering the likely pathogens; and has no psychosocial barrier to safe oral treatment. [S1]
- **No minimum IV lead-in is needed:** switch to oral as soon as all the criteria are met, even during empirical therapy. [S1]

## Duration (clear recommendation, then clinical review)

- ✅ **Without a retained implant, give no more than 6 weeks of antibiotics** for haematogenous or contiguous osteomyelitis (including diabetic foot), provided source control is adequate (no undrained abscess too big for antibiotics alone, possibly 2–3 cm or more). [S1]
- **After debridement, 3 or 4 weeks may be enough** (small RCTs; confirmation needed). After confident total resection of infected bone, antibiotics can be omitted, or limited to 2 to 5 days if there is no soft-tissue infection. [S1]
- **Prosthetic joint infection managed with debridement, antibiotics and implant retention (DAIR): 12 weeks is preferred over 6** (DATIPO trial). After prosthesis exchange, some experts prefer 12 weeks, while others see equipoise, especially when the pathogen is not S. aureus. [S1]
- **Other infected implants (e.g. fracture fixation):** one approach is to treat until the bone has healed enough for the hardware to come out. Chronic oral suppression is an option when curative surgery is too risky, though its risks and benefits are poorly defined. [S1]

## Diabetic foot osteomyelitis (Australian guideline)

- **Initial tests for suspected diabetic foot osteomyelitis: combine the probe-to-bone test, ESR (or CRP and/or procalcitonin) and plain x-rays** (strong; moderate). If the x-ray, clinical and laboratory findings are most compatible with osteomyelitis, no further foot imaging is needed to make the diagnosis (strong; low). [S2]
- **If the diagnosis remains in doubt, consider MRI, 18F-FDG-PET/CT or leukocyte scintigraphy (with or without CT)** (strong; moderate). [S2]
- **Take a bone sample (percutaneous or surgical) for culture and, if possible, histopathology when a definitive diagnosis or the causative pathogen is needed to choose treatment** (strong; low). The panel notes that expertise in percutaneous bone biopsy is low in many Australian locations. [S2]
- **Uncomplicated forefoot osteomyelitis with no other reason for surgery: consider antibiotics without surgical bone resection** (strong; moderate). 'Uncomplicated' means no severe (grade 4) infection, necrotising tissue infection, bone exposed in the base of the ulcer, kidney injury or peripheral artery disease. [S2]
- ⚠️ **Probable diabetic foot osteomyelitis with soft-tissue infection: urgently evaluate the need for surgery,** with intensive post-operative medical and surgical follow-up (strong; moderate). [S2]
- **Choose antibiotics with demonstrated efficacy for osteomyelitis** (strong; low); the drugs used for non-diabetic osteomyelitis are reasonable, including beta-lactams, beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitors, (fluoro)quinolones, glycopeptides and lipoglycopeptides, oxazolidinones, sulfonamides, lincosamides, rifamycins and fusidic acid. [S2]
- **If parenteral therapy was needed at first, consider switching to a highly bioavailable oral antibiotic after perhaps 5 to 7 days,** when the pathogens are susceptible to an available oral drug and nothing precludes oral therapy (weak; moderate). [S2]
- ⚠️ **Duration: the Australian panel did NOT adopt the international 'no longer than 6 weeks' recommendation for diabetic foot osteomyelitis,** judging the population too heterogeneous for it to be broadly applied. Its advice to reconsider bone culture, resection or a different regimen if there is no clinical improvement within 2 to 4 weeks was kept as a general principle. [S2]
- **After all infected bone has been surgically removed and there is no soft-tissue infection, give antibiotics for just a few days** (weak; low), defined by the panel as 2 to 5 days, which allows time for the histopathology and culture of the residual bone margin. [S2]
- **At resection, consider sending bone from the stump for culture (and histopathology)** (weak; moderate). If that aseptic specimen grows pathogens or the histology shows osteomyelitis, give appropriate antibiotics for up to 6 weeks (strong; moderate). From remote sites, results take longer, so continue antibiotics until they are available. [S2]

## Monitoring response (clinical review)

- **Do not follow ESR, CRP or repeat imaging routinely;** neither predicts long-term cure for the individual patient. Repeat imaging when a patient is failing therapy, to guide source control, find complications such as pathological fracture, or rethink the diagnosis. [S1]
