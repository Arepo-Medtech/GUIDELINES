# Acute hepatic porphyria: diagnosis, acute attacks and long-term management

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-24 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and adolescents with acute intermittent porphyria, variegate porphyria, hereditary coproporphyria or ALAD porphyria: who to test, how to confirm, treating an acute attack, preventing recurrent attacks (haem prophylaxis, givosiran, liver transplant) and long-term surveillance. The cutaneous porphyrias are out of scope. HCC surveillance method: see `hepatocellular-carcinoma-surveillance`; CKD care: `chronic-kidney-disease`.

## Who to test

- **Consider testing women aged 15–50 with unexplained, recurrent severe abdominal pain** when an initial work-up has found no cause. [S1]
- **Attacks cause neurovisceral symptoms:** severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation, muscle weakness, neuropathy, tachycardia and hypertension. [S1]
- **About 90% of symptomatic patients are women.** Most have only one or a few attacks in a lifetime; 3%–5% have recurrent attacks, usually defined as 4 or more a year. [S1]
- **The four types (Table 1):** AIP, HCP and VP are autosomal dominant, present at 15–50 years and are commoner in women; HCP and VP can also affect the skin. ALAD porphyria is autosomal recessive, presents from childhood to adulthood and is commoner in men. [S1]

## Confirm the diagnosis

- ⚠️ **First test: a random (spot) urine for ALA, PBG, porphyrins and creatinine.** In an attack, ALA and PBG are at least 5 times the upper limit of normal, so a 24-hour collection is not needed. [S1]
- **Normalise the spot urine ALA and PBG to creatinine; above 5 times the upper limit of normal is diagnostic. In ALAD porphyria only ALA is raised.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not screen with urine porphyrins alone:** mild rises (secondary porphyrinuria) are often misread as porphyria and lead to overdiagnosis. [S1]
- **Test during or soon after an attack.** Between attacks, 15%–44% of people with sporadic AIP have normal ALA and PBG, and levels can fall quickly in HCP and VP. In someone with frequent symptoms, normal ALA and PBG likely rule AHP out. [S1]
- **Confirm the type with genetic testing** (ALAD, HMBS, CPOX, PPOX) once biochemistry is positive, then offer testing to first-degree relatives. Genetics is not a first-line screen, because most carriers never have an attack. [S1]

## Treat an acute attack

- ⚠️ **Attacks severe enough for admission: intravenous haem (hemin), 3–4 mg/kg once daily, typically for 4 days**, preferably into a high-flow central vein (PICC or port) because of thrombophlebitis. [S1]
- **Collect the spot urine before the first haem dose.** In a patient with known AHP, haem can be started on clinical grounds without waiting for results. [S1]
- **Supportive care:** stop precipitating drugs; treat pain and nausea aggressively; intravenous carbohydrate (about 300 g/day in adults) early in the attack; watch electrolytes and correct hyponatraemia slowly. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Seizures:** avoid barbiturates, hydantoins (phenytoin), carbamazepine and valproate. Magnesium sulfate, benzodiazepines and levetiracetam appear safe. [S1]
- **Advise every patient to avoid triggers:** alcohol, tobacco, fasting, stress, acute illness and cytochrome P450-inducing drugs. Progesterone is a known trigger. Check drug safety in the online porphyria drug databases. [S1]

## Prevent recurrent attacks

- ⚠️ **With recurrent attacks (4 or more a year), consider outpatient prophylactic haem or givosiran.** [S1]
- **Prophylactic haem is off-label and less proven for prevention.** Long-term use brings central-line infections and iron overload, so screen for iron overload. [S1]
- **Givosiran** (monthly subcutaneous siRNA against ALAS1) lowered attack rates in a phase 3 trial. Use it only when recurrent attacks are confirmed both biochemically and genetically, and not in pregnancy or when pregnancy is planned. [S1]
- **Monitor on givosiran:** liver enzymes, urea and creatinine, homocysteine, amylase and lipase. Check liver enzymes monthly for the first 3–6 months (ALT rises above 3 times normal occur), then reduce to twice a year if normal. [S1]
- **Givosiran laboratory panel (from the testing table):** comprehensive metabolic panel, plasma homocysteine, urinalysis, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, B12 and folate, amylase and lipase. Do it before the first dose and before each of the first 3 monthly injections; if stable and tolerated, every 3 months for the next year, then at least every 6 months. [S1]
- **Menstrual-cycle attacks** can be treated with hormonal suppression such as a GnRH agonist, with limited success. [S1]
- **Liver transplant only for intractable, drug-refractory disease** with badly reduced quality of life, ideally in an experienced centre. It has been curative in AIP. Screen related living donors genetically. [S1]

## Long-term surveillance

- **Liver:** check liver enzymes at least yearly in treated patients. Abnormal results should prompt a search for other causes. [S1]
- **Annual monitoring list (from the testing table):** liver enzymes, creatinine and eGFR, and, from age 50, liver ultrasound with α-fetoprotein every 6 months. [S1]
- **Iron on prophylactic haem (at least monthly):** check ferritin and iron every 3–6 months; consider venesection if ferritin is above 1000 ng/mL. [S1]
- ⚠️ **HCC surveillance from age 50 for everyone with AHP, whatever the symptoms:** liver ultrasound every 6 months. HCC can occur without cirrhosis. [S1]
- **Kidneys:** check creatinine and eGFR at least yearly, more often on givosiran (which may worsen eGFR in some), and control blood pressure. Kidney transplant is the treatment of choice for kidney failure due to AHP. [S1]
- **Counsel patients about long-term complications** (neuropathy, CKD, hypertension, HCC) and the need for lifelong monitoring. Asymptomatic high excretors may also need yearly monitoring. [S1]
